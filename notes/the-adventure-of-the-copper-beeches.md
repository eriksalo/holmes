# The Adventure of the Copper Beeches

## What happens
Holmes, complaining that his practice has sunk to "giving advice to young ladies from boarding-schools," receives Violet Hunter, a governess without family or savings who has been offered £120 a year by a jovial fat man named Jephro Rucastle on condition that she cut off her hair, wear a particular blue dress, and sit where she is told. Holmes says he would not let a sister take the place; she takes it. A fortnight later she wires from Winchester. At the Copper Beeches she has been made to sit with her back to a window laughing at Rucastle's stories while a man watches from the road; she has found a coil of hair identical to her own locked in a drawer; a mastiff is loosed at night; a wing is barred, and when she entered it Rucastle threatened to throw her to the dog. Holmes deduces she is impersonating Rucastle's imprisoned daughter Alice, whose suitor the Rucastles wish to drive off. That evening, with the Rucastles out and Toller drunk, Miss Hunter locks Mrs. Toller in the cellar; Holmes and Watson break into the barred room and find it empty. Rucastle returns, runs for the dog, and is mauled; Watson shoots it. Mrs. Toller explains that Alice had money by will, refused to sign it over to her father, fell ill, and was imprisoned; her sailor fiancé bribed the Tollers and carried her off by ladder. Alice marries him; Rucastle survives, broken; Holmes loses interest in Miss Hunter.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **Pay that is too good is itself a warning, and need makes people disregard it.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "Well, yes, of course the pay is good—too good. That is what makes me uneasy. Why should they give you £120 a year, when they could have their pick for £40? There must be some strong reason behind." Miss Hunter: "But the money, Mr. Holmes, the money!" And earlier: "when I got back to my lodgings and found little enough in the cupboard, and two or three bills upon the table, I began to ask myself whether I had not done a very foolish thing."
   - What the action does with it: She reasons herself into it exactly as Jabez Wilson did in The Red-Headed League and Hatherley did in The Engineer's Thumb: "what use was my hair to me?" The story confirms Holmes's unease and also confirms her competence; she goes in with eyes open, carries a mirror in her handkerchief, and ends up the rescuer rather than the victim. Need made her accept; character made her useful.

2. **The countryside's beauty hides cruelty that a city would never allow.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "It is my belief, Watson, founded upon my experience, that the lowest and vilest alleys in London do not present a more dreadful record of sin than does the smiling and beautiful countryside... The pressure of public opinion can do in the town what the law cannot accomplish... It is the five miles of country which makes the danger."
   - What the action does with it: A daughter imprisoned in a barred room under a skylight, a starving mastiff loosed nightly, a child who tortures small creatures: all of it behind a damp whitewashed house with copper beeches at the door and Lord Southerton's woods on three sides. No neighbour knows. The story confirms Holmes's claim completely, and his speech is framed against Watson's "dear old homesteads" so that the reader is made to feel the gap between what the landscape says and what it contains.

3. **A child's cruelty is the clearest evidence of the parent's.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "I have frequently gained my first real insight into the character of parents by studying their children. This child's disposition is abnormally cruel, merely for cruelty's sake, and whether he derives this from his smiling father, as I should suspect, or from his mother, it bodes evil for the poor girl who is in their power."
   - What the action does with it: Rucastle's first description of his son was "Oh, if you could see him killing cockroaches with a slipper! Smack! smack! smack!" delivered laughing. Miss Hunter calls the boy a creature whose "one idea of amusement" is "giving pain to any creature weaker than himself." Rucastle, when crossed, drops the jollity in an instant: "I'll throw you to the mastiff." The story confirms the inference: the smiling man is the cruel child grown up, and the "most serious point in the case," as Holmes says, was never the hair or the dress.

4. **Good humour can be a mask so complete that it convinces even the one who sees through it.**
   - Where it is spoken: Miss Hunter: "He was such a comfortable-looking man that it was quite a pleasure to look at him." And: "I laughed until I was quite weary." Later: "But his voice was just a little too coaxing. He overdid it. I was keenly on my guard against him." And: "here in an instant the smile hardened into a grin of rage, and he glared down at me with the face of a demon."
   - What the action does with it: Rucastle's comedy is literally a performance for an audience of one man in the road; Miss Hunter laughs genuinely while serving as a prop. Mrs. Rucastle, who knows what the laughter is for, never smiles. The story shows that charm can be instrumental to its core and still work on the person who suspects it, and that the one person immune is the one who shares the secret.

5. **A woman's money by will is the hidden motive of domestic imprisonment.**
   - Where it is spoken: Mrs. Toller: "Miss Alice had rights of her own by will, but she was so quiet and patient, she was, that she never said a word about them but just left everything in Mr. Rucastle's hands. He knew he was safe with her; but when there was a chance of a husband coming forward, who would ask for all that the law would give him, then her father thought it time to put a stop on it. He wanted her to sign a paper, so that whether she married or not, he could use her money."
   - What the action does with it: This is the same engine as A Case of Identity and The Speckled Band: a daughter's income enjoyed by the household so long as she stays unmarried, and a father who acts when a suitor appears. Alice's patience and silence are what made her exploitable; her refusal to sign is what got her locked up. The story confirms that the whole grotesque theatre (hair, dress, laughter, dog) exists to protect a revenue, and that the daughter's virtue of quietness was the condition of her imprisonment.

6. **Fidelity and bribery, not detection, are what free the prisoner.**
   - Where it is spoken: Mrs. Toller: "that didn't make no change in her young man, and he stuck to her as true as man could be." Holmes: "Mr. Fowler being a persevering man, as a good seaman should be, blockaded the house, and having met you succeeded by certain arguments, metallic or otherwise, in convincing you that your interests were the same as his." Mrs. Toller: "Mr. Fowler was a very kind-spoken, free-handed gentleman."
   - What the action does with it: Holmes's deduction is correct in every particular and arrives at an empty room. The rescue was done before he got there, by a sailor with a ladder and money for the servants. Holmes's own closing remark is that "our _locus standi_ now is rather a questionable one." The story confirms his reasoning and makes it beside the point: the people who saved Alice were the ones who loved her and the ones who could be paid.

7. **Interest in a person can be entirely a function of the problem she represents.**
   - Where it is spoken: Watson: "As to Miss Violet Hunter, my friend Holmes, rather to my disappointment, manifested no further interest in her when once she had ceased to be the centre of one of his problems." Holmes, earlier: "I should not ask it of you if I did not think you a quite exceptional woman." And: "no sister of his should ever have accepted such a situation."
   - What the action does with it: Holmes's praise of Miss Hunter is the warmest he gives anyone in this collection, and it ends with the case. Watson's disappointment is the story's own. The action shows a man whose attention is real and complete while the problem lasts, and gone when it is solved, and puts this beside his own opening complaint that Watson has "degraded what should have been a course of lectures into a series of tales." The story is the tale; the person is what the lecture leaves out.

8. **Those who know too much about a man's past cannot be dismissed, and so they stay.**
   - Where it is spoken: Watson's close: "They still live with their old servants, who probably know so much of Rucastle's past life that he finds it difficult to part from them."
   - What the action does with it: The Tollers took Fowler's money, got drunk on his drink, and set up the ladder, and they are still employed at the end. Rucastle is "a broken man, kept alive solely through the care of his devoted wife," surrounded by the people who betrayed him and whom he cannot sack. The story's last glimpse of the house is of a household bound together by mutual knowledge of guilt, which is a kind of imprisonment of its own.

## What it teaches
This is the story in which the detective's methods are vindicated and his presence is nearly irrelevant. Holmes deduces the whole plot from a coil of hair, a blue dress, and a laugh, and arrives to find the bird flown and the villain being eaten by his own dog. Everything that actually freed Alice Rucastle was done by a faithful sailor, a bribable servant, and a governess brave enough to lock a woman in a cellar. The reader is asked to admire the reasoning and to notice that the world was already moving without it.

What stays with you is Holmes's speech on the train about the countryside, because the story proves it and then goes further: the cruelty was not hidden by distance alone but by jollity. Rucastle's stories were genuinely funny. The governess genuinely laughed. The whole apparatus of imprisonment was staffed by laughter and good pay, and the only person who never smiled was the wife who knew. The story leaves you with the unpleasant knowledge that comfort and charm are not evidence of anything, and with the quieter sadness of Watson's last sentence, in which the exceptional woman who made the rescue possible is dropped the moment she stops being interesting.

## Preoccupations
- money as the motive beneath domestic tyranny
- the countryside's beauty hiding cruelty
- charm and good humour as a mask
- pay too good to be honest, accepted from need
- the child as evidence of the parent
- the detective's interest ending with the problem
