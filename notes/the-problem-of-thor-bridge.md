# The Problem of Thor Bridge

## What happens
Neil Gibson, an American "Gold King" of ruthless reputation, hires Holmes to clear his children's governess, Grace Dunbar, of murdering his wife Maria, who was found shot at the mouth of Thor Bridge clutching a note from Dunbar fixing a meeting there; a discharged revolver was found in Dunbar's wardrobe. Gibson's own manager arrives first to call him "an infernal villain" who tormented his wife. Holmes forces Gibson to admit he loved the governess and propositioned her; she refused, stayed only to turn his power toward good. At the bridge Holmes finds a fresh chip on the underside of the parapet, fifteen feet from the body. Visiting Dunbar in her cell, he hears that the wife summoned her, screamed hatred at her, and was left alive. The clutched note, the pistol planted in the one place a murderer would never leave it, and the chip tell Holmes the truth: Maria Gibson shot herself with a revolver tied by string to a stone hung over the parapet, so that the weapon would be whisked into the mere, and framed her rival. Holmes proves it by re-enacting it with Watson's revolver, which leaves a matching chip.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **Unreturned love can curdle into a hatred strong enough to die for.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "I do not think that in our adventures we have ever come across a stranger example of what perverted love can bring about... Her first resolution was to end her own life. Her second was to do it in such a way as to involve her victim in a fate which was worse far than any sudden death could be."
   - What the action does with it: The story confirms it with a plan of "remarkable subtlety": the extracted note, the planted gun, the string and stone. Maria Gibson's love for her husband never faded ("Do what I might, she was as devoted as ever"), and its unchanging intensity is exactly what turned it into a weapon against the woman he preferred. The dead woman is the most intelligent person in the story, and her intelligence is entirely in the service of her wound.

2. **Cruelty can be a man's attempt to be released from love he no longer feels.**
   - Where it is spoken: Gibson: "If I have been harsh to her, even brutal as some have said, it has been because I knew that if I could kill her love, or if it turned to hate, it would be easier for both of us. But nothing changed her."
   - What the action does with it: Gibson offers this as explanation, not excuse, and the story neither accepts nor rejects it outright. Bates reports "words of cold, cutting contempt, even before the servants"; Dunbar "frequently reproached him" for his harshness. His brutality did not free him; it gave his wife someone to blame instead of him, and she died hating the governess, not the husband. The method he describes failed in the exact way he intended it to succeed.

3. **There is a jealousy of the mind as fierce as any jealousy of the body.**
   - Where it is spoken: Gibson: "There is a soul-jealousy that can be as frantic as any body-jealousy... she was aware that this English girl exerted an influence upon my mind and my acts that she herself never had. It was an influence for good, but that did not mend the matter." Dunbar: "she loved so vividly in a physical sense that she could hardly understand the mental, and even spiritual, tie which held her husband to me."
   - What the action does with it: The relations between Gibson and Dunbar were, by every account the story credits, chaste. This changes nothing. Maria's hatred and her plot are as total as if she had found them in bed. Holmes's summary makes the point directly: "Whether Miss Dunbar was her rival in a physical or in a merely mental sense seems to have been equally unforgivable in her eyes."

4. **Great wealth assumes everything is for sale, and is surprised to find a thing that is not.**
   - Where it is spoken: Gibson: "money is nothing to me in this case. You can burn it if it's any use in lighting you to the truth... Name your figure!" Holmes: "My professional charges are upon a fixed scale... I do not vary them, save when I remit them altogether." And later: "Some of you rich men have to be taught that all the world cannot be bribed into condoning your offences."
   - What the action does with it: Gibson tries money, then fame ("You'll be the talk of two continents"), then threat ("I have broken stronger men than you"), and gets nowhere; he has to come back and tell the truth. The story also shows the one thing he could not buy before this: Dunbar, who "would have none of it." Yet the ending leaves him with his wealth intact and the woman he wanted apparently within reach. The rich man is lectured, not deprived.

5. **A good person may stay in a compromised position because she believes her influence does more good than her absence.**
   - Where it is spoken: Gibson: "She believed and said that a fortune for one man that was more than he needed should not be built on ten thousand ruined men who were left without the means of life... she believed she was serving the world by influencing my actions. So she stayed." Dunbar: "I can see now that I was wrong. Nothing could justify me in remaining where I was a cause of unhappiness."
   - What the action does with it: The story grants that her influence was real and good, and it also shows the consequence: a wife driven to self-murder, a governess in a cell. Dunbar's own judgement at the end is against herself. The story gives you her high motive and its terrible result in the same breath and does not resolve them.

6. **Overdoing a deception is what reveals it; the planted clue is too perfect.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "We must look for consistency. Where there is a want of it we must suspect deception." And: "In her anxiety that it should be discovered she somewhat overdid it, by holding it in her hand to the last. This alone should have excited my suspicions earlier than it did."
   - What the action does with it: The note clutched so tightly that "we could hardly open the fingers" and the pistol dropped "in the very first place that would be searched" are both read by two juries as damning and by Holmes as impossible for a competent murderer. The story confirms the method: the frame is undone by the framer's thoroughness. "When once your point of view is changed, the very thing which was so damning becomes a clue to the truth."

7. **Everyone judges a powerful man by his public face, and the public face is both true and misleading.**
   - Where it is spoken: Bates: "Those public charities are a screen to cover his private iniquities... Don't take him at his face value. There is more behind." Watson: "An Abraham Lincoln keyed to base uses instead of high ones would give some idea of the man."
   - What the action does with it: Bates is right about the brutality and wrong about the murder; the sergeant suspects Gibson too ("these Americans are readier with pistols than our folk are"). The story lets the reader share these suspicions for most of its length, then clears him of the crime without clearing him of the character. Watson's own ability to "admire him" for his self-command sits beside his judgement of "hunger and rapacity." No single face is the whole man.

8. **Suffering is a school, but not everyone enrolled in it learns.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes, closing: "the financial world may find that Mr. Neil Gibson has learned something in that schoolroom of Sorrow where our earthly lessons are taught."
   - What the action does with it: This is Holmes's hope, stated as a hope ("may find"). Gibson has already said "I thank God that my plans did not work out as I intended" and "I guess she was right." But the story gives no evidence of changed conduct, only changed words; and it anticipates that Gibson and Dunbar will "join their forces," which is to say that the man gets what he wanted after all. The lesson is proposed, not shown.

## What it teaches
Thor Bridge is about three people who each loved in a way that could not be met. Maria Gibson loved a man who had stopped loving her and could not stop. Neil Gibson wanted a woman he had no right to and tried to buy her. Grace Dunbar loved, if that is the word, the good she could do through a bad man's power, and stayed in a house where her presence was poison. None of them is simply guilty; one of them is dead by her own hand, and the plot she died to execute very nearly worked. The story's deepest observation is that intelligence does not cool passion; it arms it. Maria's scheme is admired by Holmes as "deep and subtle," and that admiration is the story's way of taking her seriously as a mind, not just as a "creature of the Tropics."

What it leaves you with is the asymmetry of the ending. The dead woman is explained. The governess is freed. The Gold King, who by his own account made his wife's life a misery to drive her away, ends the story a widower with a clear path to the woman he wanted, and Holmes's only sanction is a sentence of hope that he has learned something. The text knows this is unequal: it puts the word "Sorrow" in capitals and leaves the lesson in the conditional. A thoughtful reader may feel that justice here went exactly as far as the evidence and not one step into the question of who made Maria Gibson into what she became.

## Lines worth keeping
- "A problem without a solution may interest the student, but can hardly fail to annoy the casual reader."
- "I can discover facts, Watson, but I cannot change them."
- "Love, fortune, power, all depending upon one middle-aged life. Ugly, Watson--very ugly!"
- "My professional charges are upon a fixed scale... I do not vary them, save when I remit them altogether."
- "No man ever crossed me and was the better for it." "So many have said so, and yet here I am."
- "I guess most men have a little private reserve of their own in some corner of their souls where they don't welcome intruders."
- "if I could kill her love, or if it turned to hate, it would be easier for both of us. But nothing changed her."
- "I guess all my life I've been a man that reached out his hand for what he wanted."
- "There is a soul-jealousy that can be as frantic as any body-jealousy."
- "women lead an inward life and may do things beyond the judgment of a man."
- "Where a crime is coolly premeditated, then the means of covering it are coolly premeditated also."
- "that schoolroom of Sorrow where our earthly lessons are taught."

## Preoccupations
- love that will not die turning into hatred
- jealousy of the mind as well as the body
- the rich man who assumes everything can be bought
- the good person who stays in a bad position for a good reason
- the frame-up undone by its own thoroughness
- suffering as a possible but unproven teacher
