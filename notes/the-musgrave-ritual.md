# The Musgrave Ritual

## What happens
Pressed by Watson to tidy his papers, Holmes instead pulls out relics of an early case and tells it. Reginald Musgrave, an aristocratic college acquaintance, had come to him in Montague Street: his brilliant butler Brunton, twenty years in service, had been caught at two in the morning studying a family document called the Musgrave Ritual, a catechism every Musgrave recites at his coming of age and which nobody thinks has any meaning. Dismissed with a week's grace after begging that disgrace would kill him, Brunton vanished three days later, leaving his boots and money; Rachel Howells, the housemaid he had jilted, went into hysterics, then disappeared herself, her footprints ending at the lake, from which the drags brought up a bag of rusted metal and dull stones. Holmes reads the Ritual as a set of directions — sun over the oak, shadow under the elm, so many paces, "and so under" — measures the shadow of a tree that no longer exists, and is led to a cellar in the old wing, where under a flagstone Brunton crouches dead beside an emptied chest, suffocated when the stone came down. Holmes reconstructs the night: Brunton recruited the woman he had wronged to help lift the stone; she either let it fall or struck the prop away; she threw the treasure in the mere and fled. The dull stones are the ancient crown of the Stuarts, hidden by a Cavalier Musgrave for a king who never reclaimed it, and recited for centuries by a family that did not know what it was saying. Of Rachel Howells nothing was ever heard.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **People can keep and repeat a thing for generations without ever asking what it means, and the outsider who asks will understand it better than the heirs.**
   - Where it is spoken: Musgrave: "It is rather an absurd business, this ritual of ours... But it has at least the saving grace of antiquity to excuse it." And: "of no practical use whatever." Holmes: "your butler appears to me to have been a very clever man, and to have had a clearer insight than ten generations of his masters."
   - What the action does with it: The crown of England lay for two hundred years under a passage the family walked across daily, and the directions to it were spoken aloud by each heir at his majority. The person who finally read the words as words was the servant who was not supposed to be reading them. The ending adds a quiet sting: the Musgraves had "some legal bother and a considerable sum to pay before they were allowed to retain it." They did not even own what they had been guarding.

2. **Intelligence confined to a station below it turns inward and becomes dangerous to its possessor.**
   - Where it is spoken: Musgrave: "With his personal advantages and his extraordinary gifts... it is wonderful that he should have been satisfied so long in such a position." And: "this very intelligence has caused his ruin, for it seems to have led to an insatiable curiosity about things which did not in the least concern him." Brunton: "I've always been proud above my station in life."
   - What the action does with it: A man who speaks several languages and plays every instrument has spent twenty years handing round plates, and the only door out of the house he can find is a hole in the cellar floor. The story does not argue that Brunton was wronged — Musgrave calls his conduct "most infamous" — but it does show that the quality which made him "quite invaluable" is the same one that puts him under the stone. Holmes, reconstructing the crime, treats Brunton's mind as the equal of his own: "it was unnecessary to make any allowance for the personal equation."

3. **A man's claim that disgrace would kill him is both a lie and true.**
   - Where it is spoken: Brunton: "I can't bear disgrace, sir... disgrace would kill me. My blood will be on your head, sir—it will, indeed—if you drive me to despair."
   - What the action does with it: The plea is manipulative — he wants a fortnight to finish the theft — and Musgrave sees through it enough to cut the time to a week. But Brunton is dead within three days, and in a sense it is pride that kills him: he will not leave empty-handed, so he goes down into the hole. The story lets a bargaining speech become an accurate prophecy, and leaves the reader to notice that Musgrave's "lenient" week was exactly enough rope.

4. **Men who have wronged a woman cannot believe she has stopped loving them.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "A man always finds it hard to realize that he may have finally lost a woman's love, however badly he may have treated her. He would try by a few attentions to make his peace with the girl Howells, and then would engage her as his accomplice."
   - What the action does with it: Brunton, who could decode a seventeenth-century cipher, could not read the woman he had jilted into brain fever. The story's whole catastrophe depends on his miscalculation of her, and Holmes states the general rule as something he can rely on when reconstructing events. But note what the story does not do: it never decides whether Rachel killed him. "Was it a chance that the wood had slipped...? Had she only been guilty of silence...? Or had some sudden blow from her hand dashed the support away?" Her act is left in three alternatives, and she is never found.

5. **The reasoner's method is to become the other man, and it works exactly as far as the other man resembles him.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "I put myself in the man's place and, having first gauged his intelligence, I try to imagine how I should myself have proceeded under the same circumstances."
   - What the action does with it: This gets Holmes to the billets of wood and the shape of the night. It gets him nowhere with Rachel Howells, whom he can only describe from outside as a "passionate Celtic woman's soul" with "smouldering fire of vengeance," and whose action he must offer as three guesses. The story shows the method's reach and its wall in the same scene: Holmes can think like a clever thief and cannot think like a jilted housemaid. His vivid picture of her flight — "with her ears ringing perhaps with the muffled screams from behind her" — is labelled by him as "surmise," and the reader should keep that label on.

6. **The keeper of papers he cannot bear to destroy tells a story about a family that kept a paper it could not read.**
   - Where it is spoken: Watson: "He had a horror of destroying documents, especially those which were connected with his past cases." Holmes, holding up the relics: "So much so that they _are_ history."
   - What the action does with it: The frame and the tale rhyme. Holmes's rooms are stacked with bundles "on no account to be burned," and he has kept a peg, a string, and three rusty coins for years. The Musgraves kept a formula for three centuries. The story does not say whether this hoarding is wisdom or compulsion; it shows both in the same man and the same house, and lets Watson's exasperation at the mess stand beside the fact that the mess contained the crown of England.

7. **What the ritual asks for is paid, by the wrong people.**
   - Where it is spoken: "'What shall we give for it?' 'All that is ours.' 'Why should we give it?' 'For the sake of the trust.'"
   - What the action does with it: The words were written as a Cavalier's pledge to a king. In the event, Brunton gives his life for it, Rachel gives whatever she had left, and the Musgraves — who never gave anything — are made to pay a sum to keep it. The crown itself is "bent and twisted out of its original shape." The story takes a formula of loyalty and shows it fulfilled as a bill, presented to people who never signed it.

8. **Ambition waits for its chance and knows itself.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "the very chance for which I had been panting during all those months of inaction seemed to have come within my reach. In my inmost heart I believed that I could succeed where others failed, and now I had the opportunity to test myself."
   - What the action does with it: This is one of the few places where Holmes admits plain hunger. Set against Brunton, who was "satisfied so long in such a position," and Musgrave, whose pride "was really an attempt to cover extreme natural diffidence," the story offers three kinds of men in relation to their gifts: one who waits for a stage, one who waits too long and then grabs, and one who has inherited everything and understands none of it. Only the first ends well, and he is telling the story.

## What it teaches
The lesson that stays is about inheritance: you can receive a thing of enormous value and recite its instructions perfectly, and still have no idea what you are holding, because understanding is not transmitted with the words. The Musgraves were not stupid; they were incurious in the way that comfortable people are, and the story's cruelty is that the man who was curious enough to understand their heirloom was the one who could never be allowed to have it. Brunton is a thief and a seducer, and the story does not soften that, but it also makes him the only character who takes the Ritual seriously, and he is rewarded with the Ritual's own literal answer: "All that is ours."

What is left unresolved is Rachel Howells, and the story knows it. Holmes offers three versions of what she did and chooses none; he imagines her flight with a novelist's vividness and then says that nothing was ever heard of her. The reader is handed a reconstruction labelled "surmise" and a woman who has escaped both justice and explanation. If you take Holmes's rule about men who cannot believe they have lost a woman's love, you also have to take his admission that he cannot say what that woman did when she had the man in her power. The method that reads shadows off a vanished tree stops at the edge of another person's grief.

## Preoccupations
- inheritance received without understanding
- intelligence trapped below its station
- the servant who sees what the masters cannot
- men misjudging the women they have wronged
- the reasoner's method and its limit at another's passion
- the past preserved in papers and relics
- the woman who escapes both justice and explanation
