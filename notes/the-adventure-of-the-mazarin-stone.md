# The Adventure of the Mazarin Stone

## What happens
Told in the third person rather than by Watson, this is a single evening in the Baker Street sitting-room. Holmes, starving himself on a case, has been tracking Count Negretto Sylvius, a big-game shooter who stole the Crown's yellow Mazarin diamond, and has set up a wax dummy of himself in the window to draw the Count's air-gun. The Count comes in person to murder him, finds the dummy, and is caught mid-swing. Holmes lays out the evidence against him and his boxer accomplice Sam Merton, offers to "compound a felony" (freedom for the stone), then retires to his bedroom to play the violin while they confer. The violin is a gramophone; Holmes has slipped into the dummy's chair behind the curtain, hears where the stone is, and snatches it from the Count's hand. The police take the pair. Holmes then plants the diamond in the pocket of Lord Cantlemere, the peer who never believed in him, and lets him "discover" it after suggesting his arrest.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **A man who thinks himself all brain treats his body, and other people, as expendable appendages.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "I am a brain, Watson. The rest of me is a mere appendix. Therefore, it is the brain I must consider." And on Billy: "How far am I justified in allowing him to be in danger?"
   - What the action does with it: The story shows the brain-man surrounded by people worried for his flesh: Billy ("I'm frightened for his health"), Mrs. Hudson, Watson's "honest face... twitching with anxiety." Holmes dismisses his own danger ("Does it matter very much?") but not Billy's, and he sends Watson away to keep him safe ("You would be horribly in the way... in my way"). The claim is partly refuted by his own conduct: he does weigh the lives around him, and the story ends with him ordering "dinner for two." The appendix gets fed.

2. **The hunter and the detective are the same animal; the difference is what they count as a pest.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "You used to shoot lions in Algeria... And, no doubt, to free the country from a pest?" "Exactly!" "My reasons in a nutshell!"
   - What the action does with it: The Count's whole method of coming to Baker Street is a stalk; Holmes's whole method of catching him is a decoy. Holmes admits he is "Not far from" the devil the Count calls him. The story confirms the kinship and lets Holmes enjoy it; it does not pretend his pleasure in the chase is purer than the Count's ("the sport--the excitement--the danger!").

3. **Pride in one's own cleverness is what delivers the clever into a trap.**
   - Where it is spoken: The Count: "I've fooled better men than he." Also: "I'll see this sucker and fill him up with a bogus confession."
   - What the action does with it: The Count is caught by the exact confidence he boasts of: having spotted "the old sporting man" and "the elderly woman," he thinks himself the better observer ("Other people can observe as well as you"), then fails to notice the dummy's chair has changed occupants. Sam Merton, the "stupid" one, is the one who worries about "too many curtains" and notices the sound at the window. Stupidity is more cautious than cleverness here, and cleverness loses.

4. **Honour among thieves is a fiction; the strong partner despises the weak one even as he uses him.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "Not a bad fellow, Sam, but the Count has used him." Merton: "What--d'ye think I'm going to snatch it off you? See here, mister, I'm getting a bit tired of your ways."
   - What the action does with it: The Count will not let Sam even hold the stone, and it is this small contempt, forcing Sam to the window for a look, that puts the diamond in reach of Holmes's hand. The alliance breaks on exactly the mistrust Holmes had diagnosed. Sam's "faithful but rather fatuous" loyalty is the only honest thing between them.

5. **The purpose of the law and the purpose of the client are not the same; the expert serves the commission, not justice.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "We can make the world a better place by laying them by the heels. But that is not what I am out for. It's the stone I want." And: "I'll compound a felony. We don't want you or Sam. We want the stone."
   - What the action does with it: Holmes openly offers to let a murderer-thief go free in exchange for property. The offer turns out to be a feint and the pair are arrested anyway, so the story has it both ways: the bargain is sincere enough to be believed, and never has to be paid. The reader is left unsure whether Holmes would have kept it.

6. **Those who disbelieve in you want you to fail more than they want the job done.**
   - Where it is spoken: Billy on Lord Cantlemere: "he don't believe in Mr. Holmes and he was against employing him. He'd rather he failed." Cantlemere: "Every man finds his limitations, Mr. Holmes, but at least it cures us of the weakness of self-satisfaction."
   - What the action does with it: Cantlemere comes to gloat and gets the stone put in his pocket. The story confirms Billy's diagnosis (Cantlemere's sneer on hearing it is "difficult--very difficult") and then punishes it with a joke rather than a rebuke. Cantlemere's grudging retraction ("I withdraw any reflection I have made") is the nearest thing to a conversion in the tale, and it comes only from being made ridiculous.

7. **Showmanship is not separable from skill; the dramatic instinct is part of the method.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "I have an impish habit of practical joking. Also that I can never resist a dramatic situation." And, of Baron Dowson: "in my case what the law had gained the stage had lost."
   - What the action does with it: Every device in the story is theatre: the disguises, the waxwork, the gramophone violin, the planted stone. The story lets theatre win a real diamond back, so the "perverted" sense of humour Cantlemere complains of is the thing that produced the result. But it also shows the risk: Billy "in danger of your life" for a stage effect.

## What it teaches
The story is a comedy, and its comedy rests on an uncomfortable idea: that Holmes and his quarry are built the same way. Both are hunters who enjoy the stalk, both are proud of seeing more than others, both treat the people around them as instruments, Sam as a "fighting bully," Watson and Billy as pieces to move. Holmes wins because he is better at it and because he can wait in a chair while a gramophone plays. He does not win because he is better morally; he offers to trade a murderer's liberty for a stone. The lesson, if it is one, is that excellence and virtue are different things, and that the story is content to admire excellence.

What is unresolved is the cost. The chapter opens on a page-boy afraid for his master's health and a detective who will not eat because he is "a brain." It closes with a practical joke on an old peer and an order for dinner. Nothing in between tells you whether the brain-man has learned anything about the body he lives in or the people who feed it. The dummy in the window is the clearest image the story offers of Holmes: lifelike, admired, hollow, and useful precisely because it can be shot at.

## Lines worth keeping
- "I am a brain, Watson. The rest of me is a mere appendix."
- "Because the faculties become refined when you starve them."
- "Yes, Watson, I could. That's what worries him so."
- "Life is full of whimsical happenings, Watson." "It might have been tragedy."
- "We all have neglected opportunities to deplore."
- "You are absolute plate-glass. I see to the very back of your mind."
- "When the other fellow has all the trumps, it saves time to throw down your hand."
- "You won't die in your bed, Holmes." "I have often had the same idea. Does it matter very much?"
- "Why not give ourselves up to the unrestrained enjoyment of the present?"
- "Every man finds his limitations, Mr. Holmes, but at least it cures us of the weakness of self-satisfaction."
- "I believe you are the devil himself." "Not far from him, at any rate."
- "I can never resist a dramatic situation."

## Preoccupations
- the hunter and the hunted as mirror images
- mind set against body
- theatre and disguise as instruments of truth
- bargaining with criminals; the client's interest versus justice
- contempt between accomplices
- the pleasure of confounding one's doubters
