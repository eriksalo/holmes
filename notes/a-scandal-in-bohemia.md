# A Scandal in Bohemia

## What happens
The hereditary King of Bohemia, arriving masked and under a false name, hires Holmes to recover a compromising photograph from Irene Adler, a retired opera singer who has refused to sell it and has survived five attempts at theft and waylaying. Holmes, disguised as a groom, watches her house and is accidentally pressed into service as witness at her hurried wedding to the lawyer Godfrey Norton. That evening he stages a street brawl, a fake injury, and a false fire alarm so that Adler, in fear, will betray the photograph's hiding place; she does. But she has recognised him, follows him home in male dress, wishes him good night in the street, and leaves England by the morning train. In the hiding place she has left a letter and a photograph of herself alone. The King declares the matter a success; Holmes refuses his ring and asks instead for the photograph.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **Seeing is not the same as noticing, and most people only see.**
   - Where it is spoken: "You see, but you do not observe. The distinction is clear." Holmes tests Watson with the seventeen steps he has climbed "some hundreds of times" and cannot count.
   - What the action does with it: The story confirms the maxim and then turns it on its author. Holmes observes Watson's boot, the Bohemian watermark, the German syntax. But Adler observes Holmes just as well: "You really did it very well. You took me in completely. Until after the alarm of fire, I had not a suspicion. But then, when I found how I had betrayed myself, I began to think." The slim youth who says "Good-night, Mister Sherlock Holmes" is seen by Holmes but not observed; he wonders "who the deuce that could have been." The observer is, for once, the one who merely saw.

2. **Fear strips away pretence and shows what a person values most.**
   - Where it is spoken: "When a woman thinks that her house is on fire, her instinct is at once to rush to the thing which she values most. It is a perfectly overpowering impulse... A married woman grabs at her baby; an unmarried one reaches for her jewel-box."
   - What the action does with it: The trick works exactly as predicted; Adler goes straight to the panel. But the story quietly reverses the logic of the generalisation. Adler, now married, reaches not for a baby or a jewel but for the instrument of her own safety. And Holmes, at the end, "reaches" for her photograph rather than an emerald ring. What each person grabs when frightened or offered a reward is particular, not a function of sex or marital status, and the thing Holmes values turns out to be the woman who beat him.

3. **Rank is not the same as worth, and the two can run in opposite directions.**
   - Where it is spoken: The King: "I wish she had been of my own station! What a queen she would have made!" and again, "Is it not a pity that she was not on my level?" Holmes: "From what I have seen of the lady, she seems, indeed, to be on a very different level to your Majesty."
   - What the action does with it: The King has hired burglars twice, had Adler's luggage diverted and had her waylaid twice; he calls her an "adventuress" and himself "mad—insane." Adler keeps her word, protects herself only, and takes nothing. The action gives the moral high ground entirely to the woman the King is trying to silence. Holmes signals his verdict by turning away "without observing the hand which the King had stretched out to him."

4. **The feeling the detached man disclaims is the one that catches him.**
   - Where it is spoken: Watson's opening frame: "All emotions, and that one particularly, were abhorrent to his cold, precise but admirably balanced mind... Grit in a sensitive instrument, or a crack in one of his own high-power lenses, would not be more disturbing than a strong emotion in a nature such as his."
   - What the action does with it: Watson insists this is not love, and the story never says it is. But Holmes keeps the photograph, asks for it in place of a king's ring, and stops making jokes about women's cleverness: "He used to make merry over the cleverness of women, but I have not heard him do it of late." The narrator's confident claim that Holmes is a "reasoning and observing machine" is complicated by the one thing the machine chooses to keep.

5. **The person who deceives for a good cause still feels the shame of it.**
   - Where it is spoken: Watson at the window: "I never felt more heartily ashamed of myself in my life than when I saw the beautiful creature against whom I was conspiring... After all, I thought, we are not injuring her. We are but preventing her from injuring another."
   - What the action does with it: Watson's justification rests on the King's account of Adler as a blackmailer bent on ruin. Her letter shows she never intended to use the photograph except as a shield: "I keep it only to safeguard myself." So Watson's rationalisation was built on a false premise, and his shame was the better guide than his reasoning. The story lets the feeling stand uncorrected and the argument collapse.

6. **A promise from a person of character is worth more than a possession.**
   - Where it is spoken: The King, on reading the letter: "I know that her word is inviolate. The photograph is now as safe as if it were in the fire."
   - What the action does with it: The whole case has been premised on the idea that only physical recovery can make the King safe; he offered "one of the provinces of my kingdom" for the object. In the end he gets no object at all and declares himself fully satisfied by her word. The action confirms the observation, with the irony that the King, who has lied about his name and worn a mask, trusts a woman's honour more than he has demonstrated his own.

7. **Disguise is a skill, not a gender, and it belongs to whoever has trained for it.**
   - Where it is spoken: Watson on Holmes: "His expression, his manner, his very soul seemed to vary with every fresh part that he assumed." Adler: "I have been trained as an actress myself. Male costume is nothing new to me. I often take advantage of the freedom which it gives."
   - What the action does with it: Holmes's two disguises (groom, clergyman) both succeed at their immediate goals. Adler's one disguise succeeds at hers. The symmetry is exact and the story draws it deliberately. Holmes's parting generalisation, "Women are naturally secretive, and they like to do their own secreting," is true of Adler, but the story shows that she is also naturally good at the things Holmes thinks of as his own.

## What it teaches
This is a story about a man being beaten, and what the text makes you sit with is how little the loss costs him and how much it gives him. The King asked for a photograph; he leaves with a letter, a promise and the peace he wanted. Holmes asked for a fee; he leaves with a portrait he did not know he wanted. The only person who gets precisely what she came for is Adler, and she gets it by leaving. Everything the case was supposed to prove about method and observation turns out to be true, and still insufficient, because the person on the other side had the same method and used it with more restraint.

What is hard here is that Watson's narration cannot quite hold the story it tells. He opens by insisting that Holmes feels nothing, and closes by recording a change in Holmes's habits that he does not explain. He justifies his own part in the deception with an argument the ending dismantles, and he never goes back to revise it. The reader is left to notice what Watson has seen but not observed: that the cold instrument has a crack in it, and that the crack is the most human thing in the story.

## Lines worth keeping
- "You see, but you do not observe. The distinction is clear."
- "It is a capital mistake to theorise before one has data. Insensibly one begins to twist facts to suit theories, instead of theories to suit facts."
- "She has the face of the most beautiful of women, and the mind of the most resolute of men."
- "When a woman thinks that her house is on fire, her instinct is at once to rush to the thing which she values most."
- "I never felt more heartily ashamed of myself in my life than when I saw the beautiful creature against whom I was conspiring."
- "After all, I thought, we are not injuring her. We are but preventing her from injuring another."
- "I love and am loved by a better man than he. The King may do what he will without hindrance from one whom he has cruelly wronged."
- "I wish she had been of my own station! What a queen she would have made!"
- "From what I have seen of the lady, she seems, indeed, to be on a very different level to your Majesty."
- "I know that her word is inviolate. The photograph is now as safe as if it were in the fire."
- "He used to make merry over the cleverness of women, but I have not heard him do it of late."

## Preoccupations
- the gap between seeing and noticing
- rank versus worth
- the detached mind and the feeling it denies
- deception used for a cause, and the shame it carries
- a woman's competence against men's assumptions about women
- what a person reaches for when frightened
