# The Adventure of the Veiled Lodger

## What happens
A Brixton landlady, Mrs. Merrilow, comes to Holmes about her lodger of seven years, a woman who never lifts her veil, pays in cash, and now cries "Murder!" and "You cruel beast!" in the night. The lodger has asked for Holmes by name and sent two words to bring him: "Abbas Parva," the village where, seven years earlier, the circus-owner Ronder was found with his skull crushed beside his lion's open cage and his wife with her face torn off, a death ruled misadventure. Holmes had been troubled by the case at the time: the lion's behaviour made no sense, and a man's screams were heard. The veiled woman, Eugenia Ronder, tells the truth: her husband was a drunken sadist who whipped her; she and the strongman Leonardo loved each other and killed Ronder with a club fitted with five nails to mimic a lion's paw; when she opened the cage to complete the deception, the lion, scenting blood, attacked her, and Leonardo fled. She lied to the police to protect him. He has just drowned; she wanted one person to know before she dies. Holmes tells her to keep her hands off her own life. She lifts her veil: "I wonder if you would bear it." Two days later a bottle of prussic acid arrives by post: "I send you my temptation. I will follow your advice."

## What the work observes about being human

1. **A person can commit murder and be the chief victim of the story.**
   - Where it is spoken: Eugenia: "From that day I was in hell, and he the devil who tormented me... He tied me down and lashed me with his riding-whip when I complained. They all pitied me and they all loathed him, but what could they do?" And: "My husband was not fit to live. We planned that he should die."
   - What the action does with it: The story never disputes her guilt and never asks for her punishment. Holmes says "Poor girl! Poor girl!" and pats her hand "with such a show of sympathy as I had seldom known him to exhibit." The lion did what the law would not have done, and the law did nothing after. Watson's own phrase before he hears a word is "by some retribution of Fate," so even the narrator reads her disfigurement as sentence served. The story confirms that justice and law have come apart here, and it lets them stay apart.

2. **Love for a beautiful body does not survive the body; the lover who flees is the measure of what the love was.**
   - Where it is spoken: Eugenia: "I know now the poor spirit that was hidden in that splendid body, but compared to my husband he seemed like the Angel Gabriel." And: "He might as soon have loved one of the freaks whom we carried round the country as the thing which the lion had left. But a woman's love is not so easily set aside."
   - What the action does with it: Leonardo "could have saved me... But the man lost his nerve... I saw him turn and fly." He never writes. The photograph she keeps shows "the self-satisfied smile of the man of many conquests." The story confirms her bitter judgement of him and then shows her still shielding him for seven years. Her love outlasts his cowardice, her face, and his death, which is the hardest thing in the tale.

3. **Keeping the beast for a living makes one a beast kept.**
   - Where it is spoken: Watson: "From keeping beasts in a cage, the woman seemed, by some retribution of Fate, to have become herself a beast in a cage." Eugenia: "A poor wounded beast that has crawled into its hole to die--that is the end of Eugenia Ronder."
   - What the action does with it: Both the narrator and the woman reach for the same image independently. Her chosen life (a room "close, musty, ill-ventilated," a landlady who "only take[s] the one," a face seen once in seven years) is a cage she pays for. The story gives this as fact, not metaphor. What complicates it is that she was caged before the lion: tied down and whipped in a van, with "no one in the show who did not know."

4. **Everyone who loathes a tyrant waits for someone else to act.**
   - Where it is spoken: Eugenia: "They all pitied me and they all loathed him, but what could they do? They feared him, one and all." And of her husband: "I think that he was a coward as well as a bully, and that Leonardo was the one man that he was afraid of."
   - What the action does with it: A whole camp of circus men watched a woman beaten for years and did nothing until the night she screamed under a lion, when they drove it off "with poles." The story shows courage arriving only when the danger was an animal and not a man. Ronder's own cowardice beneath his bullying is confirmed by his flight: "the claw-marks were on the back of his head."

5. **A confession is sometimes made not for forgiveness but so that one person understands.**
   - Where it is spoken: Eugenia: "it would ease my mind if someone knew the truth before I died... I wanted to find one man of judgment to whom I could tell my terrible story, so that when I am gone all might be understood." And: "For God's sake, not the police!... the clergy can't change what is past."
   - What the action does with it: She chooses Holmes because she has read Watson's stories and "know[s] your character and methods too well," and she is right: he warns her he may go to the police and then does not. The story confirms that the need to be understood is distinct from the need to be absolved, and that Holmes, not a priest or a magistrate, is the one who can meet it. "The case is closed," he says; "Yes," she says, "the case is closed," and the difference in their meanings is where the story turns.

6. **Telling a sufferer that suffering is instructive is a hard thing to say to a face.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "Your life is not your own. Keep your hands off it... The example of patient suffering is in itself the most precious of all lessons to an impatient world." Eugenia, lifting her veil: "I wonder if you would bear it."
   - What the action does with it: This is the sharpest exchange in the twelve stories. Holmes offers a maxim; she answers with her face, and he can only raise "his hand in a gesture of pity and protest" and leave. The story does not let his words stand unchallenged. And then it does something stranger: she sends him the poison and says she will follow his advice. The maxim worked, or something did. Whether it was the maxim or the fact that one person finally knew is left to the reader. Holmes calls her "the brave woman," and keeps the bottle on his mantelpiece "with some pride."

7. **Familiarity with danger breeds the contempt that kills.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "It was deposed at the inquest that there had been some signs that the lion was dangerous, but, as usual, familiarity begat contempt, and no notice was taken of the fact." And: "they believed that so long as they were the food-carriers he would regard them as benefactors, and would never molest them."
   - What the action does with it: The plan to blame the lion depended on the lion staying tame; the lion, "quick... to scent human blood," did not. The story confirms the maxim in the most literal way, and extends it: Eugenia was also familiar with a dangerous husband for years and "in an evil moment" married him. The thing you feed and live beside is still what it is.

## What it teaches
This is the barest story in the collection: Holmes deduces almost nothing, the mystery is solved by the person who committed it, and the detective's whole role is to listen. What it teaches is about the difference between the law and the truth. Eugenia Ronder's confession would have sent a man to the gallows; it would have done nothing for her and nothing for the dead. She waits until the man is drowned and then tells it anyway, not for absolution but so that "all might be understood." Holmes, who threatens to refer it to the police, does not, and Watson, who begins the story boasting of his friend's "discretion and high sense of professional honour," reports this without a flicker. The story believes some truths belong to a listener rather than a court.

The harder lesson is in the last two pages. Holmes tells a mutilated woman that her patient suffering is a lesson to the world, and she shows him what the lesson costs. He has no answer. Then she takes his advice. A reader may find this either a triumph of a word spoken at the right time or a woman doing what a famous man told her because he was the one person who had heard her. The text gives you both and chooses neither. Holmes's remark about compensation hereafter ("If there is not some compensation hereafter, then the world is a cruel jest") is as close as these stories come to admitting that the cases they solve do not add up to justice.

## Lines worth keeping
- "The problem has always been, not to find, but to choose."
- "the most terrible human tragedies were often involved in these cases which brought him the fewest personal opportunities."
- "'For God's sake, not the police!' says she, 'and the clergy can't change what is past.'"
- "as usual, familiarity begat contempt, and no notice was taken of the fact."
- "From keeping beasts in a cage, the woman seemed, by some retribution of Fate, to have become herself a beast in a cage."
- "I know now the poor spirit that was hidden in that splendid body, but compared to my husband he seemed like the Angel Gabriel."
- "They all pitied me and they all loathed him, but what could they do?"
- "not because he had torn away my beauty, but because he had not torn away my life."
- "A poor wounded beast that has crawled into its hole to die."
- "If there is not some compensation hereafter, then the world is a cruel jest."
- "Your life is not your own. Keep your hands off it."
- "I wonder if you would bear it."

## Preoccupations
- justice outside the law, and the law left out
- the murderer who is the victim
- love that outlasts the lover's cowardice
- the cage one chooses after the cage one escaped
- confession as the need to be understood, not forgiven
- what the healthy may say to the suffering
