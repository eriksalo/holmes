# The Adventure of Black Peter

## What happens
Captain Peter Carey, a retired whaler known as Black Peter, is found in the locked wooden "cabin" where he slept apart from his family, pinned to the wall by one of his own harpoons. He was a drunkard who flogged his wife and daughter through the park at night; no one in the village has "one single word of sorrow" for him. Young Inspector Stanley Hopkins, Holmes's protégé, reports "absolute failure" and brings a bloodstained notebook of share listings initialled J.H.N. Holmes, who has already spent a morning stabbing a dead pig to prove a harpoon cannot be driven through a body without a practised arm, goes down to Sussex. That night they catch a frail youth breaking into the cabin to read the ship's log: John Hopley Neligan, son of a banker who vanished with his creditors' securities in 1883, trying to clear his father's name. The notebook is his; Hopkins arrests him and tells Holmes his presence was "unnecessary." Holmes, who has been posing as "Captain Basil" and advertising for harpooners, invites Hopkins to breakfast and handcuffs the third applicant, Patrick Cairns, a harpooner from Carey's old ship. Cairns tells it plainly: in 1883 the Sea Unicorn picked up a drifting landsman with a tin box; Carey threw him overboard and kept the box; Cairns came years later to be paid for silence, Carey drew a knife drunk, and Cairns killed him first and took the box, which held only papers he could not sell. Holmes orders Neligan released and the box returned, then leaves for Norway.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **A man can build a private world to be alone with what he did, and it will come for him there.** Carey's cabin is a ship's room on land where no one else may set foot.
   - *Where it is spoken:* Hopkins: "He kept the key in his pocket, made his own bed, cleaned it himself, and allowed no other foot to cross the threshold." And of its interior: "you would have thought that you were in a ship."
   - *What the action does with it:* The cabin is a replica of the place where Carey committed his murder; he sleeps every night in a copy of the scene. The one man who saw the deed finds his way into it, and Carey dies pinned to its wall with the ship's own harpoon, "SS. Sea Unicorn, Dundee" engraved on the stock. The village pointed at the lit window and "wondered what Black Peter was doing in there"; the story answers that he was living inside his crime, and that privacy did not protect him.

2. **Cruelty at home leaves a household that is glad when the tyrant dies, and the story does not pretend otherwise.** The widow is terrified; the daughter blesses the killer.
   - *Where it is spoken:* Hopkins: "He has been known to drive his wife and daughter out of doors in the middle of the night and flog them through the park until the whole village outside the gates was aroused by their screams." Watson: "her eyes blazed defiantly at us as she told us that she was glad that her father was dead, and that she blessed the hand which had struck him down."
   - *What the action does with it:* Watson records the daughter's words without rebuke and calls the home "a terrible household that Black Peter Carey had made for himself," leaving the house "with a sense of relief." The investigation never treats the family as suspects, and never consoles them either; they simply disappear from the story after one paragraph. The text lets the daughter's blessing stand as the one honest obituary.

3. **"Killed" and "murdered" are not the same word, and the man who did it insists on the difference.** Cairns argues he struck in self-defence and that the law owes him thanks.
   - *Where it is spoken:* Cairns: "I would have you call things by their right names. You say I murdered Peter Carey, I say I killed Peter Carey, and there's all the difference." And: "if I killed Black Peter, the law should give me thanks, for I saved them the price of a hempen rope."
   - *What the action does with it:* The story gives Cairns a long, coherent, apparently truthful confession ("A very clear statement," says Holmes) and a motive that is squarely criminal: he came "to squeeze" a murderer for hush-money, and he had watched a man thrown overboard and "kept my knowledge to myself" for twelve years. Self-defence and blackmail sit in the same man. The text never adjudicates. Cairns's own conscience does: "his face gets between me and my sleep."

4. **Silence is a kind of partnership in crime, and the witness who waits to profit becomes the next victim or the next killer.** Cairns watched a man drown and said nothing.
   - *Where it is spoken:* "Only one man knew what had happened to him, and that was me... Well, I kept my knowledge to myself, and waited to see what would come of it. When we got back to Scotland it was easily hushed up, and nobody asked any questions. A stranger died by accident and it was nobody's business to inquire."
   - *What the action does with it:* The silence buys Cairns nothing: the box holds "nothing but papers that I would not dare to sell," and he ends "stranded in London without a shilling" with "only my trade left," which is the trade Holmes uses to catch him. Meanwhile Neligan's mother and son have lived twelve years under the belief that their father was a thief, a belief Cairns could have dissolved with a word. The story makes the cost of silence fall on the innocent and the profit evaporate for the silent.

5. **A son will risk prison to prove his dead father was honest, and the proof, when it comes, confirms the father and cannot restore the money.** Neligan's whole errand is to clear a name.
   - *Where it is spoken:* "He left us a list of the securities he was taking, and he swore that he would come back with his honour cleared, and that none who had trusted him would suffer." And: "if I could prove from Peter Carey's evidence how these securities came on the market it would be a proof that my father had not sold them, and that he had no view to personal profit."
   - *What the action does with it:* The story vindicates the father completely: he was rescued at sea and murdered for his box. But Holmes's last instruction is exact about the limit: "The tin box must be returned to him, but, of course, the securities which Peter Carey has sold are lost forever." The ruined families of Cornwall stay ruined. Honour is restored; nothing else is. And the son's reward for his loyalty is a night in a cell and an apology Holmes has to prompt.

6. **The strongest evidence misleads the investigator who has stopped looking for alternatives.** Hopkins's bloodstained notebook is real evidence and the wrong conclusion.
   - *Where it is spoken:* Holmes: "One should always look for a possible alternative, and provide against it. It is the first rule of criminal investigation." And: "It is very possible if I had known about this notebook it might have led away my thoughts, as it did yours."
   - *What the action does with it:* The story gives Hopkins a genuinely strong case (presence at the hut, a motive involving the securities, his own book on the floor) and a genuinely weak man as suspect; it then has Holmes refute the case not by argument but by a dead pig: "Have you tried to drive a harpoon through a body? No?" The body's physical facts outvote the documentary trail. Holmes's own admission that the notebook might have misled him too keeps the lesson from being mere superiority.

7. **The master's pleasure in instructing the pupil includes a streak of cruelty, and the pupil's pride includes a streak of ingratitude.** Hopkins dismisses Holmes; Holmes stages his lesson over cold eggs.
   - *Where it is spoken:* Hopkins: "As it turns out your presence was unnecessary, and I would have brought the case to this successful issue without you." Holmes, after the arrest: "I fear that the scrambled eggs are cold. However, you will enjoy the rest of your breakfast all the better, will you not, for the thought that you have brought your case to a triumphant conclusion." Hopkins: "I understand now, what I should never have forgotten, that I am the pupil and you are the master."
   - *What the action does with it:* Watson's framing calls Holmes "unworldly" or "capricious," and the story shows both: he lets Hopkins arrest an innocent boy and go home satisfied rather than tell him what he knows, so the correction can arrive as theatre. The young man's "hopes and his ambitions were all crumbling about him." The story treats this as comedy and as education; it is also a night in jail for Neligan that Holmes could have spared him.

8. **Violence is a skill, and skill leaves a signature.** The whole case turns on what sort of arm could have struck the blow.
   - *Where it is spoken:* Holmes: "I have satisfied myself that by no exertion of my strength can I transfix the pig with a single blow." And: "The amazing strength, the skill in the use of the harpoon, the rum and water, the sealskin tobacco-pouch with the coarse tobacco—all these pointed to a seaman, and one who had been a whaler."
   - *What the action does with it:* The story rewards an argument from trade: a man's drink, his tobacco, his weapon of choice, his way of standing ("He saluted and stood sailor-fashion, turning his cap round in his hands") are a biography. Holmes catches Cairns by offering him exactly his trade at good wages. People are found, the story says, by what they know how to do and cannot stop doing; Cairns had "only my trade left," and the trade was the trap.

## What it teaches
The story sets a murder inside a murder and asks you to notice that almost no one in it is innocent except the two people who suffer most: the drowned banker who was trying to pay his creditors, and the son who spends twelve years under his father's disgrace. Carey killed for a box; Cairns kept silent for twelve years hoping to be paid for the silence; Hopkins jails the wrong man and congratulates himself; Holmes lets him do it to make the lesson land. Everyone acts from motives that are legible and human and slightly dishonourable, and the story moves through them without raising its voice. The widow and the daughter get one paragraph and a blessing on the killer's hand; the text gives them no more because there is nothing more to give.

What is left for the reader is Cairns's distinction between killing and murder, which the story neither endorses nor refutes. His account is clear and probably true; he also came as a blackmailer and brought a hidden readiness to use the harpoon ("I thought I might need it before I was through"). The law will decide, and Holmes, who has been so exact about pig carcasses and tobacco, declines to say anything about it beyond "a very clear statement." The securities are gone; the name is cleared; Holmes is off to Norway. The lesson may be that justice in this story is mostly a matter of getting the facts right, and that getting the facts right settles far less than one would like.

## Lines worth keeping
- "So unworldly was he—or so capricious—that he frequently refused his help to the powerful and wealthy where the problem made no appeal to his sympathies."
- "I have not heard one single word of sorrow about his terrible end."
- "He kept the key in his pocket, made his own bed, cleaned it himself, and allowed no other foot to cross the threshold."
- "He was pinned like a beetle on a card."
- "I have never yet seen one which was committed by a flying creature."
- "She was glad that her father was dead, and that she blessed the hand which had struck him down."
- "One should always look for a possible alternative, and provide against it. It is the first rule of criminal investigation."
- "It seems to me to have only one drawback, Hopkins, and that is that it is intrinsically impossible."
- "You say I murdered Peter Carey, I say I killed Peter Carey, and there's all the difference."
- "A stranger died by accident and it was nobody's business to inquire."
- "His face gets between me and my sleep."
- "The securities which Peter Carey has sold are lost forever."

## Preoccupations
- a secret crime that waits years for its witness to return
- domestic tyranny and the relief that follows a tyrant's death
- the difference between killing and murder
- a son's loyalty to a disgraced father
- evidence that points the wrong way, and the duty to seek alternatives
- the teacher's pride and the pupil's humiliation
