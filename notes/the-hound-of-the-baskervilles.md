# The Hound of the Baskervilles

## What happens

Dr. Mortimer brings Holmes two documents: a 1742 manuscript recounting how the wicked Hugo Baskerville was torn apart by a hell-hound, and a newspaper account of Sir Charles Baskerville's recent death from heart failure at the moor-gate of his yew alley, beside which Mortimer privately saw "the footprints of a gigantic hound." The heir, Sir Henry, arrives from Canada and is at once shadowed, warned by an anonymous pasted-up letter, and robbed of first a new boot and then an old one. Holmes pretends to stay in London and sends Watson to Dartmoor as guardian and reporter. Watson's letters and diary work through the neighbours: the butler Barrymore signalling at night to the escaped convict Selden, who proves to be his wife's brother; the naturalist Stapleton and his "sister" Beryl, who warns Watson, mistaking him for Sir Henry; the litigious crank Frankland; and Laura Lyons, who had asked Sir Charles to meet her at the gate on the night he died. Watson tracks a mysterious man on the tor to a Neolithic hut and finds Holmes already there. That night Selden, wearing Sir Henry's cast-off clothes, is run to his death by the hound. Holmes recognizes Stapleton's face in the portrait of Hugo, proving him a Baskerville with designs on the estate; lacking a case a jury could accept, he uses Sir Henry as bait, and he and Watson shoot the phosphorus-painted hound as it brings the baronet down. Beryl is found bound and whipped; Stapleton flees into the Grimpen Mire and is never seen again. In a closing chapter in Baker Street, Holmes reconstructs the whole scheme.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **Fear kills through a mind already prepared to be killed, and no one is exempt from it.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Mortimer of Sir Charles: "He was a strong-minded man, sir, shrewd, practical, and as unimaginative as I am myself. Yet he took this document very seriously, and his mind was prepared for just such an end as did eventually overtake him" (Ch. 2). Stapleton, half-confessing: "His nerves were so worked up that the appearance of any dog might have had a fatal effect upon his diseased heart" (Ch. 7). Sir Henry in London: "There is no devil in hell, Mr. Holmes, and there is no man upon earth who can prevent me from going to the home of my own people" (Ch. 4).
   - *What the action does with it:* Confirmed for Sir Charles, who ran "until he burst his heart" (Ch. 3). But the work refuses to leave fear to the superstitious and the sick. Sir Henry, the sceptic, hears the cry on the moor and says "that sound seemed to freeze my very blood. Feel my hand!" (Ch. 9), adding "it was one thing to laugh about it in London, and it is another to stand out here in the darkness." Selden, a hardened murderer, screams for help and runs to his death. In Ch. 14 Holmes, Watson and Lestrade are all "paralyzed... by the apparition" and Lestrade "threw himself face downward upon the ground." Knowledge that the hound is chemistry comes after the terror, not instead of it.

2. **The devil's agents are flesh and blood; the supernatural is a story someone finds useful.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Mortimer: "There is a realm in which the most acute and most experienced of detectives is helpless" (Ch. 3). Holmes: "The devil's agents may be of flesh and blood, may they not?... we are bound to exhaust all other hypotheses before falling back upon this one" (Ch. 3).
   - *What the action does with it:* Holmes is right in fact: the hound is a bloodhound-mastiff cross daubed with "a cunning preparation" of phosphorus (Ch. 14). The work goes further and shows why the legend was useful to a murderer: "what peasant would venture to inquire too closely into such a creature should he get sight of it" (Ch. 14), and the newspaper itself is relieved that a "prosaic finding" will keep a tenant at the Hall. Yet the 1742 manuscript's own moral — "Learn then from this story not to fear the fruits of the past, but rather to be circumspect in the future, that those foul passions whereby our family has suffered so grievously may not again be loosed" (Ch. 2) — turns out truer than the writer knew: the passion is loosed again, in Hugo's living double.

3. **Blood tells, but it does not tell you which way.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Mortimer on Rodger: "He came of the old masterful Baskerville strain and was the very image, they tell me, of the family picture of old Hugo" (Ch. 3). Watson on Sir Henry: "how true a descendant he was of that long line of high-blooded, fiery, and masterful men" (Ch. 6). Holmes before the portrait: "an interesting instance of a throwback, which appears to be both physical and spiritual. A study of family portraits is enough to convert a man to the doctrine of reincarnation" (Ch. 13).
   - *What the action does with it:* Stapleton has Hugo's face and Hugo's cruelty, so the hereditary claim is confirmed at the centre. But the same line yields the generous Sir Charles and the brave, decent Sir Henry, whose inherited "fiery temper" (Ch. 4) only makes him refuse to run. Mrs. Barrymore and Selden share blood too — "Was it possible that this stolidly respectable person was of the same blood as one of the most notorious criminals in the country?" (Ch. 9) — and she blames upbringing, not inheritance: "We humoured him too much when he was a lad and gave him his own way in everything until he came to think that the world was made for his pleasure" (Ch. 9). The work holds both explanations and settles neither.

4. **The real curse is money; fate is what greed looks like from outside.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Holmes, hearing the estate is worth nearly a million: "It is a stake for which a man might well play a desperate game" (Ch. 5). Sir Henry: "House, land, and dollars must go together" (Ch. 5). Mortimer: "it cannot be denied that the prosperity of the whole poor, bleak countryside depends upon his presence... I fear lest I should be swayed too much by my own obvious interest in the matter" (Ch. 3).
   - *What the action does with it:* Holmes's retrospection reduces the "curse" to a succession plot: "He meant in the end to have the estate, and he was ready to use any tool or run any risk for that end" (Ch. 15). Everyone in the book has a financial relation to the Hall — the Barrymores' legacy, Mortimer's thousand pounds, the charity to Laura Lyons, the half-built lodge paid for with "South African gold" (Ch. 6). The legend is what the money-motive wears on the moor. Only the Desmond cousin who "refused to accept any settlement" (Ch. 5) is beyond suspicion, and he is a clergyman far away.

5. **Guilty behaviour is a poor guide to guilt; nearly everyone hides something, and the murderer hides least.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Watson on Barrymore: "there was something indescribably guilty and furtive in his whole appearance" (Ch. 8). Holmes: "It is the first quality of a criminal investigator that he should see through a disguise" (Ch. 13).
   - *What the action does with it:* Every furtive person is innocent of the crime. Barrymore's night walks are a sister's charity; Laura Lyons lies because "I feared that some false conclusion might be drawn from it" (Ch. 11); Beryl's whispered warnings are the acts of a terrified wife. The murderer is the "impassive colourless man, with his straw hat and his butterfly-net" (Ch. 12), whose only slip is "a true piece of autobiography" about his school (Ch. 12). Watson's letters record his wrong suspicions honestly — "I tell you the direction of my suspicions, however much the result may have shown that they were unfounded" (Ch. 9) — so the reader is shown that the narrator's instinct for guilt is itself the problem.

6. **Love and fear are not opposites, and a beloved can be used as a tool until the love is exposed as counterfeit.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Holmes on Beryl: "an influence over her which may have been love or may have been fear, or very possibly both, since they are by no means incompatible emotions" (Ch. 15). Beryl: "I could endure it all, ill-usage, solitude, a life of deception, everything, as long as I could still cling to the hope that I had his love, but now I know that in this also I have been his dupe and his tool" (Ch. 14). Laura Lyons: "I imagined that all was for my own sake. But now I see that I was never anything but a tool in his hands. Why should I preserve faith with him who never kept any with me?" (Ch. 13).
   - *What the action does with it:* Both women keep Stapleton's secrets through murder and would have gone on keeping them; Laura admits "I knew him... But if he had kept faith with me I should always have done so with him" (Ch. 13). What breaks them is not conscience but learning of a rival. Holmes's method depends on exactly this — "If I could only use it to detach his wife" (Ch. 12) — so the detective, too, treats love as a lever. And the one love in the book that is "deep and sincere," Sir Henry's, is the one the plot exploits most cruelly: "the saddest part of all this black business was that he should have been deceived by her" (Ch. 15).

7. **Decent people put kin before law, and the work lets them, but shows the mercy has a price.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Mrs. Barrymore: "to me, sir, he was always the little curly-headed boy that I had nursed and played with as an elder sister would" (Ch. 9). Sir Henry: "I guess we are aiding and abetting a felony, Watson? But, after what we have heard I don't feel as if I could give the man up, so there is an end of it" (Ch. 10). Holmes, half-joking: "you are all on the wrong side of the law in this matter. I am not sure that as a conscientious detective my first duty is not to arrest the whole household" (Ch. 13).
   - *What the action does with it:* Watson will not shoot "an unarmed man who was running away" (Ch. 9); Holmes quietly arranges that "none of you will be troubled in the matter" (Ch. 13). Yet the charity is what kills Selden: Sir Henry's gift of old clothes to Barrymore, passed on to the convict, puts Sir Henry's scent on him, and "the clothes have been the poor devil's death" (Ch. 12). The narrator's own judgment of Selden moves from "an unmitigated scoundrel for whom there was neither pity nor excuse" (Ch. 9) to "Poor devil! Whatever his crimes, he has suffered something to atone for them" (Ch. 10) to "Evil indeed is the man who has not one woman to mourn him" (Ch. 13). Mercy is affirmed; it is not made safe.

8. **Knowing is not proving, and the gap between them is paid for by someone else's body.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Holmes: "It is not what we know, but what we can prove" (Ch. 12); "You and I know that he died of sheer fright, and we know also what frightened him, but how are we to get twelve stolid jurymen to know it?" (Ch. 12).
   - *What the action does with it:* Because a jury needs the hound caught in the act, Sir Henry must walk the moor alone: "we had to use Sir Henry, alone and apparently unprotected, as a bait... at the cost of a severe shock to our client" (Ch. 15). The exchange after the kill is exact — "You have saved my life." "Having first endangered it." (Ch. 14). And after all this, no jury ever sits: Stapleton is taken by the mire, "forever buried" (Ch. 14). The law's requirement of proof drove the whole risk, and the law then had nothing to do.

9. **The brilliant man's love of surprise is a vanity that endangers the people he protects.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Watson: "One of Sherlock Holmes's defects—if, indeed, one may call it a defect—was that he was exceedingly loath to communicate his full plans to any other person until the instant of their fulfilment. Partly it came no doubt from his own masterful nature, which loved to dominate and surprise those who were around him" (Ch. 14). Watson to Holmes: "Then you use me, and yet do not trust me!" (Ch. 12).
   - *What the action does with it:* Believing the body on the rocks is Sir Henry, Holmes says, "In order to have my case well rounded and complete, I have thrown away the life of my client" (Ch. 12). He is wrong only by accident of clothing. Later the fog he "could not predict" nearly finishes what the first mistake began, and he concedes "a reproach to my management of the case" (Ch. 15). The work does not punish Holmes, but it records the cost and the luck, and it has Watson note that Holmes's laughter "has always boded ill to somebody" (Ch. 13).

10. **Modern England is a thin crust over something older; the moor is where the crust is thinnest.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Watson: "if you were to see a skin-clad, hairy man crawl out from the low door fitting a flint-tipped arrow on to the string of his bow, you would feel that his presence there was more natural than your own" (Ch. 8). Sir Henry: "I'll have a row of electric lamps up here inside of six months... a thousand candle-power Swan and Edison right here in front of the hall door" (Ch. 6). Watson: "Life has become like that great Grimpen Mire, with little green patches everywhere into which one may sink and with no guide to point the track" (Ch. 7).
   - *What the action does with it:* Selden's face "might well have belonged to one of those old savages who dwelt in the burrows on the hillsides" (Ch. 9); Holmes himself sleeps "upon that very stone slab upon which Neolithic man had once slumbered" (Ch. 11). The scientific men win with a lens, a chemistry of phosphorus, and revolvers, but the final judgment is executed by bog, not by any human institution, and the electric lamps are never installed: Sir Henry leaves broken for a long voyage.

11. **The narrator underrates himself, overrates his deductions, and is indispensable anyway.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Holmes: "It may be that you are not yourself luminous, but you are a conductor of light," immediately followed by "I am afraid, my dear Watson, that most of your conclusions were erroneous" (Ch. 1). Watson, alone: "It would indeed be a triumph for me if I could run him to earth where my master had failed" (Ch. 11).
   - *What the action does with it:* Watson's deductions about Barrymore, the "love intrigue," and the man on the tor all miss; he lets Sir Henry walk out alone against orders; he reads character off faces ("some coarseness of expression, some hardness, perhaps, of eye," Ch. 11). Yet he finds Laura Lyons a day before Holmes would have, tracks the hut, and his reports carry the one truthful detail — Stapleton's school — that identifies the murderer. Holmes's praise is real: "You have been invaluable to me in this as in many other cases" (Ch. 12). The work neither mocks nor flatters its narrator; it shows what a loyal, limited, honest witness is worth.

12. **A man can love the law and have no use for justice; the law in turn fails the people who most need it.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Frankland: "I act entirely from a sense of public duty," and in the same breath, "nothing would induce me to help the rascals in any way" (Ch. 11). Watson: "He fights for the mere pleasure of fighting and is equally ready to take up either side of a question" (Ch. 8). Laura Lyons on her husband: "The law is upon his side, and every day I am faced by the possibility that he may force me to live with him" (Ch. 11).
   - *What the action does with it:* Frankland's lawsuits swallow a fortune while his daughter "might have starved for all that my father cared" (Ch. 11); that poverty is what Stapleton buys her letter with. The law that gives her abandoned husband rights over her is the pressure that nearly makes her an accessory to murder. Frankland's telescope, meant for spite against the police, is what leads Watson to Holmes. The work makes legal obsession comic and legal failure cruel, in the same family.

## What it teaches

The book keeps saying, through Holmes, that fear is a product that can be manufactured — a dog, a tin of phosphorus, a legend — and keeps showing, through everyone including Holmes, that knowing this does not stop the knees from shaking. The lesson is not that reason conquers superstition. It is that reason arrives afterward, with a lens and a match, and explains what the body already did. Sir Charles died of an explanation he believed; Sir Henry nearly died of one he did not. What saves him is not his scepticism but two revolvers and a good deal of luck with a convict's wardrobe.

The harder lesson is about proof. Holmes knows who the murderer is from the moment he smells jessamine on a letter in London, and he can do nothing with the knowledge for weeks. The law's requirement that guilt be shown to twelve ordinary people is a protection for the innocent that, here, puts the innocent on a dark path as bait. The work does not resolve this. It gives you the clean exchange — "You have saved my life." "Having first endangered it." — and then lets the mire render a verdict no court delivered. If you want to believe that justice was done, you have to accept that it was done by a bog.

Then there is the matter of who keeps faith with whom. Nearly every secret in the book is kept out of loyalty — a sister's to a brother, a wife's to a brutal husband, a mistress's to a liar, a butler's to a dead master's reputation — and the loyalties are broken not by conscience but by the discovery of betrayal. Beryl and Laura turn on Stapleton when they learn of each other. The detective relies on this. So the reader is left asking whether fidelity in this book is ever a virtue, or only a condition that lasts until a better-informed self-interest arrives. The Barrymores, who have nothing to gain and keep faith with the worst man on the moor, are the quiet answer, and the work makes their mercy cost a life.

## Preoccupations

- fear and the body: terror that works regardless of belief
- the supernatural as a cover story for human greed
- inheritance: whether character comes down in the blood
- the gap between what is known and what can be proved
- loyalty to kin and lovers against the law
- love and fear living together; the beloved as instrument
- the detective's vanity and the cost of his secrecy
- the thinness of civilization over an older, wilder ground
- the law as a game for some and a trap for others
