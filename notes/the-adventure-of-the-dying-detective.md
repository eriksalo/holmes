# The Adventure of the Dying Detective

## What happens
Mrs. Hudson fetches Watson, now married, to Baker Street: Holmes has been three days without food or drink and is dying of something he caught on a case in Rotherhithe. Watson finds him wasted and feverish, is ordered to stand back because the disease is "contagious by touch," is insulted as a mediocre practitioner when he insists on examining him, and is locked in when he tries to fetch a specialist. Holmes shrieks when Watson picks up a small ivory box on the mantel, rambles about oysters and half-crowns, and at six o'clock sends him to bring not a doctor but a Sumatran planter, Culverton Smith, whose nephew Holmes had suspected him of murdering. Smith, after a flash of a malicious smile in a mirror, agrees to come alone. Watson is hidden behind the bed. Smith gloats over the dying man, admits killing his nephew, explains that the ivory box sent by post carried a poisoned spring, pockets the box as the last evidence, and turns up the gas to watch Holmes die—at which Holmes asks for a cigarette in his ordinary voice. The gas was the signal; Inspector Morton enters and arrests Smith. Holmes explains that he fasted and painted himself to deceive Mrs. Hudson, who deceived Watson, who could not have deceived Smith had he known, and proposes dinner at Simpson's.

## What the work observes about being human
1. **An honest person can be used as an instrument precisely because he cannot lie; his honesty is borrowed without his consent.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Holmes afterwards: "among your many talents dissimulation finds no place, and that if you had shared my secret you would never have been able to impress Smith with the urgent necessity of his presence, which was the vital point of the whole scheme."
   - *What the action does with it:* The scheme works exactly as designed—Watson's real anguish ("Of all ruins, that of a noble mind is the most deplorable") is the bait that brings Smith. Mrs. Hudson is left "trembling and weeping" in the passage for the same reason. The story confirms the method and lets the cost show: Holmes's "thousand apologies" at the end are for forgetting Watson behind the bed, not for three days of making two people grieve.

2. **To act a part convincingly one must partly become it, and the performance may require real cruelty to those closest.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Holmes: "The best way of successfully acting a part is to be it. I give you my word that for three days I have tasted neither food nor drink." To Watson earlier: "you are only a general practitioner with very limited experience and mediocre qualifications."
   - *What the action does with it:* Watson is "bitterly hurt." Holmes's later gloss turns the insult inside out—he kept Watson at four yards because "your astute judgment would pass a dying man who, however weak, had no rise of pulse or temperature?" The cruelty was a disguised compliment, but Watson had to live inside the cruelty for two hours without the compliment. The story shows what the artist of deception spends and who pays.

3. **A murderer's need to be known as the cleverer man is what destroys him; pride in one's brains is the shared weakness of hunter and hunted.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Smith: "You are proud of your brains, Holmes, are you not? Think yourself smart, don't you? You came across someone who was smarter this time." "I'd like you to know before you die." Holmes: "Knowing his vindictive nature, I was perfectly certain that he would come to look upon his handiwork."
   - *What the action does with it:* Smith could have stayed home. He comes to gloat, explains the box in detail so his victim will understand, and pockets it in front of a hidden witness. His vanity is the whole case. Holmes's own vanity is on display too—"That pretence I have carried out with the thoroughness of the true artist"—and the story suggests the difference between them is not pride but what the pride is for.

4. **A doctor's duty can override deference to a friend, and the friend who refuses it is still loved.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Watson: "A sick man is but a child, and so I will treat you... Let him be my master elsewhere, I at least was his in a sick room." "If you think that I am going to stand here and see you die without either helping you myself or bringing anyone else to help you, then you have mistaken your man."
   - *What the action does with it:* Watson's assertion of professional authority is defeated—he is locked in and sent on an errand—but the quality it displays is exactly what Holmes relies on: "You won't fail me. You never did fail me." The man who cannot be put off is the one worth sending. The story honours Watson's stubbornness even as the plot makes a fool of it.

5. **Knowledge of how things die is power over who dies, and the collector of such knowledge may think of it as a hobby.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Smith: "He is an amateur of crime, as I am of disease. For him the villain, for me the microbe. There are my prisons... Among those gelatine cultivations some of the very worst offenders in the world are now doing time."
   - *What the action does with it:* The jars are not prisons; they are an arsenal. One of them killed his nephew "who stood between this monster and a reversion," and another was mailed to Holmes in a pretty box. The study of disease "which has been his dearest hobby" is described by Holmes to Watson with a straight face as the reason to fetch him. The story makes the parallel Smith draws exact and then shows which way it cuts.

6. **A respectable house can hold a man who will not be disturbed at his studies and who has murdered his nephew.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Watson on Lower Burke Street: "an air of smug and demure respectability in its old-fashioned iron railings, its massive folding-door, and its shining brasswork." Smith: "how often have I said that I am not to be disturbed in my hours of study?"
   - *What the action does with it:* Watson glimpses the truth—"I could have sworn that it was set in a malicious and abominable smile"—and talks himself out of it: "I persuaded myself that it must have been some nervous contraction." The honest observer distrusts his own perception of evil in a respectable setting; the story lets him record both the sight and the self-correction, and then proves the sight was right.

7. **The person who exposes a crime becomes, in the criminal's mind, the aggressor.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Smith: "Who asked you to cross my path? If you had left me alone I would not have hurt you." "It matters nothing to me that you should know how my nephew died. It's not him we are talking about. It's you."
   - *What the action does with it:* The nephew's death is, to Smith, settled business; the only wrong that matters is being seen. The story offers this as the murderer's whole moral world and does not argue with it—it simply has Watson behind the bedstead hearing every word.

8. **Fondness and fear can coexist in the same person toward the same man.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Watson on Mrs. Hudson: "The landlady stood in the deepest awe of him and never dared to interfere with him... She was fond of him, too." On Holmes: "He disliked and distrusted the sex, but he was always a chivalrous opponent."
   - *What the action does with it:* Her fondness is what makes her believable to Watson and so useful to Holmes; her awe is what kept her from calling a doctor for three days, which the plan required. The story uses her as it uses Watson, and gives her the opening paragraph as a kind of acknowledgment.

## What it teaches
This is a story about what it is to be used by someone you love for a purpose you would have approved. Watson approves, afterwards; the reader is meant to. But the text spends most of its length inside the two hours when he did not know, and it records that time without softening: the insult to his competence, the scream over the ivory box, the babble about oysters that made him think a great mind had collapsed. Holmes's reasoning—that an honest man cannot carry a lie, so the lie must be put into him whole—is sound, and it is also a description of how to treat a friend as a conductor for a current. The battery joke ("I wonder how a battery feels when it pours electricity into a non-conductor?") is Holmes mocking Watson from inside the act, and it is also exactly what he is doing.

The other thing the story leaves is the mirror image of detective and murderer. Smith says it—amateur of crime, amateur of disease—and Holmes's performance is as much a display of pride as Smith's gloating. What separates them is not temperament but direction: Smith's cleverness is spent on a reversion and a grudge; Holmes's is spent on making Smith say the truth aloud. The story does not claim more than that. It ends with claret, biscuits, and dinner, and with two people—a landlady and a doctor—who were frightened for three days so that one man could be caught.

## Preoccupations
- using an honest person as an instrument
- performance and what it costs the performer and the audience
- pride in cleverness shared by detective and criminal
- respectability as a house for murder
- friendship tested by deception
- the exposer seen by the guilty as the aggressor
