# The Adventure of the Retired Colourman

## What happens
Josiah Amberley, a retired manufacturer of paints, comes to Holmes "a pathetic, futile, broken creature": his much younger wife has run off with his chess partner Dr. Ernest and taken his life savings from his strong-room. Holmes, busy, sends Watson to Lewisham. Watson finds a neglected house, a man obsessively painting the woodwork, an unused theatre ticket for the wife (seat B31), and a tall dark stranger following him. Holmes sees the paint as a cover for another smell and checks the theatre chart: neither B30 nor B32 was occupied, so the alibi is false. He forges a telegram from a country vicar to get Amberley out of the house for a night, with Watson as escort, and burgles it, where he is caught climbing out by the stranger, Barker, a rival detective hired by Ernest's family. Together they confront Amberley: "What did you do with the bodies?" Amberley tries to swallow poison; Holmes stops him. The strong-room was a gas chamber fed by a hidden pipe; the dying man scrawled "We we--" a foot above the floor; the bodies are in a disused well. The local paper credits Inspector MacKinnon with the whole thing.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **The injured party's performance of grief can be the murderer's disguise, and self-pity is its favourite costume.**
   - Where it is spoken: Amberley to Watson: "human nature, Dr. Watson--the black ingratitude of it all! When did I ever refuse one of her requests? Was ever a woman so pampered? And that young man--he might have been my own son... Oh, Dr. Watson, it is a dreadful, dreadful world!" And: "I never wish to see her damned face again."
   - What the action does with it: Every word of his grievance is spoken by the man who gassed the two people he is grieving over. Watson believes him; Holmes, who has not met him, half-believes him ("my information confirms the man's story"). The story shows how exactly the vocabulary of the wronged husband fits the mouth of the killer, and how a photograph torn up "in a tempest of passion" reads as heartbreak when it is hatred.

2. **Miserliness is a form of jealousy, and jealousy a form of possession.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "He was a miserable miser who made his wife so wretched by his niggardly ways that she was a ready prey for any adventurer... Like all misers, he was a jealous man, and his jealousy became a frantic mania. Rightly or wrongly, he suspected an intrigue."
   - What the action does with it: The story shows Amberley grudging a third-class fare and a hotel bill while two bodies lie in his well. His wife was twenty years younger and "a good-looking woman"; he kept her, like the securities, in a strong-room he had built. The action confirms Holmes's link: the man who could not bear to part with money could not bear to part with a wife, and treated her flight, real or suspected, as theft. The securities were never stolen; he hid them himself.

3. **Life reaches and grasps and is left with a shadow; the pessimist's view is spoken by the detective and acted out by the murderer.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "is not all life pathetic and futile? Is not his story a microcosm of the whole? We reach. We grasp. And what is left in our hands at the end? A shadow. Or worse than a shadow--misery."
   - What the action does with it: Holmes says this about a man he believes to be a victim. The story then reveals that the man reached, grasped, and killed, and is left with "Broadmoor rather than the scaffold." The philosophic mood is undercut: the futility was not Fate's but Amberley's own making. Yet the maxim is not quite refuted either. A man with "A competence, a wife, leisure" did end as "as broken and miserable a creature as crawls beneath the sun," by his own hand.

4. **A careful criminal is undone by the one detail he thought proved his innocence.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "He made a bad slip when he allowed my astute friend to notice the number of the seat taken for his wife." And: "Why should this man at such a time be filling his house with strong odours? Obviously, to cover some other smell."
   - What the action does with it: Amberley produced the unused ticket as proof he had been at the theatre; the ticket's number is what proved he had not. He painted to hide the gas; the paint is what drew attention. The story confirms a pattern seen elsewhere in the collection: the overdone alibi and the overdone cover are the confessions. Holmes names the cause: "Pure swank! He felt so clever and so sure of himself that he imagined no one could touch him."

5. **The dying want above all to be understood, and will spend their last strength on a message.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "we will suppose that you were shut up in this little room, had not two minutes to live, but wanted to get even with the fiend who was probably mocking at you from the other side of the door. What would you do?" "Write a message." "Exactly. You would like to tell people how you died."
   - What the action does with it: The purple pencil scrawl "We we--" a foot above the skirting is the story's one moment of direct contact with the victims, who are otherwise names. It confirms the claim with terrible economy: a man on the floor, losing consciousness, writing to a future he would not see. Holmes's method, "always putting yourself in the other fellow's place," is here a form of sympathy, not just technique.

6. **Credit for the truth goes to whoever is positioned to receive it, and the truth-finder may prefer it that way.**
   - Where it is spoken: MacKinnon: "You will excuse us for feeling sore when you jump in with methods which we cannot use, and so rob us of the credit." Holmes: "I assure you that I efface myself from now onwards." The newspaper: "a standing example of the intelligence of our professional detectives."
   - What the action does with it: The story ends with a column of print giving every one of Holmes's inferences to the inspector, and Holmes smiling: "Some day the true story may be told." Holmes's irregular methods (forgery, burglary, a bluff that bypasses the caution) were what caught the man; the official record omits them because they cannot be admitted. The story is frank that the public account of justice is a fiction agreed between those who know better.

7. **Watson sees and does not infer; Holmes infers and does not see; the work needs both.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "though in your mission you have missed everything of importance, yet even those things which have obtruded themselves upon your notice give rise to serious thought." And: "You can thank Dr. Watson's observation for that, though he failed to draw the inference."
   - What the action does with it: Both decisive clues, the paint and the seat number, are Watson's. Holmes mocks his "poetry," lists what he failed to ask, and then builds the whole case on what he brought back. Watson calls himself "long-suffering"; Holmes calls him "invaluable throughout." The story confirms a partnership in which one man's limitations are the other's material, and it lets Watson's small pride in remembering "my old school number" stand as the hinge of a double murder case.

## What it teaches
The Retired Colourman is the last story in the book, and it begins with Holmes asking whether all life is "pathetic and futile." The story's answer is sly: the man who prompted the thought was not a victim of life but its author. He had everything a cautious man works for and destroyed it out of jealousy and avarice, then came to Holmes to be pitied. The lesson is partly about reading grief: the loudest complaint about ingratitude came from the person with the most to hide. It is also about the way philosophy can be a mistake of attribution. Holmes's melancholy was real; it was simply aimed at the wrong man.

What the story leaves unresolved is the ending's cheer. Two people were gassed in a sealed room, and the tale closes on a newspaper joke about police credit and Holmes's "tolerant smile." The victims are the wife who was "a ready prey" because her husband starved her of everything, and a young doctor who "probably played the fool," and neither is given a word except the two the dying man managed to write. The story knows how to catch a murderer and does not much ask what was lost. A reader who notices that may feel the opening question was not answered after all, only changed hands.

## Lines worth keeping
- "A pathetic, futile, broken creature."
- "We reach. We grasp. And what is left in our hands at the end? A shadow. Or worse than a shadow--misery."
- "Just as medical men occasionally send their incurables to a quack."
- "The old story, Watson. A treacherous friend and a fickle wife."
- "Cut out the poetry, Watson... I note that it was a high brick wall."
- "One must do something to ease an aching heart."
- "Let us escape from this weary workaday world by the side door of music."
- "There is always Nature, Watson--Nature and Josiah Amberley--you can be in close commune with both."
- "No short cuts, Josiah Amberley. Things must be done decently and in order."
- "Burglary has always been an alternative profession, had I cared to adopt it."
- "You'll get results, Inspector, by always putting yourself in the other fellow's place, and thinking what you would do yourself. It takes some imagination, but it pays."
- "Some day the true story may be told."

## Preoccupations
- the wronged party as the guilty one
- avarice and jealousy as a single appetite
- whether life is futile, and who makes it so
- the alibi that convicts
- the dying need to leave a message
- credit for justice going to the wrong people
