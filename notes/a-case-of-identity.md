# A Case of Identity

## What happens
Mary Sutherland, a short-sighted typist with a small independent income that her mother and young stepfather enjoy while she lives at home, comes to Holmes because her fiancé, Hosmer Angel, stepped into a cab on their wedding morning and was not in it when it arrived at the church. Her stepfather, James Windibank, has refused to involve police or detectives. From the typewritten letters, the typewritten signature, the tinted glasses and whispering voice, and the fact that Angel appeared only when Windibank was abroad, Holmes concludes that the stepfather, with the mother's help, invented Angel to keep Mary bound to a phantom and her income in the house. He confronts Windibank, who admits it and points out that nothing he did is illegal. Holmes reaches for a whip; Windibank runs. Holmes decides not to tell Mary, since she would not believe him.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **Ordinary life is stranger than fiction, but you have to know where to look to see it.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "life is infinitely stranger than anything which the mind of man could invent... Depend upon it, there is nothing so unnatural as the commonplace." Watson disagrees and picks a headline, "A husband's cruelty to his wife," which Holmes shows to be the Dundas case of a teetotaller who threw his false teeth at his wife.
   - What the action does with it: The case confirms Holmes. A domestic non-event with no crime in it turns out to contain a stepfather courting his stepdaughter in disguise with her mother's connivance. But the story also qualifies the claim: Holmes himself calls the problem "rather a trite one" with parallels in Andover and The Hague. What is strange is not the trick but the human arrangement that produced it, and the person who is "most instructive" is the client, not the puzzle.

2. **A person can be kept in dependence by the people who profess to protect her.**
   - Where it is spoken: Mary on Windibank: "He never did wish us to go anywhere. He would get quite mad if I wanted so much as to join a Sunday-school treat." And: "he used to say that a woman should be happy in her own family circle. But then, as I used to say to mother, a woman wants her own circle to begin with, and I had not got mine yet."
   - What the action does with it: Holmes's reconstruction confirms that the confinement was economic: "her marriage would mean, of course, the loss of a hundred a year." Mary's own account of her finances ("as long as I live at home I don't wish to be a burden to them, and so they have the use of the money") shows that she takes her generosity for a virtue while the household takes it as a revenue. The story shows a family that uses the language of propriety and protection to enforce a financial arrangement, and the protected person never sees it.

3. **The small things are the important ones.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "It has long been an axiom of mine that the little things are infinitely the most important." And later: "I can never bring you to realise the importance of sleeves, the suggestiveness of thumb-nails, or the great issues that may hang from a boot-lace."
   - What the action does with it: The case is solved by a typewriter's slurred "e" and tailless "r," and by the fact that a lover typed his own signature. Mary herself notices small things and reads them as tenderness: he would not let her typewrite because "the machine had come between us," and she adds, "That will just show you how fond he was of me, Mr. Holmes, and the little things that he would think of." The same detail that Holmes reads as a mark of fraud, she reads as a mark of love. The story confirms the axiom while showing that detail is neutral until interpreted, and the interpretation follows the heart.

4. **Loyalty to a vow can be noble even when the person it is given to does not exist.**
   - Where it is spoken: Mary: "I shall be true to Hosmer. He shall find me ready when he comes back." Watson: "For all the preposterous hat and the vacuous face, there was something noble in the simple faith of our visitor which compelled our respect."
   - What the action does with it: The story confirms Watson's respect and then makes it tragic. Her faith was engineered exactly to produce this result: "James Windibank wished Miss Sutherland to be so bound to Hosmer Angel, and so uncertain as to his fate, that for ten years to come, at any rate, she would not listen to another man." Her virtue is real and it is also the mechanism of her imprisonment. Watson's judgment stands; the story shows the price of it.

5. **The worst wrongs may be outside the reach of the law.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "The only drawback is that there is no law, I fear, that can touch the scoundrel." Windibank: "It—it's not actionable." And: "if you are so very sharp you ought to be sharp enough to know that it is you who are breaking the law now, and not me."
   - What the action does with it: The confrontation reverses who is the offender under the law. Holmes has locked the door; Windibank is right that this is "illegal constraint." Holmes's response is to reach for a hunting crop and to say, "there never was a man who deserved punishment more." The story leaves the gap between law and desert fully open: the only punishment Windibank receives is fright, and Holmes's own instinct for justice takes the form of an assault he does not quite commit.

6. **Cruelty often begins as a joke and grows because it works.**
   - Where it is spoken: Windibank: "It was only a joke at first... We never thought that she would have been so carried away." Holmes: "it was as cruel and selfish and heartless a trick in a petty way as ever came before me." And: "That fellow will rise from crime to crime until he does something very bad, and ends on a gallows."
   - What the action does with it: Windibank's excuse and Holmes's prophecy sit side by side without the story deciding between them. The scheme escalated step by step: letters, meetings, an engagement, an oath on the Testament, a vanishing. Each step was "obvious" given the one before. The story shows how a petty scheme acquires the shape of a serious one without any single moment of decision, and Holmes's prediction says that character formed this way does not stop.

7. **Sometimes the kindest thing is to leave a person her illusion, and this is also a way of failing her.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "If I tell her she will not believe me. You may remember the old Persian saying, 'There is danger for him who taketh the tiger cub, and danger also for whoso snatches a delusion from a woman.'"
   - What the action does with it: Holmes told Mary at the outset, "try to let Mr. Hosmer Angel vanish from your memory," and she refused. Now he knows the truth and withholds it, on the ground that she would not accept it. The result is that the stepfather's plan succeeds completely: Mary stays bound to a phantom, the income stays in the house, and the one man who knows chooses silence. The story does not say Holmes is wrong. It shows that his decision and Windibank's goal produce the same outcome.

## What it teaches
This is the only story in the group where the detective solves everything and changes nothing. Windibank walks free because he broke no law. Mary keeps her faith because no one will take it from her. The household goes on drawing her hundred a year. Holmes's closing proverb is wise, and it is also a shrug. You are left asking what detection is for if its result is a private satisfaction shared between two men over a pipe, while the person who paid for it goes home to the people who deceived her.

The difficulty is that there is no clean alternative. Telling Mary would, as Holmes says, probably fail, and might cost her the one thing that gives her life shape. Whipping Windibank would make Holmes the criminal. The story's honesty is in refusing to pretend otherwise: it names a wrong that is real, petty, and permanent, and lets you feel the uselessness of knowing. Watson's phrase about Mary, "something noble in the simple faith," is the story's truest note and its saddest, because the nobility is exactly what was being exploited.

## Lines worth keeping
- "life is infinitely stranger than anything which the mind of man could invent."
- "Depend upon it, there is nothing so unnatural as the commonplace."
- "a woman wants her own circle to begin with, and I had not got mine yet."
- "That will just show you how fond he was of me, Mr. Holmes, and the little things that he would think of."
- "It has long been an axiom of mine that the little things are infinitely the most important."
- "For all the preposterous hat and the vacuous face, there was something noble in the simple faith of our visitor which compelled our respect."
- "Never trust to general impressions, my boy, but concentrate yourself upon details."
- "He conceives an idea more creditable to his head than to his heart."
- "The law cannot, as you say, touch you, yet there never was a man who deserved punishment more."
- "It was only a joke at first."
- "There is danger for him who taketh the tiger cub, and danger also for whoso snatches a delusion from a woman."

## Preoccupations
- the gap between law and justice
- dependence disguised as protection within a family
- loyalty to an illusion
- money as the hidden motive beneath respectability
- the strangeness of ordinary life
- the limits of knowing the truth when no one will act on it
