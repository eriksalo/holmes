# The Final Problem

## What happens
Two years after the event, Watson writes to answer "the recent letters in which Colonel James Moriarty defends the memory of his brother." On the evening of 24 April 1891 Holmes walked into Watson's consulting-room pale and thin, closed the shutters against air-guns, showed two bleeding knuckles, and asked Watson to come to the Continent. He explained Professor Moriarty: a mathematician of good birth and "hereditary tendencies of the most diabolical kind," the unseen "Napoleon of crime" who sits "like a spider in the centre of its web," whom Holmes has spent three months netting and whom he expects to see arrested with his whole gang on Monday. That morning Moriarty had called at Baker Street, listed the dates on which Holmes had inconvenienced him, told him to "stand clear... or be trodden under foot," and promised that if Holmes destroyed him he would do as much in return; since then a van, a brick and a bludgeon-man had each nearly killed Holmes. Watson follows elaborate instructions, is driven to Victoria by a cloaked coachman who turns out to be Mycroft, and shares a carriage with an aged Italian priest who turns out to be Holmes. Moriarty reaches the platform as the train leaves; Holmes predicts he will hire a special, abandons the luggage at Canterbury, and watches the special roar past. In Strasburg a telegram reports the gang taken and Moriarty escaped. Holmes tells Watson to go home; Watson refuses. They walk through Switzerland, Holmes in "exuberant spirits," repeating that if society were freed of Moriarty he would cheerfully end his career. At the Reichenbach Falls a Swiss boy brings a letter calling Watson back to a dying Englishwoman; Watson goes, sees a man walking fast up the path, finds the letter was a hoax, and climbs back to find only Holmes's Alpine-stock, two sets of footprints leading out and none returning, and a note under a cigarette-case in which Holmes admits he knew the letter was a hoax and let Watson go. Experts conclude the two men went over "locked in each other's arms."

## What the work observes about being human

1. **The same gift in two men can make the law's champion and its enemy, and they recognise each other.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "A criminal strain ran in his blood, which, instead of being modified, was increased and rendered infinitely more dangerous by his extraordinary mental powers." And: "at the end of three months I was forced to confess that I had at last met an antagonist who was my intellectual equal. My horror at his crimes was lost in my admiration at his skill." Moriarty: "It has been an intellectual treat to me to see the way in which you have grappled with this affair."
   - What the action does with it: Each man explains the other by the same move — Holmes deduces what Moriarty will do by asking "What I should do?", and Moriarty reads the pistol in Holmes's dressing-gown pocket and remarks on his "frontal development." Holmes's last note praises Moriarty's methods as confirming "the very high opinion which I had formed of his abilities." They die together in the same water. The story does not say the two are morally alike; it says they are alike in kind, and shows that two such minds can only cancel.

2. **The deepest evil is the one nobody has heard of, and it stays deniable even after it is dead.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "The man pervades London, and no one has heard of him. That's what puts him on a pinnacle in the records of crime." And: "The agent may be caught... But the central power which uses the agent is never caught—never so much as suspected." Watson: "Never."
   - What the action does with it: The police do take the gang — and lose Moriarty: "They have secured the whole gang with the exception of him." The head escapes the law and is stopped only by a cliff. And the frame of the story confirms the point from the other side: the whole account exists because Moriarty's brother is writing letters to clear his name, and "of their terrible chief few details came out during the proceedings." Invisibility survives the man. The world is left with a colonel's letters and a doctor's word.

3. **Recognising danger is not cowardice; refusing to recognise it is stupidity, and Holmes then walks into it on purpose.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "it is stupidity rather than courage to refuse to recognise danger when it is close upon you." Later, in the note: "I was quite convinced that the letter from Meiringen was a hoax, and I allowed you to depart on that errand under the persuasion that some development of this sort would follow."
   - What the action does with it: For most of the story Holmes practises exactly what he preaches: shutters, garden walls, disguises, decoy luggage. Then on a three-foot path he dismisses his only ally, keeps a boy he must have suspected, and waits. The maxim is confirmed and then deliberately set aside, and the story offers no sentence that bridges the two. Watson writes "I had often admired my friend's courage, but never more than now" of the earlier evening; of the last afternoon he can only read footprints.

4. **A life can be weighed and found sufficient by the person living it, and that is not the same as being finished with it.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "I think that I may go so far as to say, Watson, that I have not lived wholly in vain. If my record were closed to-night I could still survey it with equanimity. The air of London is the sweeter for my presence. In over a thousand cases I am not aware that I have ever used my powers upon the wrong side." Earlier, to Moriarty: "if I were assured of the former eventuality I would, in the interests of the public, cheerfully accept the latter."
   - What the action does with it: He accepts it. The note says "no possible conclusion to it could be more congenial to me than this." But Watson records that "Again and again he recurred to the fact" of being willing to end his career, that Holmes arrived "paler and thinner than usual" having "been using myself up rather too freely," and that he spoke of turning from crime to "the problems furnished by nature." The story gives the reader a man at peace and a man repeating himself, and does not say whether the equanimity is contentment or exhaustion. Watson, who felt "a void in my life," is the counterweight the sentence does not include.

5. **Friendship can protect by excluding, and the one excluded is the one who refused to leave.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "No, my friend, you might find me a dangerous guest." And in Strasburg: "I think that you had better return to England, Watson." Watson: "It was hardly an appeal to be successful with one who was an old campaigner as well as an old friend." The note: "at a cost which will give pain to my friends, and especially, my dear Watson, to you."
   - What the action does with it: Watson argues for half an hour and stays. Holmes then removes him by letting a forged letter do what argument could not, and faces Moriarty alone. The friend who would not be sent away is sent away by a trick, and gets his "last word of greeting" on three pages torn from a notebook. The story treats this as love and does not deny that it is also management; Watson's loyalty is honoured in the note and overridden in the plan.

6. **Intelligence does not give control over outcomes; luck and institutions still decide.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes, watching the special pass: "There are limits, you see, to our friend's intelligence. It would have been a coup-de-maître had he deduced what I would deduce and acted accordingly." Two days later: "I might have known it! He has escaped!... Of course, when I had left the country there was no one to cope with him. But I did think that I had put the game in their hands."
   - What the action does with it: The man who mocked his opponent's limits had, that same week, miscalculated his own: he left the capture to the police and the police lost the one man who mattered. The chase is a series of correct predictions — the special, Paris, the luggage — that still ends with the quarry loose on a Swiss path. The story gives the detective every point in the game and the game to no one.

7. **Manners are not morals; the most courteous man in the story is the one who means to kill.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes on Moriarty: "His soft, precise fashion of speech leaves a conviction of sincerity which a mere bully could not produce." Moriarty: "I say, unaffectedly, that it would be a grief to me to be forced to take any extreme measure." The note: "I write these few lines through the courtesy of Mr. Moriarty, who awaits my convenience for the final discussion of those questions which lie between us."
   - What the action does with it: The interview in Baker Street is conducted with a cocked pistol on the table and the forms of a social call ("Pray take a chair. I can spare you five minutes"). The courtesy holds to the end — Moriarty waits while Holmes writes — and then the two men go over the edge "locked in each other's arms," which the experts call a "personal contest." The story shows that civility can accompany murder to the last minute without softening it at all.

8. **Watson claims the absolute truth, and the truth he has is what he could read on the ground.**
   - Where it is spoken: Watson: "I alone know the absolute truth of the matter." Later: "I began to think of Holmes's own methods and to try to practise them in reading this tragedy. It was, alas, only too easy to do." And: "An examination by experts leaves little doubt..." and "there can be no doubt that he was one of the numerous agents whom Moriarty kept in his employ."
   - What the action does with it: Watson was not at the fall. His certainty is built from two lines of footmarks, a ploughed patch of mud, torn ferns, a note, and an inference about a Swiss boy who was "never found again." The pupil applies the master's method to the master's death and arrives at "no doubt" — the very phrase of confidence the story has spent its length showing to be a habit of brilliant men. The narrator who begins by correcting "an absolute perversion of the facts" ends by giving a reading, and calling the man he read "the best and the wisest man whom I have ever known."

## What it teaches
The story is a portrait of two men who are each the only one who can see the other. Holmes alone has deduced Moriarty; Moriarty alone has recognised Holmes as the one obstacle; everyone else — the police, the public, the colonel writing letters afterwards — either does not know or does not believe. The reader is asked to consider what it means to be truly matched: not comforted by it, as Holmes half is ("My horror at his crimes was lost in my admiration at his skill"), but consumed. The story's answer is that such a match ends in a mutual fall, and that the world then goes on as if the greater of the two men had been a slander on a respectable mathematician. What survives of a life lived against invisible evil is a friend's account of it.

The difficulty the story leaves is in Holmes's last choice. He has been careful for a week; he has said that ignoring danger is stupidity; he has a friend who will not leave him. On the path he sends that friend away with a lie and waits for the man who has promised to destroy him. The note calls this congenial. Watson calls Holmes the best and wisest man he ever knew. The story does not tell you whether to read the last afternoon as a sacrifice made "in the interests of the public," as a tired man's chosen exit, or as the one time pride overrode prudence; it gives you all three and the sound of the water. What you are left holding is the plain fact that the person you most admire may decide to face his ending without you, and that the deciding will have been done for your sake and without your consent.

## Lines worth keeping
- "it is stupidity rather than courage to refuse to recognise danger when it is close upon you."
- "The man pervades London, and no one has heard of him."
- "He sits motionless, like a spider in the centre of its web, but that web has a thousand radiations, and he knows well every quiver of each of them."
- "My horror at his crimes was lost in my admiration at his skill."
- "'All that I have to say has already crossed your mind,' said he. 'Then possibly my answer has crossed yours,' I replied."
- "'Danger is part of my trade,' I remarked. 'That is not danger,' said he. 'It is inevitable destruction.'"
- "His soft, precise fashion of speech leaves a conviction of sincerity which a mere bully could not produce."
- "One would think that we were the criminals."
- "There are limits, you see, to our friend's intelligence."
- "I have not lived wholly in vain... The air of London is the sweeter for my presence."
- "listening to the half-human shout which came booming up with the spray out of the abyss."
- "It was, alas, only too easy to do."
- "I allowed you to depart on that errand under the persuasion that some development of this sort would follow."
- "the best and the wisest man whom I have ever known."

## Preoccupations
- the mirror between the detective and the criminal
- evil that is organised, invisible, and deniable
- courage, prudence, and the choice to face death alone
- a life judged sufficient by the one living it
- friendship that protects by deceiving
- the limits of foresight: plans that work and still fail
- the narrator's certainty resting on inference
