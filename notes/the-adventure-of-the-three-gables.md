# The Adventure of the Three Gables

## What happens
A hired bruiser, Steve Dixie, bursts into Baker Street to warn Holmes off "Harrow way"; Holmes cows him by naming a killing Steve was part of. The case is Mrs. Maberley's: a stranger offers to buy her modest house and every object in it, with a contract forbidding her to remove even her own possessions. Her maid Susan is caught eavesdropping and turns out to be married to the gang's leader. Mrs. Maberley's son Douglas, a brilliant diplomat who had become "a worn-out cynical man" after a love affair, has just died in Rome, and his unopened luggage is in the hall. That night the house is burgled, Mrs. Maberley chloroformed, and the trunks ransacked; one page of a manuscript remains, a novel in which a man beaten under a woman's window swears revenge. Holmes goes to Isadora Klein, the beautiful rich widow who had Douglas beaten, who has burned the manuscript to protect her coming marriage to a young duke. Holmes "compounds a felony as usual," extracting a cheque for five thousand pounds so Mrs. Maberley can travel round the world, and lets Klein go.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **A heart can be broken badly enough to change a person's whole nature.**
   - Where it is spoken: Mrs. Maberley: "In a single month I seemed to see my gallant boy turn into a worn-out cynical man." Douglas's page: "It was at that moment that love died and hate was born. Man must live for something. If it is not for your embrace, my lady, then it shall surely be for your undoing and my complete revenge."
   - What the action does with it: The story confirms it twice over. Douglas "lived intensely--every fibre of him," and Holmes "could not connect death with such a man"; yet he dies of pneumonia within months of the beating, and his mother blames the intensity itself: "Too intensely... That was the ruin of him." The revenge he lived for is a manuscript, and the manuscript ends as "a calcined mass" in Klein's grate. His hate outlives him by weeks and achieves nothing; the story gives the broken man no victory.

2. **People who give on their own terms resent being asked to give again.**
   - Where it is spoken: Klein: "Because I had given he seemed to think that I still must give, and to him only. It was intolerable." Holmes on her: "When her caprice is satisfied, the matter is ended, and if the other party in the matter can't take her word for it, she knows how to bring it home to him."
   - What the action does with it: Klein's view is the story's clearest statement of a certain kind of freedom, and the story lets her state it in full. It also shows what "bringing it home" means: hired men beating a lover under her window while she smiles. Holmes's answer is flat: "The original sin was yours." Yet he lets her keep her duke. The plot refuses to punish the view, only to tax it.

3. **Writing is a weapon, and the writer who tells his own story cannot keep himself out of it.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes on the surviving page: "Did you notice how the 'he' suddenly changed to 'my.' The writer was so carried away by his own story that he imagined himself at the supreme moment to be the hero." Klein: "He wrote a book in which he described his own story. I, of course, was the wolf; he the lamb."
   - What the action does with it: Douglas's novel was his revenge, and its being recognizable ("who in all London would have failed to recognize it?") is exactly what made it dangerous and exactly why it was destroyed. The story treats the book as a genuine threat to real power, enough to mobilize a gang and a burglary, and also as the slip of a man who could not stay fictional. Holmes calls it "within his rights."

4. **Money buys silence and muscle, and it can be made to buy restitution too.**
   - Where it is spoken: Susan: "Someone that could lay down a thousand pounds for every ten you have in the world." Klein: "They will take what comes to them. That is what they are paid for. I shall not appear in the matter." Holmes: "I suppose I shall have to compound a felony as usual. How much does it cost to go round the world in first-class style?"
   - What the action does with it: Klein's wealth hires Barney, Susan, and Steve; the gang will go to prison and she will not. Holmes does not reverse this. He prices her guilt at five thousand pounds and gives it to the victim. The story is honest that justice here is a transaction, that the rich woman keeps everything but a cheque, and that the poor hired hands pay with their liberty.

5. **The hired bully is also somebody's instrument and is frightened for his own skin.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "he is really rather a harmless fellow, a great muscular, foolish, blustering baby, and easily cowed." Steve: "My boss Barney gives me orders and that's all." Holmes: "Such hounds have a way sooner or later of biting the hand that feeds them."
   - What the action does with it: Steve arrives as menace and leaves as informant within a page; Holmes turns him with one old killing. The story shows how thin intimidation is when the intimidator has his own secrets. But Klein corrects Holmes's prediction: her hounds "run silent," and the story gives no sign they will bite her. Holmes's claim about betrayal stays unproven.

6. **Beauty and age sit uneasily together, and the proud try to control the light.**
   - Where it is spoken: Watson: "The lady had come, I felt, to that time of life when even the proudest beauty finds the half-light more welcome." Holmes: "the young Duke of Lomond, who might almost be her son."
   - What the action does with it: Klein's whole crime is in defence of a last marriage before beauty fades: "a woman who sees all her life's ambition about to be ruined at the last moment." Watson is half-seduced ("I felt of all Holmes's criminals this was the one whom he would find it hardest to face"), then reassures himself Holmes "was immune from sentiment." The story lets her charm work on the narrator even as it reports her ruthlessness.

7. **Being a gentleman is a lever others use to move you.**
   - Where it is spoken: Klein: "You have the feelings of a gentleman. How quick a woman's instinct is to find it out." And: "No, no; you would not. You are a gentleman. It is a woman's secret."
   - What the action does with it: She is right. Holmes announces "I am not the law, but I represent justice so far as my feeble powers go," and then does precisely what she predicts: he does not bring her in. His parting words are a warning, not a consequence. The story shows the code of gentlemanly discretion functioning as a shield for the powerful, and Holmes knowing it and complying anyway.

## What it teaches
This story asks what the dead are owed. Douglas Maberley loved wholly, was beaten for it, wrote his revenge, and died before he could publish it. Everything that follows is a fight over his last word, and his last word loses. His mother gets a trip round the world. The woman who had him beaten gets her duke. Holmes gets a cheque signed. The gang, who had the least to gain, get the police. It is hard to read the ending as justice, and Watson does not call it that; the story's own phrase is "compound a felony as usual," with the "as usual" doing a lot of work. What the story seems to know is that in a world of hired hands and rich principals, the detective's power is to redistribute a little, not to right much.

The harder lesson is in Klein's speech. She asks Holmes to "look at it with my eyes": a woman whose beauty is her only capital, near its end, defending the one marriage that will secure her. The story gives her the floor and does not sneer. It also shows that her freedom to give and withdraw love at will was purchased with another person's life. Neither view cancels the other. The reader is left holding both, and holding Holmes's final warning, which is only a warning.

## Lines worth keeping
- "He lived intensely--every fibre of him!" "Too intensely, Mr. Holmes. That was the ruin of him."
- "In a single month I seemed to see my gallant boy turn into a worn-out cynical man."
- "when people bury treasure nowadays they do it in the Post Office bank. But there are always some lunatics about. It would be a dull world without them."
- "there is something which you do not know that you have, and which you would not give up if you did know."
- "It was at that moment that love died and hate was born. Man must live for something."
- "The writer was so carried away by his own story that he imagined himself at the supreme moment to be the hero."
- "Surely no man would take up my profession if it were not that danger attracts him."
- "I am not the law, but I represent justice so far as my feeble powers go."
- "They are good hounds who run silent."
- "Because I had given he seemed to think that I still must give, and to him only. It was intolerable."
- "The original sin was yours."
- "You can't play with edged tools for ever without cutting those dainty hands."

## Preoccupations
- love turning to hate, and the cost of loving too hard
- money buying muscle, silence, and escape from consequences
- writing as revenge and as self-exposure
- justice outside the law, settled by bargain
- fading beauty and ambition
- gentlemanly discretion as a shield for the powerful
