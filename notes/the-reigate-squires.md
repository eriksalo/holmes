# The Reigate Squires

## What happens
Holmes, collapsed in a Lyons hotel with "the blackest depression" after two months of triumphant overwork, is taken by Watson to convalesce at Colonel Hayter's house near Reigate. A neighbouring magnate, Acton, has just been burgled of an absurd haul — an odd Pope's Homer, candlesticks, a ball of twine — and the next morning the coachman of the other great house, the Cunninghams', is found shot dead at midnight with a torn corner of a note in his hand reading "at quarter to twelve ... learn what ... maybe." Father and son Cunningham say they saw a stranger shoot him and flee over the hedge. Holmes, revived instantly by the problem, reads the scrap as written alternately by two hands — a young one and an old one, blood-relatives, one distrusting the other — then stages a fake fit to stop the Inspector mentioning the note, tricks old Cunningham into writing "twelve" on a reward form, knocks over a table and blames Watson so he can search Alec's dressing-gown, and is half-throttled by the two squires before the rest of the note is recovered. Old Cunningham confesses: the two had burgled Acton's library seeking a paper in a ruinous lawsuit, the coachman William had followed them and begun to blackmail them, and the son decoyed him with a note promising news "of the greatest service to you and also to Annie Morrison" and shot him, using the burglary scare as cover. Holmes declares his rest cure a complete success.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **Success and praise do not touch depression; only the next problem does.**
   - Where it is spoken: Watson: "at a time when Europe was ringing with his name and when his room was literally ankle-deep with congratulatory telegrams I found him a prey to the blackest depression." And: "You are here for a rest, my dear fellow. For Heaven's sake don't get started on a new problem when your nerves are all in shreds."
   - What the action does with it: Watson's medical caution is "wasted" within a day. The instant Holmes studies the torn scrap, "his cheek was tinged with colour, and his eyes as bright as before his illness." The story ends with Holmes, half-strangled, declaring "our quiet rest in the country has been a distinct success." The treatment that works is the thing the doctor forbade, and the story does not resolve whether this is health or a deeper form of the disease; it shows that for this man the two are the same.

2. **The investigator's worst enemy is the assumption that respectable people are not involved.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "the Inspector had overlooked it because he had started with the supposition that these county magnates had had nothing to do with the matter. Now, I make a point of never having any prejudices, and of following docilely wherever fact may lead me."
   - What the action does with it: The Colonel vouches for old Cunningham as "our leading man about here... and a very decent fellow too," and for the coachman as "a very faithful fellow." Both characterisations are wrong: the J.P. and his son are murderers, and the faithful coachman was a blackmailer. Holmes also confesses his own lapse — he had thought Reigate "the last parish in England to which the thief or thieves would be likely to turn their attention—which shows that I have still much to learn." The story punishes the assumption of innocence by class in both directions.

3. **Accomplices who are family still do not trust each other.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes on the alternating handwriting: "Obviously the business was a bad one, and one of the men who distrusted the other was determined that, whatever was done, each should have an equal hand in it." And: "They belong to men who are blood-relatives... a family mannerism can be traced."
   - What the action does with it: The note is physical evidence of a father and son who will commit murder together but not sign for it separately. When caught, the old man is "tractable" and confesses; the son is "a perfect demon, ready to blow out his own or anybody else's brains." The same blood in the handwriting produces two different men, and the one who wrote his words first — the planner — is the young one. The story treats heredity as legible in the hand and inadequate as a guide to character.

4. **Deception, including the exploitation of a friend's sympathy, is a legitimate tool, and the story is cheerful about it.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "It is an art which is often useful." Watson: "Speaking professionally, it was admirably done." And earlier Holmes to the Cunninghams: "I am liable to these sudden nervous attacks."
   - What the action does with it: Holmes fakes a seizure, lets Watson suffer "sympathetic pain," deliberately writes a wrong hour so the magistrate will correct it in his own hand, smashes a carafe and says "You've done it now, Watson." Every trick works. The Inspector mutters that "some folks might say there was madness in his method." The story never suggests Holmes should feel anything about using his friend's worry as a stage property; the only cost it registers is Watson's rueful "Oh, what an ass I have been!"

5. **A servant who learns his masters' secret has acquired a weapon that will be used on him.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "William had secretly followed his two masters on the night when they made their raid upon Mr. Acton's, and having thus got them into his power, proceeded, under threats of exposure, to levy blackmail upon them. Mr. Alec, however, was a dangerous man to play games of that sort with."
   - What the action does with it: The dead man, mourned as faithful, had been living off what he knew. The story's chain is explicit: a lawsuit that "sucked the blood out of both of them" leads to a burglary, which creates a blackmailer, who is murdered under cover of the burglary scare he himself witnessed. Each party's greed supplies the next party's opportunity. Nobody in the chain is innocent, including the victim.

6. **A trap is baited with what the victim cares about, and the story never tells us what that was.**
   - Where it is spoken: The recovered note: "you will learn what will very much surprise you and maybe be of the greatest service to you and also to Annie Morrison. But say nothing to anyone upon the matter." Holmes: "we do not yet know what the relations may have been between Alec Cunningham, William Kirwan, and Annie Morrison. The results shows that the trap was skillfully baited."
   - What the action does with it: A man was lured to his death at midnight by a promise concerning a woman, and the story closes without saying who she was or what he hoped. Holmes passes straight from this to admiring "the traces of heredity shown in the p's." The reader is left holding the one line in the case about a human attachment, unexplained, while the detective delights in penmanship.

7. **Faces confess when the mask is dropped suddenly.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "Tut, man, look at their faces!" Watson: "Never, certainly, have I seen a plainer confession of guilt upon human countenances." Holmes later: "the sudden change from absolute security to complete despair made them perfectly desperate."
   - What the action does with it: Alec Cunningham spends the story needling Holmes — "I thought you Londoners were never at fault" — in a "jaunty, dashing style" with "showy dress"; in a second it becomes "the ferocity of a dangerous wild beast." The story suggests that confident people are most dangerous at the instant their confidence breaks, and that guilt is legible not in steady deceit but in the transition.

8. **Detection is the discipline of knowing which facts matter.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "It is of the highest importance in the art of detection to be able to recognise, out of a number of facts, which are incidental and which vital. Otherwise your energy and attention must be dissipated instead of being concentrated."
   - What the action does with it: The "extraordinary assortment" stolen from Acton — Homer, twine, a barometer — is what tells Holmes the burglary was staged, and the scrap of paper is the one fact he never lets go of. The Inspector has every fact Holmes has and misses the case because he weighs them by the status of the people involved. The story makes the method a matter of attention rather than information.

## What it teaches
The story's surface is a conjuring act — the fake fit, the knocked-over oranges, the forged mistake on the reward form — and it is a pleasure to watch. Underneath is a harder observation about the social world: a county's whole arrangement of trust (the magistrate, the faithful servant, the decent neighbour) turns out to be a set of masks over a lawsuit, a burglary, a blackmail, and a murder, and the only person who sees through it is the outsider who makes a rule of having no prejudices and admits he has them anyway. A thoughtful reader takes away not that respectable people are secretly wicked but that respectability is simply not evidence, and that the presumption of innocence extended to the comfortable is just another form of bias.

What the story leaves hard is its own cheerfulness. Holmes recovers his health by the exposure of two murderers; Watson is used as a prop and takes it as a compliment; the dead coachman was a blackmailer and so his death is filed without much feeling; and Annie Morrison — the name that drew a man out of his bed at midnight — is dropped without explanation while the detective savours the tails of the g's. The story knows exactly what it is doing and does not apologise. If there is a lesson, it is that the cure for Holmes's depression is other people's catastrophe, and the story is honest enough to let him say so: "I shall certainly return much invigorated."

- "this was probably the last parish in England to which the thief or thieves would be likely to turn their attention—which shows that I have still much to learn."
- "they've had a lawsuit for some years which has sucked the blood out of both of them"
- "I have usually found that there was method in his madness." "Some folks might say there was madness in his method"
- "I thought you Londoners were never at fault. You don't seem to be so very quick, after all."

- "one of the men who distrusted the other was determined that, whatever was done, each should have an equal hand in it."

## Preoccupations
- respectability as a mask, and the bias toward the comfortable
- work as the only cure for despair
- blackmail breeding murder
- distrust within a family of accomplices
- deception as the detective's legitimate tool
- the detail about love that goes unexplained
