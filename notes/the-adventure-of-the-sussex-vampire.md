# The Adventure of the Sussex Vampire

## What happens
A firm of machinery assessors forwards an inquiry "Re Vampires" to Holmes. Robert Ferguson, an old rugby opponent of Watson's, writes that his Peruvian wife has twice beaten his crippled fifteen-year-old son Jack from a first marriage, and has been seen twice with her mouth at the neck of their own baby, blood on her lips. She has locked herself in her room and will not see her husband. Holmes, who has solved the case before leaving Baker Street, goes to the old Sussex farmhouse, notes the South American weapons on the wall with an empty quiver beside a small bow, notes a spaniel that was suddenly half-paralysed four months earlier, and watches Jack's face in a window-glass while the father fondles the baby. He then explains to the husband, in the wife's presence: Jack has been shooting poisoned darts at the baby out of jealous, devouring love for his father; the mother has been sucking the poison out and beating the boy, and would not say so because she knew it would break her husband's heart. Holmes prescribes "a year at sea" for Jack and leaves the couple to themselves.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **Love can be so exclusive that it turns murderous toward whatever shares its object.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "It is the more painful because it is a distorted love, a maniacal exaggerated love for you, and possibly for his dead mother, which has prompted his action. His very soul is consumed with hatred for this splendid child, whose health and beauty are a contrast to his own weakness."
   - What the action does with it: The story shows the love before it names the hate. Jack "threw his arms round his neck with the abandon of a loving girl"; Ferguson says "My life is his life. He is absorbed in what I say or do." The same embrace, reflected in a window, shows "such jealousy, such cruel hatred, as I have seldom seen in a human face." The plot never separates the two feelings; the boy's devotion and his attempt to kill the baby are one thing.

2. **Appearances accuse the innocent; what looks like the crime is the rescue.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "Did it not occur to you that a bleeding wound may be sucked for some other purpose than to draw the blood from it?"
   - What the action does with it: Every witness (nurse, husband, Watson reading the letter) sees the same image and reads it the same way: "It was she--she beyond all question--who had drunk the poor baby's blood." The action reverses the reading completely. The only fact that was wrong was the interpretation. And the woman's "savage" beatings of Jack, which looked like the cruelty of a stepmother, were a mother's fury at her child's attacker: "There is a limit to a mother's patience."

3. **The foreigner is blamed first, and the explanation reached for is the exotic one.**
   - Where it is spoken: Ferguson's letter: "the fact of her foreign birth and of her alien religion always caused a separation of interests and of feelings between husband and wife... He felt there were sides of her character which he could never explore or understand." And: "Is it madness, Mr. Holmes? Is it something in the blood?"
   - What the action does with it: The husband and nurse leap to vampirism, a "wild tale of foreign parts," while the true cause is an English boy with an English grievance. The weapons are Peruvian, but the hand on the bow is Jack's. The story quietly indicts the husband's readiness to believe the worst of a wife he admits he never knew ("I had only known her a few weeks"), and Holmes's one sharp question makes the point: "Then your wife's character would really be better known by Dolores than by you?"

4. **Love that will not ask for trust, and love that will not give it, can both be forms of silence that nearly destroy a marriage.**
   - Where it is spoken: The wife to Watson: "Do I not love him even to sacrifice myself rather than break his dear heart. That is how I love him. And yet he could think of me--he could speak of me so... No, he cannot understand. But he should trust."
   - What the action does with it: She chooses to be thought a monster rather than tell him his son is one. He chooses to believe his eyes rather than his wife. The story honours her sacrifice and also shows it as a mistake: "All is destroyed. Do what I will, all is destroyed." Only a third party's note ("that he knew all") can break the deadlock. Neither spouse could speak the truth to the other; it had to arrive from outside.

5. **The weak and overlooked are capable of the greatest harm, and the strong find it hardest to see.**
   - Where it is spoken: Ferguson: "You would think that the dear lad's condition would soften anyone's heart... But the dearest, most loving heart within." Holmes: "the boy--he is fifteen, I understand, and probably very developed in mind, since his body has been circumscribed in action."
   - What the action does with it: Ferguson cannot imagine "poor little inoffensive cripple" as a threat; Holmes's first notes concern the boy. The story confirms the detective's suspicion and the father's blindness, and it is careful to make the blindness a form of love ("Luckily I am one of his likes"). The dog, Carlo, has been paralysed for four months as a test of the poison, under everyone's eyes.

6. **The rational mind that refuses the supernatural can still be surprised by the natural.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "This Agency stands flat-footed upon the ground, and there it must remain. The world is big enough for us. No ghosts need apply." And Watson's suggestion: "the vampire was not necessarily a dead man? A living person might have the habit."
   - What the action does with it: Holmes's refusal is vindicated: there is no vampire. But Watson's half-joke proves nearer the mark than either expected: a living person in the house has been drawing blood from the child, and another living person has been putting her mouth to the wound. The story's rationalism is confirmed and also shown to be less comforting than it sounds: what people do to each other is as dark as the folklore.

7. **The confident solver can wound even while curing; the "swiftest surgery" is still surgery.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "I fear that there is pain for you, Mr. Ferguson, whatever the solution may be." And: "The swiftest surgery is the least painful... I will do so, but in doing so I must wound you deeply in another direction."
   - What the action does with it: Ferguson's protest ("It may be a mere intellectual puzzle to you, but it is life and death to me!") lands, and Holmes's handling afterwards is gentle: he arranges the scene so the wife hears herself cleared before the father hears his son accused. Yet the ending leaves Ferguson "choking, his hands outstretched and quivering," and Holmes's prescription for the boy, "a year at sea," is brisk to the point of coldness. The story withdraws before anyone speaks of what to do with Jack.

## What it teaches
The story's whole energy is in the gap between what is seen and what it means. A woman with blood on her mouth; a crippled boy clinging to his father; a stepmother striking a child. Each image is true, and each reading of it is wrong. The lesson is not merely "appearances deceive"; it is that the readings people choose reveal them. Ferguson reaches for "something in the blood" because his wife is foreign and he never understood her. He cannot reach for his son because he loves him. The nurse reaches for vampires because she loves the baby. Everyone's error is an expression of what they love and fear, and the person who sees clearly is the one who loves no one in the house.

What the story leaves unresolved is Jack. He is fifteen, deformed, consumed by a love his father gently disengages from twice in one scene, and he has tried to kill an infant more than once. Holmes says "a year at sea" and walks out, and the narrative closes with a business letter about a "satisfactory conclusion." The marriage may be saved; the boy is handed off. A thoughtful reader may feel that the detective has solved the puzzle and left the human problem exactly where he found it, locked in a room with a father who still has to decide which child he will protect.

## Lines worth keeping
- "For a mixture of the modern and the mediæval, of the practical and of the wildly fanciful, I think this is surely the limit."
- "This Agency stands flat-footed upon the ground, and there it must remain. The world is big enough for us. No ghosts need apply."
- "There is surely nothing in life more painful than to meet the wreck of a fine athlete whom one has known in his prime."
- "He felt there were sides of her character which he could never explore or understand."
- "day and night the silent, watchful mother seemed to be lying in wait as a wolf waits for a lamb."
- "One forms provisional theories and waits for time or fuller knowledge to explode them. A bad habit, Mr. Ferguson; but human nature is weak."
- "It may be a mere intellectual puzzle to you, but it is life and death to me!"
- "Do I not love him even to sacrifice myself rather than break his dear heart. That is how I love him."
- "No, he cannot understand. But he should trust."
- "I want my child. I have a right to my child."
- "The swiftest surgery is the least painful."
- "There is a limit to a mother's patience."

## Preoccupations
- jealousy born of love
- the gap between what is seen and what it means
- suspicion of the foreigner and the stranger in the house
- silence chosen as sacrifice, and its cost
- the overlooked weak as the source of harm
- the rational explanation being darker than the superstition
