# The Sign of the Four

## What happens
Holmes, idle and injecting cocaine three times a day, is rescued from "the dull routine of existence" by Mary Morstan, a governess whose father, an officer from the Andaman Islands convict settlement, vanished in London ten years before; since 1882 she has received a pearl every year from an unknown sender, and now a letter calling her "a wronged woman" summons her to a meeting. The sender is Thaddeus Sholto, who tells her that his father, Major Sholto, confessed on his deathbed that Captain Morstan died of a heart attack during a quarrel over a treasure the two had brought from India, that he hid the body and kept the whole hoard out of greed, and that he died before he could say where it was hidden, terrified by a bearded face at the window. Thaddeus's twin brother Bartholomew has just found the Agra treasure in a sealed garret — and when the party reaches Pondicherry Lodge he is dead in a locked room, a poisoned thorn in his scalp and the treasure gone. Inspector Athelney Jones arrests Thaddeus and the servants; Holmes follows a creosote scent with the dog Toby, sets his street boys hunting a steam launch, goes down the river in disguise, and finally runs the Aurora down in a night chase, shooting its passenger, the Andaman islander Tonga, and dragging the one-legged Jonathan Small out of the mud. Watson carries the iron box to Mary, breaks it open, and finds it empty; freed of the fortune that stood between them, he proposes and is accepted. Small then tells his story — the Indian Mutiny, the murder of the merchant Achmet at Agra fort, the oath of the four, Sholto's betrayal, twenty years in the convict barracks, escape with Tonga, and the long wait for the treasure to surface — and explains that he scattered the jewels in the Thames rather than let them go to the kin of the men who robbed him. Jones takes the credit, Watson takes a wife, and Holmes reaches for the cocaine bottle.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **A mind that cannot bear stagnation will poison itself rather than be still.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes, ch. I: "My mind rebels at stagnation. Give me problems, give me work... I abhor the dull routine of existence. I crave for mental exaltation." And: "I cannot live without brain-work. What else is there to live for?"
   - What the action does with it: The case does what the needle does — Holmes becomes "bright, eager, and in excellent spirits," then, balked for one day by a hidden launch, is found by Mrs. Hudson walking "up and down, and up and down" through the night, "worn and haggard, with a little fleck of feverish colour upon either cheek" (ch. IX). The last line of the book is "For me, there still remains the cocaine-bottle." The novel is built as a circle: the work is a stronger drug than the drug, and when it is gone the drug returns. Holmes states his need as a fact of nature; the action shows it as a captivity.

2. **The doctrine that feeling spoils judgment is held most firmly by a man who is ruled by moods.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes, ch. II: "A client is to me a mere unit,—a factor in a problem. The emotional qualities are antagonistic to clear reasoning." Ch. XII: "love is an emotional thing, and whatever is emotional is opposed to that true cold reason which I place above all things. I should never marry myself, lest I bias my judgment."
   - What the action does with it: Watson, who falls in love on the first evening, nonetheless fetches Toby, holds the fort at Baker Street, fires at Tonga, and misses nothing he is asked to see. Mary, the emotional "unit," is praised by Holmes himself for "a decided genius" in preserving the Agra chart. Meanwhile the apostle of cold reason swings between "nervous exaltation" and "the blackest depression," cannot sleep when thwarted, and is kind to everyone — a hand on Thaddeus's shoulder, a flask for the wet prisoner. Watson tries the doctrine on himself in ch. II ("She was a unit, a factor,—nothing more") and cannot hold it for a paragraph. The work does not refute Holmes's claim so much as show it to be a description of a man who has displaced his feeling, not escaped it.

3. **Avarice is blind: the feeling of possession matters more than any use the possession could have.**
   - Where it is spoken: Major Sholto's deathbed, ch. IV: "The cursed greed which has been my besetting sin through life has withheld from her the treasure... And yet I have made no use of it myself,—so blind and foolish a thing is avarice. The mere feeling of possession has been so dear to me that I could not bear to share it with another." He cannot even release a chaplet of pearls: "send her nothing... until I am gone. After all, men have been as bad as this and have recovered."
   - What the action does with it: Nobody in the book ever spends a rupee of the Agra treasure. Sholto hoards it and dies before naming the hiding place; his sons dig for six years until the grounds "look like a gravel-pit"; Bartholomew, "a little inclined to my father's fault," finds it and is dead within a day; Small, who waited twenty years, pours it into the Thames because "if I can't have the loot I'll take darned good care that no one else does." Small's own summary (ch. XI): "To him it brought murder, to Major Sholto it brought fear and guilt, to me it has meant slavery for life." The work confirms the Major's deathbed insight with every subsequent event.

4. **A fortune is a wall between people as readily as a bridge.**
   - Where it is spoken: Watson, ch. IV, on hearing the treasure may be worth half a million: "selfishness took me by the soul, and... my heart turned as heavy as lead within me." Ch. VII: "This Agra treasure intervened like an impassable barrier between us."
   - What the action does with it: The one unambiguously good thing the treasure does is vanish. "The box was empty!... a great shadow seemed to pass from my soul... 'Thank God!' I ejaculated from my very heart" (ch. XI). Mary, told she may be the richest heiress in England, "gave a toss of her proud head, as though the matter were one in which she took small interest" and worries only about clearing Thaddeus. The inspector in the cab gives the counter-voice: "Where there is no money there is no pay." The book is unusual in letting its romance be rewarded by loss and its officers be disappointed by it.

5. **Concealing a death you did not cause makes you as guilty as if you had.**
   - Where it is spoken: Major Sholto, ch. IV, on Morstan's heart attack: "If my own servant could not believe my innocence, how could I hope to make it good before twelve foolish tradesmen in a jury-box?" And then: "My fault lies in the fact that we concealed not only the body, but also the treasure."
   - What the action does with it: The Major probably did not kill his friend — the "difference of opinion" is interrupted by a seizure, not a blow. But hiding the body commits him to hiding the treasure, and hiding the treasure to a decade of fear: two prize-fighters at the gate, a revolver fired at a harmless one-legged tradesman, a death at the sight of a face at the window. Lal Chowdar's smiling "Let us hide him away, and who is the wiser?" is the moment a possibly innocent man chooses to live as a guilty one. The story shows the cover-up costing more than the truth could have.

6. **When life around a man is cheap, he will find his own conscience cheap too — and will say so.**
   - Where it is spoken: Small, ch. XII: "In Worcestershire the life of a man seems a great and a sacred thing; but it is very different when there is fire and blood all round you and you have been used to meeting death at every turn." Abdullah Khan's inducement: "We only ask you to do that which your countrymen come to this land for. We ask you to be rich."
   - What the action does with it: Small's heart "softened" twice toward the fat, frightened Achmet, and twice "the thought of the treasure turned me hard and bitter"; he trips the running man with his musket. Watson "conceived the utmost horror of the man, not only for this cold-blooded business... but even more for the somewhat flippant and careless way in which he narrated it." Holmes says only, "Go on with your story." Small's defense — "I should like to know how many fellows in my shoes would have refused a share of this loot when they knew that they would have their throats cut" — is left standing. So is Khan's sentence about what Englishmen come to India for. The work records both the crime and the frame that excuses it, and does not adjudicate.

7. **The oath of convicts holds where the honour of officers breaks.**
   - Where it is spoken: Small, ch. XII: "It's been the sign of four with us always." To Sholto: "None or all... We have sworn it. The four of us must always act together." "Black or blue, they are in with me, and we all go together." Sholto's reply: "What have three black fellows to do with our agreement?" Morstan: "It's a dirty business... Yet, as you say, the money would save our commissions handsomely."
   - What the action does with it: The two gentlemen take the chart and the Major steals everything "without carrying out one of the conditions on which we had sold him the secret." The murderer keeps his word for twenty years and throws half a million into a river for three men in a convict barracks who will never know. The work makes the only kept promise in the book belong to the most disreputable man in it. But the text also shows the edges of Small's loyalty: he exhibits "poor Tonga at fairs... as the black cannibal," "welted the little devil with the slack end of the rope," and leaves him to drown. His faith is a master's faith, and the story does not notice the contradiction even as it writes it down.

8. **Everyone in the chain of a stolen thing believes he has earned it, and justice means whatever gives it to him.**
   - Where it is spoken: Small, ch. XII: "Justice! A pretty justice! Whose loot is this, if it is not ours? Where is the justice that I should give it up to those who have never earned it? Look how I have earned it! Twenty long years in that fever-ridden swamp." Jones: "If you had helped justice, instead of thwarting it." Holmes: "we cannot tell how far justice may originally have been on your side."
   - What the action does with it: Trace the title: a rajah hoards it; Achmet carries it and is murdered; four men swear over it; two officers buy it with a promise and one steals it; two sons dig for it; Small scatters it. Mary, the "wronged woman" who "shall have justice" according to Thaddeus's letter, is wronged only of a share in loot. No claim in the book is clean, and the "justice" promised in chapter II turns out to be the dispersal of the thing itself. Holmes's one line is the only honest position the work allows: nobody can tell.

9. **The one who does the work does not get the credit, and says he does not want it.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes, ch. I: "I claim no credit in such cases. My name figures in no newspaper. The work itself, the pleasure of finding a field for my peculiar powers, is my highest reward." Ch. IX, to Jones: "You are welcome to all the official credit."
   - What the action does with it: The Standard praises Jones's "well-known technical knowledge and his powers of minute observation" for arresting four innocent people; Jones himself, humbled, says Holmes "would have made a most promising officer." Watson's closing arithmetic — "You have done all the work in this business. I get a wife out of it, Jones gets the credit, pray what remains for you?" — gets the answer "the cocaine-bottle." Holmes's stated indifference may be true, but the book sets the needle directly beside it, and lets you decide whether the reward he names is the one he lives on.

10. **The individual is an insoluble puzzle; the crowd is a sum — and the detective's whole art depends on the first part being false.**
    - Where it is spoken: Holmes, ch. X, watching dock workers: "Dirty-looking rascals, but I suppose every one has some little immortal spark concealed about him... A strange enigma is man!" Then, from Winwood Reade: "while the individual man is an insoluble puzzle, in the aggregate he becomes a mathematical certainty. You can, for example, never foretell what any one man will do."
    - What the action does with it: Minutes earlier Holmes has explained that he found the launch by doing exactly what he says cannot be done: "I then put myself in the place of Small, and looked at it as a man of his capacity would... I wondered what I should do myself if I were in his shoes." The case is solved by foretelling what one man will do. And the "immortal spark" granted to the dirty workmen is nowhere extended to Tonga, "the unhallowed dwarf," shot on sight and left at "the bottom of the Thames." The work's generosity about human nature stops at a line the work never draws out loud.

11. **Vanity survives in the most disciplined mind, and shows itself as contempt for the thing it secretly wants.**
    - Where it is spoken: Watson, ch. I: "a small vanity underlay my companion's quiet and didactic manner." Holmes on Watson's book: "You have attempted to tinge it with romanticism, which produces much the same effect as if you worked a love-story or an elopement into the fifth proposition of Euclid."
    - What the action does with it: The man who disdains romance works through theatre — a sailor's rig, a false beard, "the proper workhouse cough" — and Jones tells him, "You would have made an actor, and a rare one." He announces "I don't wish to be theatrical" in the middle of a bravura reconstruction. And the book Watson writes about this case is, again, a love-story, and Holmes is again the one who objects. The work treats this as a comic constant rather than a fault: the great reasoner's one unreasoned spot is himself.

## What it teaches

This is a book about what people live for, and it gives four answers and tests each. Holmes lives for the problem, and the book shows the problem running out. Small lives for revenge and his share, and gets a cell at Dartmoor and the satisfaction of having drowned the thing he waited twenty years for. The Sholtos live for possession, and die of it. Watson lives for Mary, and his is the only hunger the book feeds — but notice that it is fed by an empty box. The story is severe about the treasure in a way it is not about anything else: not one person is made better by so much as touching it, and the plot's one happy turn is its disappearance. If the book has a plain lesson it is the Major's own, delivered too late to help him: that the feeling of having is not the same as having, and that a man can hoard for years what he will never use and never be able to give away.

What is harder to settle is what the book thinks of Small. It gives him the longest speech, the only kept oath, and a grievance that is real: a sentence of death for a murder he assisted, twenty years of chains, and a theft by a gentleman who answered "What have three black fellows to do with our agreement?" It also lets him tell, with a flippancy that sickens Watson, how he tripped a terrified man so a knife could reach him, and lets Khan say that what the four did at Agra is only "that which your countrymen come to this land for." The novel records this sentence and never answers it. It is content to show a chain of theft running from a rajah's vault to a London garret, with an Englishman at every link, and then to shoot the one person in the chain who was never promised a share. A thoughtful reader will feel the book's sympathy run freely to Small and stop dead at Tonga, and will have to decide whether that is the author's blindness or the book's honesty about where sympathy actually stops.

The quieter difficulty is Holmes. He is at his most human here — kind to a frightened hypochondriac, generous to a prisoner, delighted by a dawn — and he is also a man who, having just saved an innocent from the gallows and recovered the truth of two deaths, finds nothing in it to keep him. "What else is there to live for?" is asked as a rhetorical question in chapter I, and the last line is the answer. The book does not ask you to pity him, and it does not let you admire him without cost. It shows a mind of the first order that has arranged its life so that only crisis can satisfy it, and then it shows the crisis ending.

- "Crime is commonplace, existence is commonplace, and no qualities save those which are commonplace have any function upon earth." (ch. I)
- "A client is to me a mere unit,—a factor in a problem. The emotional qualities are antagonistic to clear reasoning." (ch. II)
- "the most winning woman I ever knew was hanged for poisoning three little children for their insurance-money, and the most repellant man of my acquaintance is a philanthropist." (ch. II)
- "Like all human kind, they flitted from the gloom into the light, and so back into the gloom once more." (ch. III)
- "so blind and foolish a thing is avarice. The mere feeling of possession has been so dear to me that I could not bear to share it with another." (Major Sholto, ch. IV)

- "A wondrous subtle thing is love, for here were we two who had never seen each other before that day... and yet now in an hour of trouble our hands instinctively sought for each other." (ch. V)

## Preoccupations

- greed and the curse of possession
- justice outside the law, and who has "earned" a thing
- reason against feeling, and whether a mind can be free of emotion
- who does the work versus who gets the credit
- oaths kept and broken, and loyalty across class and race
- the restless mind and what it does when idle
- the loot of empire coming home and what it costs there
- wealth as a barrier between people
- concealment of a wrong making a worse one
