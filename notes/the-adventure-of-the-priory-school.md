# The Adventure of the Priory School

## What happens
Dr. Thorneycroft Huxtable, founder of "the best and most select preparatory school in England," collapses on Holmes's hearth-rug: the ten-year-old Lord Saltire, only legitimate son of the Duke of Holdernesse, has vanished from the school by night, as has the German master Heidegger and his bicycle, and three days have been wasted on a false clue because the Duke wanted no scandal. At the school Holmes meets the Duke, cold and immense, and his sharp young secretary James Wilder, who resents the detective's presence. Holmes maps the moor, finds two bicycle tracks, and follows the German's to his corpse, skull crushed, miles from the school. Cow tracks appear everywhere, but no cows. At a squalid inn, the Fighting Cock, Holmes fakes a sprained ankle, finds horses newly shod with old shoes, and realises the "cows" are horses in medieval cloven-foot shoes dug from the Duke's own moat. From a hillside he watches Wilder arrive at the inn on a patched Dunlop tyre and the Duke himself slip in after dark. Next morning Holmes demands his six-thousand-pound cheque and accuses the Duke to his face. The Duke confesses: Wilder is his illegitimate son by the one woman he loved, kept near him for her face's sake, jealous of the heir, and he lured the boy away with a forged note in his mother's name; the innkeeper Hayes killed the German who followed. The Duke, knowing all, left the child at the inn for three more days to give the murderer time to escape. Holmes sends the carriage for the boy, agrees to let the police believe it was a simple ransom kidnapping, extracts the banishment of Wilder and a letter of reconciliation to the Duchess, and pockets the cheque.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **A parent can love one child in a way that endangers another, and the love may be for a dead woman's face more than for the living son.** The Duke keeps Wilder because he resembles his mother.
   - *Where it is spoken:* "I answer that it was because I could see his mother's face in his, and that for her dear sake there was no end to my long-suffering. All her pretty ways too—there was not one of them which he could not suggest and bring back to my memory. I could not send him away."
   - *What the action does with it:* The story makes the consequence exact: because Wilder cannot be sent away, the legitimate heir is sent away instead ("I dispatched him for safety to Dr. Huxtable's school"), and then kidnapped from there. Holmes names the result without softening: "To humour your guilty elder son, you have exposed your innocent younger son to imminent and unnecessary danger." The Duke's grief for his lost love is real and the text does not mock it; it shows what it costs the child who did not inspire it.

2. **Shame about scandal can outweigh a child's safety, and the powerful are especially prone to it.** The Duke wastes three days, then three more, to keep his family's affairs private.
   - *Where it is spoken:* Huxtable: "His Grace was extremely desirous to avoid all public scandal. He was afraid of his family unhappiness being dragged before the world." Holmes: "So that three days have been wasted. The affair has been most deplorably handled." Wilder, speaking for the Duke: "He prefers to take as few people as possible into his confidence."
   - *What the action does with it:* The delay costs a life (the German master dies the first night, and his body lies on the moor for days) and prolongs the boy's captivity. Then, knowing exactly where his son is and who has him, the Duke leaves him there "under solemn promises" so that a murderer may flee and his bastard's name stay clean. The story confirms the observation in the harshest possible way and lets Holmes say it: "What are promises to such people as these?"

3. **A man who takes a view he calls "moral" is willing to trade it for money and discretion when the arithmetic favours it.** Holmes claims that a criminal is guilty of every crime that springs from his crime, then arranges for the law to see only part of the truth.
   - *Where it is spoken:* Holmes: "I must take the view, your Grace, that when a man embarks upon a crime, he is morally guilty of any other crime which may spring from it." Later: "having secured the future, we can afford to be more lenient with the past. I am not in an official position, and there is no reason, so long as the ends of justice are served, why I should disclose all that I know." And: "I see no reason why I should prompt them to take a broader point of view."
   - *What the action does with it:* The story places Holmes's stern principle and his accommodation a few pages apart. He refuses the Duke's doubled hush-money ("matters can hardly be arranged so easily") but takes the six thousand he "earned," rates the Duke like a schoolboy, and then shields Wilder, suggests the Duke coach Hayes to be silent, and sends the villain to Australia. Hayes hangs; Wilder is exiled; the man who knowingly left his child in a den gets advice about his marriage. Whether this is wisdom or complicity, the text does not say; it ends on Holmes patting the cheque: "I am a poor man."

4. **Illegitimacy and the laws of inheritance breed a hatred that reason cannot reach.** Wilder's crime is driven by a resentment of "social laws."
   - *Where it is spoken:* The Duke: "there was a great deal which was unreasoning and fanatical in the hatred which he bore my heir. In his view he should himself have been heir of all my estates, and he deeply resented those social laws which made it impossible." And: "He surmised my secret, and has presumed ever since upon the claim which he has upon me."
   - *What the action does with it:* Wilder is first seen running the conversation for the Duke, "incisive and positive," correcting Holmes, intercepting questions about the post; he has made himself the master of the house that cannot acknowledge him. The story grants his grievance some weight (the entail is real, the mother was refused marriage to protect "my career") while showing where it leads: a forged letter in a grieving boy's mother's name. The man who was denied a name uses another's.

5. **The absent mother is the lever that moves the child; love for her is what the plot exploits.** The boy leaves because he believes his mother is waiting.
   - *Where it is spoken:* Huxtable: "the boy's sympathies are known to have been strongly with his mother. He moped after her departure." The Duke: "he told Arthur, whom he met in the wood, that his mother longed to see him, that she was awaiting him on the moor... Poor Arthur fell into the trap."
   - *What the action does with it:* Holmes's early reasoning anticipates this: "you would expect that some prompting from outside would be needed to make so young a lad do such a thing." The one letter the boy receives, from his father, carries the forged promise of his mother; he takes it with him. The story's last reparation is Holmes insisting the Duke write to the Duchess. The marriage broke over Wilder's presence; the kidnapping worked through the mother's absence; the repair is her return. The text treats the family's split as the first cause of everything.

6. **The hunter reads what is missing as readily as what is present; the absence of cows is the clue.** Holmes's breakthrough is noticing an animal that is nowhere to be seen.
   - *Where it is spoken:* "Strange, Watson, that we should see tracks all along our line, but never a cow on the whole moor." "Only that it is a remarkable cow which walks, canters, and gallops."
   - *What the action does with it:* Watson has seen everything Holmes has seen and remembers none of the patterns ("No, I cannot." "But I can."). The story's method is to insist that deduction is attention sustained over time, and that the medieval barons' trick works on everyone who looks at tracks without asking what made them. It also ties the deception to the Duke's own house: the shoes came from his moat and his museum, and Holmes's wet finger finds fresh mud on them. The great family's own history furnished the instrument of its heir's abduction.

7. **Villainy is easiest to see where it is plainest and hardest where it is cultivated.** Watson is sure the brutal publican is the criminal; the actual author is the demure secretary and the complicit father.
   - *Where it is spoken:* Watson: "I am convinced that this Reuben Hayes knows all about it. A more self-evident villain I never saw." Holmes: "Oh! he impressed you in that way, did he?" Of the plan: "it was no brain of a country publican that thought out such a blind as that."
   - *What the action does with it:* Hayes is guilty and does hang, so Watson is not wrong. But the story reserves its real shock for "I accuse you," spoken to a Duke with half the alphabet after his name. The man who sacked his coachman "without a character on the word of a lying corn-chandler" and the man who struck the German dead are placed on the same moor; the text lets the coachman's grievance stand unanswered.

8. **The schoolmaster who guarded the boy died doing his duty, and nobody mourns him for long.** Heidegger, unpopular and silent, is the only person in the story who acts at once and rightly.
   - *Where it is spoken:* Huxtable: "he was a silent, morose man, not very popular either with masters or boys." Holmes: "he saw the flight of the boy, because he wished to overtake him and bring him back. He seized his bicycle, pursued the lad, and in pursuing him met his death." Of the body: "That he could have gone on after receiving such an injury said much for the vitality and courage of the man."
   - *What the action does with it:* The German leaves without his socks, rides five miles with a crushed skull, and is turned over "reverently" by Holmes; then the plot moves on to cheques and entails. The Duke's phrase for him is "this man Heidegger's dead body," an obstacle to minimising scandal. The story knows what it has done: Holmes refuses the Duke's hush money precisely because "There is the death of this schoolmaster to be accounted for." Whether it is accounted for, with Hayes hanged for ransom-kidnapping and the man who set it in motion sent to Australia, is left to the reader.

## What it teaches
This is a story about a father, and it is unsparing. The Duke of Holdernesse is "one of the greatest subjects of the Crown," and in his own house he is ruled by a secretary who is his unacknowledged son, because that son has his dead mother's face. For that face he has lost his wife, exiled his heir to a boarding school, let three days pass with the child missing, and then, knowing where the boy was and that a murderer held him, left him there three days more. The text does not make him a monster; it gives him a love "such as comes only once in a lifetime" and a conscience that "held him dumb" under Holmes's rebuke. What it shows is that a grief kept alive through a surrogate can become a form of cruelty to everyone who is not the surrogate, and that the people most able to hush things up are the people whose children most need things not hushed.

The harder lesson sits with Holmes. He speaks one of the strictest moral sentences in the canon, that a man who begins a crime owns everything that follows from it, and then he brokers a settlement in which the law hangs the hired brute and never learns who hired him. He takes the money. He is right that the boy is safe, right that the Duchess should be written to, right that Wilder must go; the "future" is secured. But the German master is dead and his death is quietly filed under ransom. The story closes on a joke about poverty, and the joke lands because it is uncomfortable: the detective who refused to be bought has still been paid, and the version of the truth that reaches the public has again been edited by the people who could afford to edit it. You are not told whether to admire this. You are shown it, with the mud still on the shoes.

## Lines worth keeping
- "His Grace is never very friendly with anyone. He is completely immersed in large public questions, and is rather inaccessible to all ordinary emotions. But he was always kind to the boy in his own way."
- "Either the boy was carried off by force or he went of his own free will."
- "A criminal who was capable of such a thought is a man whom I should be proud to do business with."
- "It is impossible as I state it, and therefore I must in some respect have stated it wrong."
- "Only that it is a remarkable cow which walks, canters, and gallops."
- "Old shoes, but newly shod—old shoes, but new nails."
- "I accuse you."
- "When a man embarks upon a crime, he is morally guilty of any other crime which may spring from it."
- "I could see his mother's face in his, and that for her dear sake there was no end to my long-suffering."
- "To humour your guilty elder son, you have exposed your innocent younger son to imminent and unnecessary danger."
- "What are promises to such people as these?"
- "Having secured the future, we can afford to be more lenient with the past."
- "I am a poor man."

## Preoccupations
- a parent's unequal love and the harm it does the less-loved child
- scandal avoided at the cost of safety and justice
- illegitimacy, inheritance, and resentment of social law
- justice negotiated privately rather than enforced publicly
- the past (a dead love, medieval horseshoes) furnishing the present crime
- the detective's price: principle against payment
