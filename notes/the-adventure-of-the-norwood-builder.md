# The Adventure of the Norwood Builder

## What happens
Holmes is complaining at breakfast that London has grown dull since Moriarty's death when a frantic young solicitor, John Hector McFarlane, bursts in expecting arrest for the murder of Jonas Oldacre, a retired Norwood builder who the day before had made McFarlane his sole heir, summoned him to his house at night, and then vanished, leaving blood, McFarlane's stick, and charred remains in a burned wood-pile. Lestrade arrests the young man in Holmes's rooms. Holmes insists on going to Blackheath (the client's home) before Norwood (the crime scene), learns from McFarlane's mother that Oldacre was a cruel rejected suitor who sent her a mutilated photograph of herself on her wedding morning, and then finds nothing at Norwood that does not strengthen the police case. He confesses to Watson that "all my instincts are one way, and all the facts are the other." The next morning Lestrade produces a bloody thumb-print of McFarlane's on the hall wall as final proof, and it is exactly this that saves the client: Holmes knows the mark was not there the day before. He measures the upper corridor, finds it six feet short, sets fire to straw, has the constables cry "Fire!", and Oldacre bolts from a concealed room. The builder had faked his own death to cheat creditors and, at the same time, to hang the son of the woman who refused him.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **An old injury can govern a whole life, and the one who was refused may spend decades waiting to punish the one who refused him.** Oldacre's vengeance is aimed at a woman who turned him down a generation ago.
   - *Where it is spoken:* Mrs. McFarlane: "He sent it to me in that state, with his curse, upon my wedding morning." Holmes: "this injury, as he would consider it, has rankled in his wicked, scheming brain, and all his life he has longed for vengeance, but never seen his chance."
   - *What the action does with it:* The story makes the grudge the actual engine of the plot: the scheme is built so that the mother will believe "he had been murdered by her only child." The financial fraud alone would not need McFarlane; the cruelty is the point, and the text treats it as of a piece with the cat loosed in the aviary. What is refuted is Holmes's own hopeful gloss ("at least he has forgiven you now"), which the mother flatly rejects: "Neither my son nor I want anything from Jonas Oldacre, dead or alive!"

2. **Evidence can be too good, and the strongest proof is sometimes a sign that someone is working to produce it.** Lestrade's "final" thumb-print is what convicts Oldacre.
   - *Where it is spoken:* Holmes, early: "It strikes me, my good Lestrade, as being just a trifle too obvious." Later: "The thumb-mark, Lestrade. You said it was final; and so it was, in a very different sense."
   - *What the action does with it:* The story's structure is the test. Every fact Holmes finds at Norwood "fits in with the official theory"; it is only when the forger adds one fact too many that the whole collapses. Holmes names the failing: "he had not that supreme gift of the artist, the knowledge of when to stop." The lesson is not that evidence lies, but that an absence (Holmes having examined a clear wall yesterday) can outweigh any presence.

3. **Instinct and fact can genuinely diverge, and the honest investigator admits he may be wrong.** For a whole night Holmes believes Lestrade may be right.
   - *Where it is spoken:* "I kept a bold face before Lestrade, but, upon my soul, I believe that for once the fellow is on the right track and we are on the wrong. All my instincts are one way, and all the facts are the other."
   - *What the action does with it:* Watson records the physical toll: no sleep, no breakfast, "pale and harassed," an hour of violin "endeavouring to soothe his own ruffled spirits." The story vindicates the instinct, but it does not let the vindication be cheap; Holmes also admits that juries decide on facts, not his theories, and the man would have hanged.

4. **Appearances are no guide to guilt or innocence, in either direction.** Watson wants to rest on McFarlane's gentle looks; Holmes refuses.
   - *Where it is spoken:* Watson: "Surely the man's appearance would go far with any jury?" Holmes: "That is a dangerous argument my dear Watson. You remember that terrible murderer, Bert Stevens... Was there ever a more mild-mannered, Sunday-school young man?"
   - *What the action does with it:* The story complicates its own warning. McFarlane's weak face does match his innocence, and Oldacre's "odious face—crafty, vicious, malignant" matches his guilt; Watson's narration leans on physiognomy constantly. But the plot only turns on measured corridors, a known-clear wall, and a bank-book, never on a face. Holmes's sarcastic "how deceptive appearances may be, to be sure! Such a nice young man to look at!" is said when he already knows the face told the truth.

5. **Where you begin an inquiry decides what you find; the crime is not always the right starting point.** Holmes goes to Blackheath before Norwood.
   - *Where it is spoken:* "The police are making the mistake of concentrating their attention upon the second, because it happens to be the one which is actually criminal. But it is evident to me that the logical way to approach the case is to begin by trying to throw some light upon the first incident—the curious will."
   - *What the action does with it:* The visit to the mother yields nothing usable as evidence and even seems to help the prosecution (a son who heard such bitterness would be "predisposed towards hatred and violence"). Yet it is the only place the motive could be learned, and Holmes throws it back at Lestrade at the end: "I told you that you should go to Blackheath first and Norwood afterwards." The method is confirmed, but only in hindsight.

6. **Pride, in the detective and the policeman alike, shapes how the truth is handled.** Both men enjoy their turn at the other's expense.
   - *Where it is spoken:* Lestrade: "You don't like being beaten any more than the rest of us do." Holmes, staging the fire: "you must not grudge me a little pomp and ceremony now." And: "I owed you a little mystification, Lestrade, for your chaff in the morning."
   - *What the action does with it:* Holmes admits "We could, of course, have gone in and taken him, but it amused me to make him reveal himself." An innocent man is in a cell while the detective arranges a conjuring trick for the satisfaction of it. The story treats this as comic, but it has placed the admission on the page. Then Holmes hands away the credit ("The work is its own reward"), so the pride is of a particular kind: it wants the private triumph, not the public one.

7. **A detective who misses the criminal is also, in a sense, a man who misses his occupation.** Holmes's opening complaint is that virtue is dull.
   - *Where it is spoken:* "London has become a singularly uninteresting city since the death of the late lamented Professor Moriarty." Watson: "I can hardly think that you would find many decent citizens to agree with you." And on hearing of a murder charge: "This is really most grati—most interesting."
   - *What the action does with it:* Watson's narration catches the slip and names it: "a sympathy which was not, I am afraid, entirely unmixed with satisfaction." The story confirms that Holmes needs crime as a painter needs light, and that his compassion for McFarlane is real but arrives second. Watson's own wry self-awareness about the "chronicle of our successes which... a patient public will sooner or later have to endure" belongs to the same register.

8. **Kindness can be a weapon; the gift that comes too easily should be suspected.** McFarlane is undone by trusting good fortune.
   - *Where it is spoken:* McFarlane: "I was not in a humour to refuse him anything that he might ask. He was my benefactor, and all my desire was to carry out his wishes in every particular."
   - *What the action does with it:* Every kindness Oldacre offers is a trap: the will, the supper, the request for secrecy ("not one word to your parents"), the kept stick ("I will keep your stick until you come back to claim it"), even the thumb pressed on a seal. The story does not blame McFarlane for gratitude, but it shows how a decent impulse gives a malicious one everything it needs. The hidden, buried cruelty of the scheme is matched by the hidden, buried man.

## What it teaches
The story is a reminder that spite is patient. Jonas Oldacre carries a rejection for roughly thirty years, and when his money runs out he does not merely flee; he builds a machine to kill his old sweetheart's child in the eyes of the law and to make her believe her son a murderer. Nothing in the text explains this beyond the earlier cruelty to animals and the defaced photograph. It asks you to accept that some people's resentments do not fade but concentrate, and that the respectable, "secretive and retiring" exterior gives no warning. Against this it sets a mother's unshaken faith and a young man's unguarded gratitude, both of which are good and both of which are nearly fatal.

What is difficult is how narrowly justice is done. Holmes spends a day confirming the prosecution's case, loses a night's sleep, and tells Watson plainly that the facts are against him and that juries do not hang on instinct. The rescue comes not from brilliance but from one remembered observation (a clear wall) and the criminal's inability to stop improving his lie. The text wants you to feel how contingent that was. And it leaves a smaller discomfort in Holmes's own conduct: the trick with the straw, admitted to be unnecessary, is performed for amusement and to pay off a morning's teasing while the innocent man waits in custody. The story treats this lightly. A reader does not have to.

## Lines worth keeping
- "London has become a singularly uninteresting city since the death of the late lamented Professor Moriarty."
- "My companion's expressive face showed a sympathy which was not, I am afraid, entirely unmixed with satisfaction."
- "If a man drew up a will which he did not intend ever to be effective, he might do it so."
- "It strikes me, my good Lestrade, as being just a trifle too obvious."
- "All my instincts are one way, and all the facts are the other."
- "He was more like a malignant and cunning ape than a human being."
- "He sent it to me in that state, with his curse, upon my wedding morning."
- "Was there ever a more mild-mannered, Sunday-school young man?"
- "Important fresh evidence is a two-edged thing, and may possibly cut in a very different direction."
- "You said it was final; and so it was, in a very different sense."
- "He had not that supreme gift of the artist, the knowledge of when to stop."
- "The work is its own reward."

## Preoccupations
- long-held grudges and revenge across a lifetime
- evidence that is too perfect, and how deception overreaches
- instinct against facts, and the limits of a detective's certainty
- appearances as an unreliable guide to character
- professional pride and rivalry between the detective and the police
- gratitude and trust exploited by a benefactor
