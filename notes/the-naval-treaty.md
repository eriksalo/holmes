# The Naval Treaty

## What happens
Watson receives a dictated letter from an old schoolfellow, Percy "Tadpole" Phelps, nephew of the foreign minister Lord Holdhurst, who has just come through nine weeks of brain-fever and begs him to bring Holmes to Woking. Phelps tells his story from a sofa, with his fiancée Annie Harrison holding his hand and her merry, plump brother Joseph playing host: ten weeks earlier he stayed late at the Foreign Office to copy a secret Anglo-Italian naval treaty, rang for coffee, went downstairs to find the commissionnaire asleep, heard the bell of his own room ring, and ran back to find the treaty gone. The commissionnaire's wife had hurried away; the police searched her and found nothing; Phelps collapsed into a "raving maniac" and has lain in Joseph's old bedroom ever since, nursed by Annie. Holmes muses on a moss-rose, advertises for a cab, interviews a touchy Scotland Yard man and a noble lord with resoled boots, and offers nothing. The next day Phelps reports that a cloaked man with a knife tried his bedroom window at two in the morning. Holmes orders Annie to sit in that room all day and lock it from outside at night, sends Phelps to London in Watson's care, and himself spends the night in the rhododendrons. At two a.m. Joseph Harrison lets himself in, lifts a floorboard by the gas-pipe, draws out the treaty, and walks into Holmes, who takes the paper after a knife-fight and lets him go. At breakfast in Baker Street Holmes serves Phelps the treaty under a dish-cover, and explains that Joseph, calling at the office on the night to collect Phelps, found the room empty, rang the bell, saw the paper, took it on impulse, and had been locked out of his own hiding-place for ten weeks by the invalid lying on top of it. Joseph had lost heavily in stocks and "did not allow either his sister's happiness or your reputation to hold his hand." Holmes suspects he will be gone before the police arrive, and thinks that "all the better for the government."

## What the work observes about being human

1. **A man's honour can rest on an object, and the world holds him responsible for accidents.**
   - Where it is spoken: Phelps: "What though I was the victim of an extraordinary accident? No allowance is made for accidents where diplomatic interests are at stake. I was ruined, shamefully, hopelessly ruined." And in his letter: "If you fail me, then my honour as well as my position are forever forfeited."
   - What the action does with it: Phelps did nothing wrong; he was drowsy and went to look for his coffee. For that he loses ten weeks of his mind, is told by his uncle that his dismissal waits only on his health, and is restored to honour by a cylinder of paper that was under his floor the whole time. The story confirms his bleak sentence about diplomatic interests and then shows what the sentence costs: a brilliant, sensitive man reduced to kissing Holmes's hand and needing brandy. Honour here is not a quality of the man but a document's location.

2. **Great consequences can come from a small opportunist, and the sufferer will invent a conspiracy before he suspects a relative.**
   - Where it is spoken: Phelps: "I begin to believe that I am the unconscious centre of some monstrous conspiracy, and that my life is aimed at as well as my honour." Holmes: "the thief's presence in the room was purely accidental. He saw his chance and he took it." And: "Being an absolutely selfish man, when a chance presented itself he did not allow either his sister's happiness or your reputation to hold his hand."
   - What the action does with it: The grand political intrigue that Phelps constructs — and that Watson half accepts on the train — collapses into a brother-in-law who "lost heavily in dabbling with stocks." No embassy, no spy, no plot; a man who happened to be in a corridor. The story prefers the small, selfish, unplanned explanation to the large one, and shows that a victim's mind reaches for the large one because it is less humiliating than the truth that the thief was sitting at his bedside offering sunshine.

3. **Hospitality and good cheer can be a thief's cover, and appearances tell you nothing.**
   - Where it is spoken: Watson on Joseph: "a rather stout man who received us with much hospitality... his cheeks were so ruddy and his eyes so merry that he still conveyed the impression of a plump and mischievous boy." Joseph: "Ah, poor old chap, he clings to any straw!" Holmes afterwards: "I am afraid Joseph's character is a rather deeper and more dangerous one than one might judge from his appearance."
   - What the action does with it: The merry host is the man who "flew at me with his knife" and "looked murder out of the only eye he could see with." For ten weeks he fetched doctors, roused the house, and found "marks on the bed outside the window" that he himself had made. Watson's first paragraph of description is wrong in every particular that matters, and the story never corrects the narrator in his own voice; it just lets Holmes's night in the bushes do it.

4. **Holmes finds his proof of Providence in a rose, and his clients want their mystery solved.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "Our highest assurance of the goodness of Providence seems to me to rest in the flowers... this rose is an extra. Its smell and its colour are an embellishment of life, not a condition of it. It is only goodness which gives extras." Watson: "Percy Phelps and his nurse looked at Holmes during this demonstration with surprise and a good deal of disappointment." Annie, "with a touch of asperity": "Do you see any prospect of solving this mystery, Mr. Holmes?"
   - What the action does with it: The story places the one theological statement in the canon in the mouth of a man who has just had a new idea about a bell, and lets the people in trouble be impatient with it. The case is then solved by nothing providential: a man lying in a bush with sandwiches, a cut knuckle, a thief's greed. Yet the "extras" the story actually shows are human — Annie's two months of nursing, Watson giving up his practice for a day, Mrs. Hudson's curried chicken, the dish-cover flourish Holmes "never can resist." The rose speech is neither confirmed nor mocked; it is left standing beside a case in which goodness arrives through people being put to trouble.

5. **Education and influence are both real forces, and the story shows them from the top and the bottom.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes on the board-schools: "Light-houses, my boy! Beacons of the future! Capsules with hundreds of bright little seeds in each, out of which will spring the wise, better England of the future. I suppose that man Phelps does not drink?" Watson on Phelps: "his abilities and the influences which he commanded had won him a good position at the Foreign Office." Phelps: "through the influences of my uncle, Lord Holdhurst, I rose rapidly."
   - What the action does with it: The man who had every prize and the best connections is the one in a sick-room; the boy "we" hit over the shins for his gaudy relationship is now the Foreign Office's embarrassment. Holmes's hope is placed in the anonymous mass of the future and withdrawn in the same breath for a question about drink. The story observes that gifts and patronage make careers and cannot protect them, and that hope for a better England is spoken from a train looking down on a "sordid" view.

6. **Illness is the result of shame and also a possible alibi for it.**
   - Where it is spoken: Phelps: "Watson there would tell you that I was a nervous, sensitive boy at school. It is my nature." And: "it is only during the last three days that my memory has quite returned. Sometimes I wish that it never had." Lord Holdhurst: "An attack of brain-fever, for example?" Holmes: "I did not say so."
   - What the action does with it: Phelps's collapse is real; it is also, for ten weeks, the only reason the treaty has not reached a foreign embassy, since his body in Joseph's bed blocks the hiding-place. His uncle — "a nobleman who is in truth noble" — flashes "a swift glance" and entertains the thought that the nephew stole it and went mad. The story uses one man's breakdown as both the pathos of the case and its accidental solution, and shows that even the honourable will suspect the sick.

7. **Reputation exempts no one from suspicion, and then exempts the guilty from the dock.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "Who is it who profits by it?... and there is Lord Holdhurst." Watson: "Not a statesman with the honourable record of Lord Holdhurst?" Holmes: "It is a possibility and we cannot afford to disregard it." Later: "I fancy that Lord Holdhurst for one, and Mr. Percy Phelps for another, would very much rather that the affair never got as far as a police-court."
   - What the action does with it: The method refuses to let a good name clear a man; Holmes notices the minister's resoled boots and his "struggle to keep up his position" and keeps him on the list. Then, having caught the real thief, Holmes lets him go and hopes the police are slow, because a trial would embarrass the same good names. The story shows suspicion applied without respect to rank and justice applied with it. Joseph Harrison walks away; nobody says what Annie, engaged to Phelps and sister to the thief, is to be told.

8. **The clearest-headed person in the case is the one asked to sit still and told nothing.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes on the handwriting: "a woman of rare character... it is something to know that your client is in close contact with some one who, for good or evil, has an exceptional nature." Annie: "If we keep our courage and our patience the truth must come out." Holmes: "She carried out every one of my injunctions to the letter, and certainly without her co-operation you would not have that paper in your coat-pocket."
   - What the action does with it: Annie guards the room all day against a threat she is not told about, which is her own brother, and locks the door at night as instructed. Her obedience is what defeats him. She is praised for her character and her co-operation and is absent from the last scene; her brother's ruin and her lover's recovery are both settled over breakfast in a house where she is not. The story credits her and does not let her speak after the promise.

## What it teaches
This is a story about how small the causes of a catastrophe can be, and how little the sufferer can see of them. A bell rings in an empty room; a man goes down for coffee; a plump brother-in-law has lost money on the exchange. From these a treaty is lost, a career is wrecked, a mind breaks for nine weeks, and a foreign minister considers whether his nephew is a thief. The reader is asked to hold two things together: that the consequences were vast and that the act was a moment's greed in a corridor. Phelps's instinct — that a thing this terrible must mean a conspiracy against him — is the instinct of anyone who has been hurt, and the story is gentle with it and still wrong-foots it completely.

What it leaves unresolved is the cost of the quiet ending. Holmes has said that Phelps's honour and his own were at stake; both are restored. The thief is let go for the comfort of the government and of the family. But the family includes a woman who nursed a man for two months and who is engaged to him, and who will have to learn what her brother did and that he was allowed to vanish. Holmes's rose speech says goodness gives extras. The story's own extras are Annie's and Watson's and Mrs. Hudson's, given freely; the mercy shown Joseph is given for reasons of state. A thoughtful reader can admire the solution and still wonder who, exactly, it was for.

## Lines worth keeping
- "No allowance is made for accidents where diplomatic interests are at stake."
- "Hitherto I had been acting, and action had numbed thought."
- "Sometimes I wish that it never had."
- "The authorities are excellent at amassing facts, though they do not always use them to advantage. What a lovely thing a rose is!"
- "It is only goodness which gives extras, and so I say again that we have much to hope from the flowers."
- "'You suspect some one?' 'I suspect myself.' 'What!' 'Of coming to conclusions too rapidly.'"
- "Light-houses, my boy! Beacons of the future!"
- "The most difficult crime to track is the one which is purposeless."
- "a nobleman who is in truth noble"
- "You noticed, of course, that his boots had been resoled."
- "If we keep our courage and our patience the truth must come out."
- "Well, my own was at stake, you know."
- "he did not allow either his sister's happiness or your reputation to hold his hand."
- "The principal difficulty in your case... lay in the fact of there being too much evidence."
- "I can only say for certain that Mr. Joseph Harrison is a gentleman to whose mercy I should be extremely unwilling to trust."

## Preoccupations
- honour that depends on an object and on accident
- the small, selfish cause behind a vast consequence
- appearances that deceive, and the thief inside the household
- goodness as something extra, given by people being put to trouble
- justice bent to protect reputations
- illness as both suffering and suspicion
- the woman who does the work and is absent from the ending
