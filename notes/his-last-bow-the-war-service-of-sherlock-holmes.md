# His Last Bow: The War Service of Sherlock Holmes

## What happens
On the night of 2 August 1914, told in the third person, the German master-spy Von Bork stands on the terrace of his house on the English coast with the embassy secretary Von Herling, congratulating himself on four years of passing as a hard-drinking sporting squire while filling a safe with English secrets. His household has gone to Flushing; only an old housekeeper, Martha, remains at her knitting. His last and best agent, the Irish-American "Altamont," is due with the naval signal codes. Von Herling leaves; Altamont arrives in a small Ford with an elderly chauffeur, complains that Von Bork's agents keep getting arrested and that the police are close to him too, demands his five hundred pounds before handing over the parcel, and watches Von Bork open it to find a *Practical Handbook of Bee Culture*. A chloroformed sponge follows. Altamont is Holmes, out of retirement for two years at the Prime Minister's request; the chauffeur is Watson; Martha is Holmes's agent. Holmes packs the safe into Von Bork's own valise, tells the bound spy that the information he fed Berlin was false and that his cousin and uncle were old clients, concedes the whole proceeding is illegal, and drives him toward Scotland Yard. On the terrace Holmes tells Watson that an east wind is coming, cold and bitter, but God's own, and that he has a cheque to cash before the drawer stops it.

## What the work observes about being human
1. **Apparent softness or sleepiness in a people or a person may hide something hard, and the one who mistakes it for weakness is the one trapped.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Von Herling: "It is that surface simplicity of theirs which makes a trap for the stranger. One's first impression is that they are entirely soft. Then one comes suddenly upon something very hard." Von Bork: "A more docile, simple folk could not be imagined." On Martha: "She might almost personify Britannia, with her complete self-absorption and general air of comfortable somnolence."
   - *What the action does with it:* The knitting woman with the cat is Holmes's agent; her lamp going out is the signal; she has "the addresses as usual" of every letter he posted. Von Herling states the truth about the English as a general warning and then applies it wrongly to the one Englishwoman in sight. The story makes the sleepy figure of Britannia literally the spy in the house.

2. **A pose one enjoys stops feeling like a pose, and that is both its strength and its blindness.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Von Bork: "No, no, don't call it a pose. A pose is an artificial thing. This is quite natural. I am a born sportsman. I enjoy it." Holmes at the end: "you have one quality which is very rare in a German, Mr. Von Bork: you are a sportsman and you will bear me no ill-will."
   - *What the action does with it:* The sportsman is so secure in his role that he shows his agent the double combination and tells him the password—"August" and "1914," his own cleverness displayed for applause. Holmes's two-year performance as Altamont is the opposite kind: "my well of English seems to be permanently defiled," and he is glad to shave the goatee. The man who believes his mask is beaten by the man who hates his.

3. **Honour is called medieval by the diplomat, held to by the spy, and finally invoked by the victor over the vanquished.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Von Bork on Belgium: "But her honour?" Von Herling: "Tut, my dear sir, we live in a utilitarian age. Honour is a mediæval conception." Holmes to the bound Von Bork: "you have done your best for your country, and I have done my best for mine, and what could be more natural?... it is better than to fall before some ignoble foe."
   - *What the action does with it:* The spy who steals a nation's secrets is more scrupulous about treaties than his own superior; Altamont mocks his honour over a cheque ("it is a business proposition"); and the man who drugs and kidnaps him ends by granting him a soldier's dignity. The story does not settle what honour is. It shows every character claiming it in the register that suits him.

4. **Those who use agents despise them; those who are used know it; and the one making the accusation is the one doing the betraying.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Von Herling: "They are useful, these traitors, but I grudge them their blood money." Altamont: "you are their employer, ain't you? It's up to you to see that they don't fall down. But they do fall down, and when did you ever pick them up?... It's enough to make a man bug-house when he has to play a part from morning to night." Holmes, revealed: "I watched them, Watson, and I picked them as they ripened."
   - *What the action does with it:* Altamont's speech about an employer's duty to his men is delivered by the man who has been handing those men to the police for two years. His line about the strain of playing a part is the one true thing Altamont says, and it is Holmes's own experience. The phrase "picked them as they ripened" treats five human beings as fruit; the story lets it stand without comment. Loyalty to one's instruments is talked about by everyone and practised by no one.

5. **In war the law is openly suspended, and the lawbreaker agrees with every charge against him.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Von Bork: "You are a private individual. You have no warrant for my arrest. The whole proceeding is absolutely illegal and outrageous." Holmes: "Absolutely." "Kidnapping a German subject." "And stealing his private papers."
   - *What the action does with it:* Holmes concedes everything and threatens him with the village mob—"'The Dangling Prussian' as a signpost." "The Englishman is a patient creature, but at present his temper is a little inflamed." In earlier stories Holmes breaks the law and someone objects; here there is no one left to object except the victim, and the tone is cheerful. The story records the moment when private justice becomes national policy and nobody minds.

6. **A nature does not retire; the man who watched criminals watches bees, and comes back when asked.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Holmes: "Behold the fruit of pensive nights and laborious days when I watched the little working gangs as once I watched the criminal world of London." "The Foreign Minister alone I could have withstood, but when the Premier also deigned to visit my humble roof—!"
   - *What the action does with it:* The bee book is the bait in the parcel: the retirement is literally the trap. "It would brighten my declining years to see a German cruiser navigating the Solent according to the mine-field plans which I have furnished." He has spent two years in Chicago, Buffalo, and Skibbareen at sixty. The story shows retirement as a change of subject, not of self.

7. **Old friends are each other's fixed points, and they know the conversation may be the last.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Holmes: "How have the years used you? You look the same blithe boy as ever." Watson: "I feel twenty years younger, Holmes." Holmes: "Good old Watson! You are the one fixed point in a changing age." "Stand with me here upon the terrace, for it may be the last quiet talk that we shall ever have."
   - *What the action does with it:* Two elderly men haul a bound prisoner down a garden walk; Watson is rejoining "your old service." The intimacy is real and brief, framed by a drugged enemy wriggling in the car. The story grants them the terrace and no more.

8. **A coming catastrophe can be named as a cleansing by the man who sees it coming, and the story frames it as both.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Holmes: "There's an east wind coming all the same, such a wind as never blew on England yet. It will be cold and bitter, Watson, and a good many of us may wither before its blast. But it's God's own wind none the less, and a cleaner, better, stronger land will lie in the sunshine when the storm has cleared." Watson: "I think not, Holmes. It is very warm."
   - *What the action does with it:* The narrator has already called it "the most terrible August in the history of the world" and said "God's curse hung heavy over a degenerate world." So the text holds two theologies in one night—a curse and a cleansing wind—and gives Holmes the second while reserving the first for itself. Watson's literal answer is the only dissent. The story ends with a cheque to cash before the drawer stops it, which is to say it ends by changing the subject.

## What it teaches
This story is about disguise as a way of life and what it does to the people inside it. Von Bork's disguise is comfortable—he is a sportsman, he enjoys it—and the comfort is what makes him careless enough to recite his own password. Holmes's disguise is a two-year penance in bad grammar and a goatee, and he comes out of it with Altamont's slang still in his mouth, apologizing for it. Martha's disguise is simply to be an old woman with a cat, which is what she is. The lesson is not that masks fail; it is that a mask one has stopped noticing is the dangerous kind, and that the only person in the story whose cover is perfect is the one who is not pretending.

What is hard is the matter of the agents. Altamont lectures Von Bork on his duty to the men who "fall down," and the lecture is true, and the man giving it has caused every fall. Holmes "picked them as they ripened." The story's own standard for honour—"you have done your best for your country, and I have done my best for mine"—covers this, and the warmth between Holmes and Von Bork at the end is genuine. But five men are in Portsmouth and Portland and will "stand the racket," and no one on the terrace says their names again. The east wind speech asks the reader to believe the coming storm will leave "a cleaner, better, stronger land." The opening paragraph has already called the same night a curse. A reader is left between those two sentences, and the text does not choose.

## Preoccupations
- disguise and the mask one stops noticing
- surface softness that hides hardness
- honour claimed by everyone and practised unevenly
- using and discarding agents
- the law suspended for a cause
- old friendship at the edge of catastrophe
- the restless nature that cannot retire
