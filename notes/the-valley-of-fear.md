# The Valley of Fear

## What happens

Holmes decodes a cipher from Porlock, a frightened informer inside Moriarty's organization, and reads "There is danger... Douglas... Birlstone" minutes before Inspector MacDonald arrives to say John Douglas of Birlstone Manor was shot dead in the night. At the moated Jacobean house the detectives find a body with its face blown off by a sawed-off American shotgun, a brand on the forearm, a card reading "V.V. 341," a missing wedding ring, a bloody footprint on the sill, a bicycle in the shrubbery, and one dumb-bell where there should be two. Douglas's wife and his friend Cecil Barker tell a story Holmes calls "a great, big, thumping, obtrusive, uncompromising lie," and Watson catches the two laughing in the garden. Holmes fishes a bundle of clothes out of the moat with Watson's umbrella, has MacDonald announce the moat will be drained, and watches Barker haul the bundle back up; then Douglas himself steps out of a priest-hole. He killed his attacker, Ted Baldwin, in a struggle, swapped identities with the corpse, and meant to vanish so his old enemies would believe him dead. Part II goes back twenty years to Vermissa Valley, Pennsylvania, where a cheerful Irish fugitive named Jack McMurdo joins the Scowrers — a lodge of the Eminent Order of Freemen turned murder-society under Boss McGinty — courts Ettie Shafter, rises to Inner Deacon, hears the conscience-stricken Brother Morris, and finally traps the lodge's leaders in his own parlour with the words "I am Birdy Edwards!" He is the Pinkerton detective sent to break them. The Scowrers hang or are imprisoned; the survivors hunt Edwards for the rest of his life. In the Epilogue Douglas is acquitted on self-defence, sent abroad on Holmes's advice, and is lost overboard off St. Helena. Holmes names the hand: Moriarty.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **The most dangerous men are the ones no one is allowed to suspect; respectability is the best disguise.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Holmes on Moriarty: "so aloof is he from general suspicion, so immune from criticism, so admirable in his management and self-effacement, that for those very words that you have uttered he could hale you to a court and emerge with your year's pension as a solatium for his wounded character" (Part I, Ch. I). MacDonald, after meeting him: "it was like a father's blessing before you go out into the cold, cruel world" (I, II). On McGinty: "Here, one would say, is a bluff, honest fellow, whose heart would be sound however rude his outspoken words might seem" (II, II); he is also "a high public official, a municipal councillor, and a commissioner of roads" (II, II).
   - *What the action does with it:* Confirmed twice over, with a difference. McGinty, whose respectability is local and crude, hangs. Moriarty, whose respectability is academic and total, murders Douglas by proxy and is never touched. The law protects the better-disguised criminal more effectively than it protects his victim: Douglas is acquitted by English law and killed anyway.

2. **Every chain of power has a weak link, and the weak link is a conscience.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Holmes on Porlock: "Led on by some rudimentary aspirations towards right, and encouraged by the judicious stimulation of an occasional ten-pound note sent to him by devious methods, he has once or twice given me advance information" (I, I). Watson: "no chain is stronger than its weakest link." McMurdo to the lodge: "Do you think there is no weaker brother among all our lodges that could not be bought?" (II, VI).
   - *What the action does with it:* The Scowrers are destroyed from inside — through Morris's letter, carried by the man they trust most. McGinty's blindness is exact: "There's not a weak man in the lodge... True as steel, every man of them. And yet, by the Lord! there is that skunk Morris" (II, VII). He suspects the one honest man and confides in the detective. But Moriarty's chain holds: Porlock, "scared out of his senses," withdraws, and the warning that reaches Holmes is too late by hours. The work shows that the conscience inside a terror-organization can break it, and also that a sufficiently disciplined organization can survive its one conscience.

3. **A lie at the threshold does not mean guilt behind the door; those who conceal may be protecting rather than hiding.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Holmes: "A lie, Watson—a great, big, thumping, obtrusive, uncompromising lie—that's what meets us on the threshold!" (I, VI). Watson, after the garden: "She must be a heartless creature to sit laughing at some jest within a few hours of her husband's murder" (I, VI). Holmes: "there are few wives, having any regard for their husbands, who would let any man's spoken word stand between them and that husband's dead body" (I, VI).
   - *What the action does with it:* Holmes is right about the fact — she is not grieving — and the reader is invited to share Watson's contempt. Then the body proves to be Baldwin's and her laughter proves to be relief at a living husband. Her plea, "Perhaps some day you will do me justice" (I, VI), is answered by the plot, not by Watson, who never does revise his judgment aloud. The work thus makes the narrator's moralizing a trap for the reader and lets Holmes's cynicism about women be true in letter and false in meaning.

4. **Fear is not a place you leave; it is a condition you carry.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Mrs. Douglas reporting her husband: "'I have been in the Valley of Fear. I am not out of it yet.'—'Are we never to get out of the Valley of Fear?' I have asked him when I have seen him more serious than usual. 'Sometimes I think that we never shall'" (I, V). Morris: "the cloud of murder hangs thicker and lower than that over the heads of the people. It is the Valley of Fear, the Valley of Death. The terror is in the hearts of the people from the dusk to the dawn" (II, IV). Ettie: "anywhere to get away from this Valley of Fear!" (II, V).
   - *What the action does with it:* Edwards leaves Vermissa by special train and is chased "from Chicago to California, then they chased me out of America" (I, VII); he raises a drawbridge every night in Sussex; he dies off St. Helena. The valley follows him across two continents and an ocean. The fear also changes hands: the oppressors, "so omnipotent and unchallenged" (II, III), see "the cloud of avenging Law drifting up in that serene sky under which they had dwelt so long" (II, VI), and McGinty dies "cringing and whining" (II, VII). No one in the book gets out.

5. **Men who kill without compunction can weep at a song; cruelty does not require the absence of feeling, only its misdirection.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "Strange indeed is human nature. Here were these men, to whom murder was familiar, who again and again had struck down the father of the family... and yet the tender or pathetic in music could move them to tears" (II, III). "To their contorted natures it had become a spirited and chivalrous thing to volunteer for service against some man who had never injured them" (II, III). Lawler and Andrews are "total abstainers... exemplary members of the society, with the one simple exception that they were assassins," who speak of their killings "with the half-bashful pride of men who had done good and unselfish service for the community" (II, V).
   - *What the action does with it:* The lodge votes a "pension to Jim Carnaway's widow" (II, III) in the same meeting that assigns two murders; it repeats a dying man's screams for amusement — "Let's hear again how he squealed" (II, V). Fellowship for insiders and butchery for outsiders are the same organ. The work insists the killers are not monsters but a society; Baldwin and Tiger Cormac are singled out as exceptions precisely because they enjoy the violence for itself.

6. **"It is war" is the sentence that makes anything permissible, and the work puts it in the mouth of a man who does not believe it.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* McMurdo: "There are some would say it was war... a war of two classes with all in, so that each struck as best it could" (II, IV); "We are but poor men that are trying in our own way to get our rights" (II, V). McGinty to Captain Marvin: "What are you but the paid tool of the capitalists, hired by them to club or shoot your poorer fellow citizen?" (II, III). Ettie: "Oh, Jack, don't let me hear you speak so! That is how he speaks—the other one!" (II, II).
   - *What the action does with it:* The speaker is a Pinkerton in character, so the class-war justification is exposed as a lie told by a liar. But the work leaves an uncomfortable structure standing. Morris observes that the Scowrers' terror has been "gradually driving all the small men out of trade" into the hands of "great companies like the Railroad or the General Iron, who have their directors in New York or Philadelphia" (II, III); the deliverance comes because "Five big corporations and the two railroads have taken the thing up in dead earnest" (II, VI); and Edwards escapes on "a special train which had been sent by the railroad company" (II, VII). The murderers' rhetoric is false and the murderers' enemies are capital. The book refuses both the Scowrers' excuse and any simple view of who benefits.

7. **A deliverer who goes "down into hell" comes back with some of it on him, and the work both rewards and kills him.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Edwards: "Maybe they will say that I was as bad as you. They can say what they like, so long as I get you... If ever I have suggested things, so as to keep my place among you, they were things which I knew I could prevent. I could not save Dunn and Menzies" (II, VII). "You call me a traitor; but I guess there's many a thousand will call me a deliverer that went down into hell to save them" (II, VII). Douglas: "from first to last I have done nothing to be ashamed of, and nothing that I would not do again" (I, VII).
   - *What the action does with it:* He stood at the door while an old editor's "white hair was dabbled with patches of blood" (II, III); he watched a manager shot in the stomach before a hundred miners; he blew up a house. His "Self-protection is no murder, anyhow" (II, VI), spoken in character to Morris, is the Scowrers' own logic. He gains a wife and a fortune, is acquitted — "The English law is in the main a just law. You will get no worse than your deserts" (I, VII) — and is then murdered by a "master hand." Holmes's "I'll die the easier when I think of the work I have done in this valley" (II, VII) is granted him, and so is the dying.

8. **The weak man's conscience does the work the strong man's nerve cannot.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Morris: "I had a conscience and a religion; but they made me a criminal among them... Maybe I'm a coward. Maybe it's the thought of my poor little woman and the children that makes me one" (II, IV). McMurdo: "You were too soft for the job" (II, IV). Scanlan: "It's not the will but the nerve that is wanting in me" (II, VII).
   - *What the action does with it:* Morris's letter is the hinge on which the whole rescue turns; Edwards's mock-compliment, "we'll have to elect you Bodymaster yet; for you've surely saved the lodge" (II, VI), is literally true of the valley. Morris groans "I feel that his blood is on my hands" when his hands are the cleanest in the room. The book honours him and then drops him: his fate after the arrests is never told. Scanlan, the other weakling, is sent on the errand that saves Ettie. The timid are the instruments; the bold are the hanged.

9. **Love is given across a gulf of ignorance, and trust is vindicated and betrayed at once.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* On Ettie: "A lovely violet growing upon one of those black slag-heaps of the mines would not have seemed more surprising" (II, I). McMurdo: "Good Lord! how little you know of me! Your innocent soul, my darling, could not even guess what is passing in mine" (II, II). Ettie: "No, no, Jack! I would trust you anywhere" (II, II). Edwards: "Not a soul, not one soul, not my nearest and dearest, knew that I was playing it" (II, VII).
   - *What the action does with it:* Her trust is right about his character and wrong about every fact; she pleads on her knees for him to give up a crime he is not committing. Mrs. Douglas, twenty years later, is in the same position: "Can a husband ever carry about a secret all his life and a woman who loves him have no suspicion of it?" (I, V). Douglas admits "I would have been a wiser man if I had told her sooner" (I, VII). The protective lie protects no one — she was "terrified if ever he came home later than was expected" — and Ettie dies in California anyway. Love in this book is real and does not shelter.

10. **The detective is an artist, and so is the criminal; the better artist wins.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* "Holmes had the impersonal joy of the true artist in his better work" (I, I). Holmes: "Watson insists that I am the dramatist in real life... Surely our profession, Mr. Mac, would be a drab and sordid one if we did not sometimes set the scene so as to glorify our results" (I, VII). Watson: "Without having a tinge of cruelty in his singular composition, he was undoubtedly callous from long overstimulation" (I, II). Holmes on the death at sea: "You can tell an old master by the sweep of his brush. I can tell a Moriarty when I see one" (Epilogue).
   - *What the action does with it:* Holmes's staging works — the moat letter, the shrubbery, Barker caught with the bundle — and Edwards's staging works even better, with rifles through every window. Both are rewarded with "the triumphant vindication of bold theories" (I, VII). But Holmes's own frame undoes the triumph: "Everything comes in circles... The old wheel turns, and the same spoke comes up. It's all been done before, and will be again" (I, II). The last brushstroke belongs to Moriarty, and Holmes can only say "you must give me time."

11. **Inside an organization of terror, obedience replaces judgment, and asking why is the first act of treason.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* McGinty to young Wilson: "Sure, it's not for the likes of you to ask what the man has done. He has been judged over there. That's no business of ours" (II, III). "If you know nothing, you can testify nothing, and no trouble can come of it" (II, III). McMurdo on the Wilcox children: "There's these two women and the children. Do they go up too?" — "They have to—else how can we get him" — "If it's right or if it's wrong, it's for you to decide" (II, V). Morris: "Things have come to a bad pass when two free citizens cannot speak their thoughts to each other" (II, IV).
   - *What the action does with it:* The lodge's "quarrelling toast" and brand are rituals that convert private conscience into collective obedience; Morris is threatened with the agenda paper for saying "Unwelcome truths" (II, III). The work shows the machinery of moral abdication — the wired triggers of the shotgun are its emblem, "made up his mind that he was going to take no chances" (I, IV) — and shows that the only man who keeps asking "What's the man done?" is the one who has already decided to destroy them.

## What it teaches

This is a book about who is allowed to be afraid. In Part I the fear belongs to a rich man in a moated house who raises his drawbridge every night; in Part II it belongs to a whole valley of shopkeepers, miners, editors and widows who dare not name the man who rules them. The two halves are joined by a single claim: that fear is a system, built and maintained by people who have found it cheaper than argument, and that it can be unbuilt only by someone willing to live inside it under a false name. The work admires that someone, and it is honest about what the admiration costs. Edwards stands at the door while a man is beaten; he lays a powder bag against a family's house. He says the valley will call him a deliverer, and the narrator does not contradict him, but the narrator also does not let him die in bed.

What is hard here is the question of complicity. Morris, who hates every murder and attends them anyway, is the most sympathetic man in Vermissa, and the book half-despises him — "You were too soft for the job." Edwards, who attends the same murders in order to end them, is the hero, and the book knows that from outside the two men look identical, standing side by side at the editor's door. The difference is intention, which cannot be seen, and result, which can only be seen later. The reader is asked to judge by the result and is then shown the result's price: a wife dead in California, a second wife widowed off St. Helena, and a "king devil" in London who has not been touched. If justice is a thing that happens in courtrooms, the book delivers it once — the Scowrers hang — and then shows that the courtroom is the smaller room.

There is also a quieter teaching about lies told for love. Douglas never tells his wife what he was; she knows anyway, from the way he looks at strangers. McMurdo never tells Ettie what he is; she trusts him anyway, and is right for the wrong reasons. Barker and Mrs. Douglas lie to the police for a living man and are read as adulterers. In every case the lie is protective and the protection fails, and in every case the person lied to turns out to have deserved the truth. The book does not say that honesty would have saved anyone. It does say, through Douglas's own mouth, "I would have been a wiser man if I had told her sooner," and leaves you to weigh that against the fact that telling her would not have stopped the ship.

## Lines worth keeping

- "so aloof is he from general suspicion, so immune from criticism, so admirable in his management and self-effacement" (I, I)
- "Knowing himself to be a traitor, he may have read the accusation in the other's eyes." (I, I)
- "Mediocrity knows nothing higher than itself; but talent instantly recognizes genius" (I, I)
- "Everything comes in circles... The old wheel turns, and the same spoke comes up. It's all been done before, and will be again." (I, II)
- "The temptation to form premature theories upon insufficient data is the bane of our profession." (I, II)
- "Can a husband ever carry about a secret all his life and a woman who loves him have no suspicion of it?" (I, V)
- "There was romance. There is always romance. There was nothing mysterious." (I, V)
- "A lie, Watson—a great, big, thumping, obtrusive, uncompromising lie—that's what meets us on the threshold!" (I, VI)
- "how often is imagination the mother of truth?" (I, VI)
- "I consider your case to be hopeless. I do not consider that it is hopeless to arrive at the truth." (I, VII)
- "It was death to the man that lost his grip." (I, VII)
- "If killing is murder, then God knows there is murder and to spare." (II, I)
- "pity and with sympathy—those two qualities which may turn so rapidly and so naturally to love." (II, II)
- "Unwelcome truths are not popular." (II, III)
- "I've the badge of shame on my forearm and something worse branded on my heart." (II, IV)
- "There is no honour in such a matter." "Well, well, it's just how you look at it." (II, V)
- "They were as hardened to human murder as a butcher to sheep." (II, VII)
- "It is crushing the nut with the triphammer—an absurd extravagance of energy—but the nut is very effectually crushed all the same." (Epilogue)

## Preoccupations

- respectability as the disguise of power
- fear as a system people live inside, not an event
- the informer and the weak link: conscience inside a criminal order
- the gap between what people say and what they do, including the narrator's judgments
- justice outside the law: who may kill, and what it costs them
- lies told to protect loved ones, and their failure
- obedience replacing moral judgment in a group
- the detective and the criminal as rival artists
- the long reach of the past: no escape by distance or by name
