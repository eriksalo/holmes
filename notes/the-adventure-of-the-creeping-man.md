# The Adventure of the Creeping Man

## What happens
Trevor Bennett, secretary to the eminent Camford physiologist Professor Presbury and engaged to his daughter Edith, brings Holmes a tangle of oddities: the sixty-one-year-old professor, newly and passionately engaged to a colleague's young daughter, vanished to Prague for a fortnight, came back furtive, receives secret letters marked with a cross, guards a small wooden box, and has been attacked three times by his own devoted wolf-hound. Bennett has seen him crawling along a corridor at two in the morning; Edith has seen his face at her second-floor window. Holmes notes the nine-day rhythm of the fits and the professor's thick, horny knuckles. On the ninth night, hidden in the garden, Holmes and Watson watch the professor drop to all fours, climb the ivy "in mere joy at his own powers," and taunt the chained dog until the collar slips and the animal tears his throat. The box holds a syringe and letters from a Prague scientist supplying a serum made from a climbing monkey: the professor has been injecting himself to become young enough for his bride. Holmes sees in it "a very real danger to humanity."

## What the work observes about being human

1. **A man who tries to make himself younger for love may lose the self that was loved.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "The real source lies, of course, in that untimely love affair which gave our impetuous Professor the idea that he could only gain his wish by turning himself into a younger man. When one tries to rise above Nature one is liable to fall below it." Bennett: "it's not he--it's never the man whom we have known."
   - What the action does with it: The story confirms the claim with grim literalness. The serum works: the professor is "stronger than I have known him for years," his lectures "as brilliant as ever," his brain never clearer. What it costs is everything else: the trust of his secretary, the love of his daughter, his dog's recognition, and finally nearly his life. The girl he did it for never appears; she is a reason, not a presence.

2. **Those closest to a person notice the change before the person admits it, and the animal notices first of all.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "A dog reflects the family life. Whoever saw a frisky dog in a gloomy family, or a sad dog in a happy one?" And: "The dog, of course, was aware of the change far more quickly than you. His smell would ensure that. It was the monkey, not the Professor, whom Roy attacked."
   - What the action does with it: Watson calls the dog theory "a little far-fetched," and the story proves it the central clue. Edith's "It was not my father with whom I lived. His outward shell was there, but it was not really he" is the human version of what Roy knew by scent. The professor's own view of himself (he is "quite himself" after each fit) is the least reliable account in the story.

3. **Loyalty to a person can require betraying their wishes.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "Certainly we must agree that the Professor has every claim upon his loyalty and devotion. But it may best be shown by taking the necessary steps to clear up this strange mystery." Bennett, handing over a blotting-paper address: "It is an ignoble position for a trusted secretary, but what else can I do?"
   - What the action does with it: Bennett spies on his master, admits detectives, reads his blotter, and is right to do it; the surveillance is what saves the professor's life. But the story also shows the price of this loyalty: the professor's rage at him, the "uncomfortable time" Holmes predicts, and the final decision to keep the whole matter secret "for God's sake" rather than call a surgeon. Bennett's loyalty ends by protecting the reputation rather than the man.

4. **Intelligence does not protect against folly; a clear mind can be in service of an unclear desire.**
   - Where it is spoken: Bennett: "And yet his mind is perfectly clear." Holmes: "Too clear! That was my miscalculation. It is evident that his memory is much more reliable than I had thought."
   - What the action does with it: The professor is a European authority on physiology, and he is injecting himself with an untested monkey serum from a man "tabooed by the profession because he refused to reveal its source." The story never suggests he does not understand the risk; the Prague letter explicitly warns him. His intellect is intact throughout and useless as a brake. "Explosive, no doubt," says Holmes, "but then from his point of view he has something to explode about."

5. **Cruelty for its own sake is the clearest mark of the loss of the human.**
   - Where it is spoken: Watson: "this impassive and still dignified figure crouching frog-like upon the ground and goading to a wilder exhibition of passion the maddened hound... by all manner of ingenious and calculated cruelty."
   - What the action does with it: The climbing is described as joy, but the scene with the dog is described as cruelty, and it is the cruelty that brings the punishment: the collar slips, "dog and man were rolling on the ground together." The coachman's flat line, "I knew the dog would get him sooner or later," says that this was not a one-time event. The story places the fall from humanity not in the crawling or the climbing but in the deliberate tormenting of a creature that loved him.

6. **A longer life for those who want it most would be a worse world.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "Consider, Watson, that the material, the sensual, the worldly would all prolong their worthless lives. The spiritual would not avoid the call to something higher. It would be the survival of the least fit. What sort of cesspool may not our poor world become?"
   - What the action does with it: This is Holmes's largest general claim in the story, and the plot supports only a small piece of it: one man, one serum, one disaster. Whether "the spiritual would not avoid the call" is asserted, not shown. The story offers the professor as a specimen and lets Holmes generalize; it also notes "I have one other client in England," so the professor is not unique. The claim is left as warning. Holmes's own remark, "It's surely time that I disappeared into that little farm of my dreams," sits oddly beside it: he too is a man thinking about age.

7. **Reputation is protected more carefully than health.**
   - Where it is spoken: Bennett, when Watson calls for a surgeon: "For God's sake, no! At present the scandal is confined to our own household... Consider his position at the University, his European reputation, the feelings of his daughter." Holmes: "Quite so."
   - What the action does with it: A man with a torn carotid region is kept from a specialist to avoid talk; Watson, a doctor, defers. The opening paragraph confirms the pattern: the facts were "entombed" for twenty years "to dispel once for all the ugly rumours." The story is told to clear a name, and it shows a household that would rather risk a death than a rumour. Nobody in the text questions this.

8. **Watson is a habit, and knows it.**
   - Where it is spoken: Watson: "He was a man of habits, narrow and concentrated habits, and I had become one of them. As an institution I was like the violin, the shag tobacco, the old black pipe... His remarks could hardly be said to be made to me--many of them would have been as appropriately addressed to his bedstead."
   - What the action does with it: Watson frames this as "my humble role," and the story bears it out: Holmes ignores him for half an hour, dismisses his lumbago theory, and gives him the diagnosis of the knuckles only after the fact. But Watson's practice is "by this time not inconsiderable," and his dressing of the professor's throat is the one indispensable thing anyone does that night. The story shows him as more than a whetstone while he describes himself as less.

## What it teaches
This is a story about a man who would not accept his age and the people who loved him enough to spy on him. The professor's crime, if it is one, is wanting what he cannot have and taking a shortcut through his own body to get it. He does not get it. The young woman he did it for is nowhere in the story; what is in the story is the dog, the daughter, and the secretary, each of whom loses him in a different way. The lesson the text draws is Holmes's: "When one tries to rise above Nature one is liable to fall below it." The lesson the action draws is less tidy. The professor's fall is not into animality in general but into cruelty in particular, and the thing he torments is the creature that knew him best. That specificity is where the story's real feeling lies.

What is hard to settle is the ending. Holmes delivers a speech on the survival of the least fit and then goes for tea. The professor lies morphined with his throat bandaged, his secret kept, his engagement presumably intact; no one says what happens to him or to the marriage. Bennett and Watson agree to hide the whole thing from the surgeon who should see him. The story opens by saying it exists to clear the professor's name, and it closes by showing how far decent people will go to protect a name. A thoughtful reader may feel the serum is not the only thing in this house that ought to have been poured out.

## Lines worth keeping
- "Come at once if convenient--if inconvenient come all the same."
- "He was a man of habits, narrow and concentrated habits, and I had become one of them."
- "You never learn that the gravest issues may depend upon the smallest things."
- "Whoever saw a frisky dog in a gloomy family, or a sad dog in a happy one?"
- "It was not my father with whom I lived. His outward shell was there, but it was not really he."
- "We can but try--the motto of the firm."
- "Always look at the hands first, Watson. Then cuffs, trouser-knees, and boots."
- "It's surely time that I disappeared into that little farm of my dreams."
- "climbing apparently in mere joy at his own powers, with no definite object in view."
- "I knew the dog would get him sooner or later."
- "When one tries to rise above Nature one is liable to fall below it."
- "It would be the survival of the least fit. What sort of cesspool may not our poor world become?"

## Preoccupations
- age refusing itself, and the cost of trying to be young
- the animal under the man
- those closest seeing the change first
- loyalty that requires betrayal
- reputation guarded above health and truth
- the long-time companion as habit
