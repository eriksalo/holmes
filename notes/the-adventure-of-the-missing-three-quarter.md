# The Adventure of the Missing Three-Quarter

## What happens
Cyril Overton, captain of the Cambridge rugby side, arrives at Baker Street in a panic: his star three-quarter Godfrey Staunton, "the hinge that the whole team turns on," has vanished from his London hotel the night before the Oxford match, after a bearded stranger brought a note that made him fall back "as if he had been pole-axed." Holmes, whose idle brain Watson has been anxiously watching for signs of the old drug craving, takes the case. A blotting-pad yields the tail of a telegram ("Stand by us for God's sake!"), Staunton's miserly uncle Lord Mount-James turns up to disclaim all expense, and a lie to a telegraph clerk gives Holmes the addressee: Dr. Leslie Armstrong of Cambridge, an eminent and forbidding scientist who refuses to answer questions, throws Holmes out, and then out-manoeuvres him for two days on the flat Cambridgeshire roads. Holmes finally tracks the doctor's brougham by squirting aniseed on its wheel and following with a draghound. The trail leads to a lonely cottage near Trumpington where Staunton is sobbing at the foot of a bed on which his secretly married wife, a landlady's daughter, has just died of consumption. Armstrong, who had hidden the marriage to protect the lad's inheritance, returns, is reconciled with Holmes by the promise of discretion, explains everything, and Holmes and Watson walk out of "that house of grief" having found no crime at all.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **An unoccupied mind can be more dangerous to its owner than any outward trouble.** Watson treats Holmes's idleness as a medical emergency.
   - *Where it is spoken:* Watson: "I knew by experience that my companion's brain was so abnormally active that it was dangerous to leave it without material upon which to work." And: "the fiend was not dead but sleeping... Therefore I blessed this Mr. Overton whoever he might be, since he had come with his enigmatic message to break that dangerous calm."
   - *What the action does with it:* The syringe does reappear, and Watson is "horrified by my first glimpse" of it, but Holmes has filled it with aniseed: "It is not upon this occasion the instrument of evil, but it will rather prove to be the key which will unlock our mystery." The case does what Watson hoped and keeps the drug asleep. But what Overton's "enigmatic message" leads to is a dead girl and a weeping husband. Watson blessed a telegram as a cure for his friend without a thought for what it was really about; the story lets his blessing stand and then shows the grief it was built on.

2. **Everyone lives inside a world that mistakes itself for the whole world.** The athlete, the scientist, and the detective each think the others' concerns absurd.
   - *Where it is spoken:* Overton: "Good Lord! Mr. Holmes, where _have_ you lived?" Holmes: "You live in a different world to me, Mr. Overton—a sweeter and healthier one." Armstrong: "I have no sympathy with these childish games... The football match does not come within my horizon at all."
   - *What the action does with it:* The match is lost, and the local paper attributes the defeat entirely to Staunton's absence, "whose want was felt at every instant of the game." The indispensable man was kneeling by a deathbed. Holmes, who began by calling amateur sport "the best and soundest thing in England," ends by saying "Personally I am in agreement with Dr. Armstrong, and football does not come within my horizon." The story quietly ranks the worlds: the public catastrophe of the game is nothing beside the private one, and the people who thought the game was everything never learn why.

3. **A secret kept for love looks, from outside, exactly like a secret kept for guilt.** Every reading of the evidence assumes crime.
   - *Where it is spoken:* Holmes lists the possibilities: "it might be worth someone's while to get at a player as the ruffians of the turf get at a race-horse," or "a plot to hold him for ransom." Of Armstrong: "I have not seen a man who, if he turns his talents that way, was more calculated to fill the gap left by the illustrious Moriarty." Later: "Oh, the cunning rascal!"
   - *What the action does with it:* The "cunning rascal" is a friend making daily visits to a dying woman; the elaborate detour that defeated Holmes was to protect a marriage, not a crime. Armstrong's explanation is pure devotion: "I knew the lad well, and I loved him for his many excellent qualities. I did all I could to help him to keep things straight." Holmes's suspicion was reasonable on every point and wrong on every point. The story shows that the same behaviours (concealment, evasion, a watch kept on followers) belong equally to the guilty and to those shielding something precious.

4. **Money and the fear of disinheritance decide whom a man may love in the open.** The miser's purse rules a marriage he never hears of.
   - *Where it is spoken:* Overton: "He never allowed Godfrey a shilling in his life, for he is an absolute miser, but it will all come to him right enough." Mount-James: "Don't look to me for a penny—not a penny!" Armstrong: "it was quite certain that the news of his marriage would have been the end of his inheritance."
   - *What the action does with it:* A wife "as good as she was beautiful and as intelligent as she was good" lives and dies hidden in a cottage because of money that has not yet been given and may never be. Mount-James's only moment of generosity ("a fiver or even a tenner") comes when Holmes frightens him about his own plate; his concern never once reaches the nephew's actual life. The uncle remains ignorant at the end. The story does not say whether the secret will hold, only that it cost a young woman her last months among people who could not be told she existed.

5. **Two honest men will read each other as villains the moment they stand in each other's way.** Holmes and Armstrong both assume the worst.
   - *Where it is spoken:* Armstrong: "I have heard your name, Mr. Sherlock Holmes, and I am aware of your profession—one of which I by no means approve." And, throwing him out: "You can tell your employer, Lord Mount-James..." Holmes, on first meeting Armstrong's hostility, half-jokes about Moriarty. Later Armstrong: "You are a good fellow... I had misjudged you."
   - *What the action does with it:* Neither deduction nor pursuit resolves the misreading; what resolves it is Holmes's unprompted promise ("you can absolutely depend upon my discretion") and Armstrong's "compunction at leaving poor Staunton all alone" that turned his carriage back. The handshake comes from a gesture of decency, not from being right. Holmes was in fact wrong about Armstrong, and Armstrong was in fact wrong about who employed Holmes; the story lets both errors stand until character, not evidence, undoes them.

6. **Public obligation can drag a person away from the one place he is needed, and a lie about a headache is the price.** Staunton goes to London because leaving the team would expose the secret.
   - *Where it is spoken:* Armstrong: "The poor boy was half crazed with grief, and yet he had to go to London to play this match, for he could not get out of it without explanations which would expose his secret." Overton: "He said he was all right—just a touch of headache."
   - *What the action does with it:* Overton reads his friend accurately ("He was a sportsman, was Godfrey, down to his marrow, and he wouldn't have stopped his training and let in his skipper if it were not for some cause that was too strong for him") and is wholly ignorant of what the cause is. The one word the porter catches is "time." The story builds the whole mystery out of a man who kept faith with his team for as long as he could and broke it only when told his wife was dying. His loyalty and his grief are never in conflict in his heart; they are in conflict only because the secret forbids him to explain.

7. **Detection is itself a trespass, and the story makes the detective confess it.** Armstrong's charge against Holmes's trade is never refuted by the plot.
   - *Where it is spoken:* Armstrong: "Where your calling is more open to criticism is when you pry into the secrets of private individuals, when you rake up family matters which are better hidden." Holmes, on blotting-paper: "the impression usually goes through—a fact which has dissolved many a happy marriage." Holmes to Armstrong at the end: "When a man is lost it is my duty to ascertain his fate, but having done so the matter ends so far as I am concerned."
   - *What the action does with it:* Holmes lies to a telegraph clerk to read another man's message, pockets Staunton's papers over the uncle's protest, sets a hound on a doctor's carriage, and walks into a stranger's house past a closed door because "We are bound to see what it means before he comes." He arrives to find a widower who takes him for a doctor "sent to his assistance." Nothing is prosecuted; nothing is gained but knowledge. Armstrong's rebuke ("you have attained your end and have certainly chosen a particularly delicate moment for your intrusion") is answered only by Holmes's silence afterwards. The story's last word is a man leaving a house he had no right to enter.

8. **Kindly concealment and blunt truth both claim to be mercy, and the story rewards the blunt one.** Armstrong withheld how near death was; the girl's father told.
   - *Where it is spoken:* Armstrong: "I did not tell him how urgent the danger was, for I knew that he could do no good here, but I sent the truth to the girl's father, and he very injudiciously communicated it to Godfrey."
   - *What the action does with it:* Because of the "injudicious" father, Godfrey was at the bedside, "kneeling at the end of her bed, until this morning death put an end to her sufferings." Armstrong's prudence would have left him in London playing a game while his wife died. The doctor is the story's wise man, and he calls the one act that got the husband home a mistake. The plot disagrees with him without saying so.

## What it teaches
This is a detective story in which there turns out to be no crime, and that absence is its subject. Every instrument of the trade is used, and used brilliantly, to break into a grief that was none of Holmes's business and that no one could have been helped by his finding. The story does not punish him for it; Armstrong forgives him, the secret stays safe, and Holmes behaves honourably once he understands. But look at what the honour consists of: promising to do nothing. The whole chase has produced a handshake and a walk out into winter sunlight. If you take the story at its word, the lesson is modest and uncomfortable: other people's concealments are not raw material, and the fact that you could find something out is no reason that you should. Watson wanted a case to keep his friend from the needle; the story grants the wish and then shows what a case is made of.

The harder thing it leaves you with is the question of who was right about secrecy. Staunton hid a good marriage from a bad uncle for money not yet in hand; Armstrong hid a dying woman from her husband for his own good; the girl's father broke that silence and was called injudicious. Only the last of these got anyone to where they needed to be. The story is full of people deciding what others should be spared from knowing, and it quietly suggests that the kindest of these decisions can be the cruelest. It does not resolve this. Mount-James never learns of the marriage; the inheritance may still come; the secret has become permanent by the simplest means, which is that the person it protected is dead.

- "You live in a different world to me, Mr. Overton—a sweeter and healthier one."
- "the impression usually goes through—a fact which has dissolved many a happy marriage"
- "Stand by us for God's sake!"
- "Don't look to me for a penny—not a penny!"
- "In that, Doctor, you will find yourself in agreement with every criminal in the country"
- "when you pry into the secrets of private individuals, when you rake up family matters which are better hidden"
- "It is not upon this occasion the instrument of evil, but it will rather prove to be the key which will unlock our mystery."
- "his shoulders bowed, his head sunk on his hands, the very image of distress"
- "a kind of drone of misery and despair which was indescribably melancholy"
- "Yes, yes, I am—but you are too late. She is dead."
- "No man need be ashamed of such a wife."
- "we passed from that house of grief into the pale sunlight of the winter day"

## Preoccupations
- secrets kept for love rather than guilt
- money and inheritance deciding whom one may love openly
- detection as an intrusion into private life
- separate worlds (sport, science, crime) each blind to the others
- idleness, addiction, and the mind that needs work
- duty to a team or public set against duty to one person
- who has the right to decide what another person should be told
