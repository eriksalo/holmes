# The Disappearance of Lady Frances Carfax

## What happens
An old governess reports that Lady Frances Carfax, a well-born, unmarried woman of forty who drifts between Continental hotels carrying her Spanish jewels with her, has missed five weeks of letters. Holmes, claiming to be detained in London, sends Watson to Lausanne, where Watson learns she fled after a bearded "savage" accosted her, picks up her trail to Baden, where she fell in with a saintly convalescent missionary, Dr. Shlessinger, and his wife, and goes on to Montpellier to find her former maid—where the savage attacks him and Holmes, disguised as a French workman, has to knock the man off. The savage is Philip Green, who has loved Lady Frances for twenty years and whom she refused for his youthful coarseness; the missionary is "Holy Peters," an Australian swindler who preys on pious lonely women. Back in London the trail is cold until a pendant is pawned. Green follows Mrs. Peters to an undertaker and then to a Brixton house where a coffin is delivered. Holmes forces his way in at gunpoint without a warrant, opens the coffin, and finds a stranger—a workhouse crone of ninety, dead of old age under a doctor's certificate; the police put him out. After a sleepless night he grasps that the coffin was built too large for its body, races to the house as the funeral leaves, tears off the lid, and finds Lady Frances chloroformed beneath the old woman. She is barely revived. The Peterses are gone before the warrant arrives.

## What the work observes about being human
1. **A person with means and no fixed ties is exposed in a way that rarely shows until something goes wrong.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Holmes: "One of the most dangerous classes in the world is the drifting and friendless woman... She is a stray chicken in a world of foxes. When she is gobbled up she is hardly missed."
   - *What the action does with it:* The structure of her life delivers her to Peters exactly as predicted—hotel to hotel, jewels in her trunk, bills paid by a stranger. But "friendless" is the wrong word, and the story shows it: a retired governess counts the weeks between letters, a maid weeps at leaving her, a man has crossed the world for her. She is missed within five weeks and nearly dies anyway. What exposes her is not the absence of love but the absence of anyone close enough to see.

2. **The rough face belongs to the devoted man and the saintly face to the predator; the narrator gets it exactly backwards.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Jules Vibart on Green: "_Un sauvage—un veritable sauvage!_" The Baden manager on Shlessinger: "the convalescent saint." Watson: "Here was this good and pious lady pursued from place to place by a sinister and unrelenting figure."
   - *What the action does with it:* Watson follows his chain to the end, confronts the savage in the street, and is half-strangled; Holmes's verdict is "I cannot at the moment recall any possible blunder which you have omitted. The total effect of your proceeding has been to give the alarm everywhere and yet to discover nothing." The man of iron grip wilts with anxiety for her; the man with the "air of superficial benevolence" has her drugged in an upper room. The story lets Watson narrate his own error in full before correcting it.

3. **Goodness can be the handle by which a person is seized.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Watson's report: "Like most lonely ladies, Lady Frances found her comfort and occupation in religion." Holmes on Peters: "His particular specialty is the beguiling of lonely ladies by playing upon their religious feelings."
   - *What the action does with it:* She nurses the fake invalid, helps his "wife," lets him pay her bills. The same couple turn her against the one wholly loyal person near her—the maid who "had even questioned her once as if she had suspicions of her honesty." Her trust and her suspicion both land on the wrong people. The story does not mock her piety; it shows how precisely it was aimed at.

4. **Moral fastidiousness in love can be its own danger; the woman who will not bear "a shadow of coarseness" flees the rough good man into the arms of the smooth bad one.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Green: "her mind was pure as snow. She could not bear a shadow of coarseness. So, when she came to hear of things that I had done, she would have no more to say to me. And yet she loved me—that is the wonder of it!—loved me well enough to remain single all her sainted days just for my sake alone."
   - *What the action does with it:* Her flight from Lausanne is a flight from Green, and it puts her on the road to Baden and Peters. Twenty years of refusal end with her being carried into a sickroom by the man she refused—"someone who has a better right to nurse this lady than we have." The story does not tell us whether she accepts him; it simply shows her strictness nearly killing her and his roughness nearly killing Watson, and relents toward both.

5. **The law's forms can be used to bury a living woman, and the man who keeps every form can taunt the man who breaks them.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Peters: "When a man's conscience is easy you can't rattle him." "Can you pick any hole in that, Mr. Holmes?" Holmes earlier: "here all is open and regular... they have done her to death in some way which has deceived the doctor and simulated a natural end."
   - *What the action does with it:* Workhouse permission, a real doctor, a real certificate, a licensed undertaker—every paper is in order, and the trick is simply one coffin "made to some special measurement" so that "Both would be buried under the one certificate." The doctor is satisfied: "there was no room for foul play." The story shows regularity itself as the weapon, and the forgotten old woman from the infirmary as its instrument: "the poor old woman who still lies in that coffin may go to her last resting-place alone."

6. **When the law is too slow, the detective breaks it, is put out by sympathetic policemen, and breaks it again; the story rewards the second breach and not the first.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Holmes: "'Thrice is he armed who hath his quarrel just.' We simply can't afford to wait for the police or to keep within the four corners of the law." Peters: "Why, you're a common burglar." Holmes: "So you might describe me." The sergeant: "Sorry, Mr. Holmes, but that's the law." Holmes: "Exactly, Sergeant, you could not do otherwise."
   - *What the action does with it:* The first forced entry finds the wrong corpse and ends in humiliation; the warrant is delayed overnight by "difficulties of procedure"; the second forced entry, still warrantless, saves her by minutes. Lestrade arrives with the paper to find "his birds have flown." The policeman apologizes for enforcing the law and then quietly watches the house for Holmes. Everyone in uniform agrees the law is right and acts as though it is not enough.

7. **The best mind can go dark at the moment it matters, and what saves the day is partly luck.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Peters: "Ah, you've blundered badly for once, Mr. Sherlock Holmes." Holmes: "Good heavens, Watson, what has become of any brains that God has given me?... I'll never forgive myself, never, if we are too late!" Afterwards: "Such slips are common to all mortals, and the greatest is he who can recognise and repair them."
   - *What the action does with it:* The clue—"It took longer, being out of the ordinary"—was in his hands all night. He wakes to it at 7:20 for an eight o'clock funeral and arrives after eight; she lives because "others were late as well as we." The man who told Watson "There is no 'perhaps' about it. I _have_ done better" spends the night prowling and the morning begging a hansom to hurry. The story shows his claim to "modified credit" as honest and also as all he is owed.

8. **Anxiety for someone else eats the body, and the cure for it is a task with limits.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Watson on Green: "His clothes were getting looser on his great body. He seemed to be wilting away in his anxiety. 'If you will only give me something to do!' was his constant wail." Green: "My nerves are like live wires."
   - *What the action does with it:* Unchecked, his love is violence—a wrist seized on a promenade, a stranger throttled in the street—and it drives the woman away and alarms the trail. Put "on your honour" with a job and a rule ("no indiscretion, and, above all, no violence"), he does the one piece of work that finds her and stops at the door because "I remembered my promise to you." The story shows the same force wrecking and saving depending on whether it has a shape.

## What it teaches
This is a story about reading people by their surfaces and getting it wrong, told by the man who gets it wrong. Watson's chapter of the inquiry is a clean demonstration of how a sympathetic observer builds a false story out of true facts: the bearded man did seize her wrist, the missionary was ill, the lady did flee. The error is in the assignment of good and evil to faces. The lesson is not that appearances always invert—Peters's "cruel, vicious mouth" is there to be seen—but that a person who wants a story will find one, and the story is more likely to fit his expectations than the world.

The harder thing the story leaves is about law and time. Everyone agrees the warrant is right, and the warrant would have arrived to an empty house and a filled grave. Holmes breaks in twice, is wrong once, and is right the second time by ten minutes and a late hearse. "Thrice is he armed who hath his quarrel just" is a fine line to say with a revolver in your pocket; the story shows the quarrel being just and the aim being wrong on the same evening. What a reader is left with is not a rule but a weighing: the forms protect the Peterses more than Lady Frances, and the man who sets the forms aside is saved from being a murderer's accomplice only by the undertaker's delay. The text does not pretend this is comfortable. It gives Holmes "modified credit" and buries the old woman alone.

- "She is a stray chicken in a world of foxes. When she is gobbled up she is hardly missed."

- "the last derelict of what only twenty years ago was a goodly fleet"

## Preoccupations
- the gap between how people look and what they are
- goodness and piety as a point of attack
- the law's forms used as cover, and the detective breaking them
- loneliness and exposure in a life without close ties
- love refused on moral grounds and what it costs
- the great mind's fallibility and the role of luck
- anxiety for another person and the need for a task
