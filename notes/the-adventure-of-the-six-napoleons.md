# The Adventure of the Six Napoleons

## What happens
Lestrade brings Holmes an "absurd business": someone is breaking cheap plaster busts of Napoleon, first in a shop, then by burglary in a doctor's house and surgery. Watson diagnoses an *idée fixe*; Holmes notes that the breaker knew where the busts were and broke them where he could see. Next morning the affair turns to murder: a journalist, Horace Harker, finds a man with his throat cut on his doorstep after his own bust is stolen and smashed under a street lamp. Lestrade works to identify the corpse (Pietro Venucci, a Mafia cut-throat) and builds a theory of Italian vendetta. Holmes traces the busts instead: six were cast at Gelder & Co. in Stepney, three sold through Morse Hudson and three through Harding Brothers; a workman called Beppo, who stabbed a man and was arrested at the factory while the busts were drying, has worked at one shop and had access to the other's sales book. Holmes plants a false story in the evening paper ("Murder by a Madman") so the criminal will feel safe, warns the Chiswick owner by letter, and catches Beppo smashing the fifth bust in the garden. Then he buys the sixth from Mr. Sandeford of Reading for ten pounds (the owner honestly admits he paid fifteen shillings), gets a signed transfer, breaks it on a white cloth with his hunting-crop, and holds up "the famous black pearl of the Borgias," hidden in the wet plaster a year before. Lestrade and Watson applaud; Holmes flushes and bows, and Lestrade tells him Scotland Yard is "very proud of you."

## What the work observes about being human

1. **Nothing is trivial until you know what it means; the smallest absurdity can be the surface of something large.** Three broken plaster heads turn out to conceal a famous jewel.
   - *Where it is spoken:* Holmes: "The affair seems absurdly trifling, and yet I dare call nothing trivial when I reflect that some of my most classic cases have had the least promising commencement." Lestrade: "it is such an absurd business, that I hesitated to bother you about it."
   - *What the action does with it:* The story is built to prove this. Lestrade thinks the busts are "petty larceny, six months at the most" and that "it is the murder that we are really investigating"; the busts turn out to be the whole case, and the murder a by-product of them. Holmes's "The busts! You never can get those busts out of your head" (Lestrade's complaint) is the sound of a man who has dismissed the one thread that leads anywhere.

2. **People explain what they cannot understand with the explanation they already have.** Madness, anarchism, the Mafia: each observer supplies his own.
   - *Where it is spoken:* Lestrade: "Madness, anyhow. And a queer madness, too." Watson: "There is the condition which the modern French psychologists have called the idée fixe." Morse Hudson: "A Nihilist plot—that's what I make it. No one but an anarchist would go about breaking statues." Lestrade again: "He is connected with the Mafia... Now, you see how the affair begins to clear up."
   - *What the action does with it:* Every one of these is a ready-made theory applied from outside, and each is partly right in an accidental way (Beppo is Italian, violent, and did have a fixation on the busts) and wholly wrong about the cause. Holmes's answer to Watson's monomania is the one piece of reasoning that stays close to the facts: "no amount of idée fixe would enable your interesting monomaniac to find out where these busts were situated." The story quietly ridicules the habit of naming a thing instead of understanding it.

3. **A man's life can be disposed of for a few shillings' worth of plaster, if the plaster might hold a fortune.** Pietro's throat is cut on a doorstep over a bust.
   - *Where it is spoken:* Holmes: "The possession of this trifling bust was worth more, in the eyes of this strange criminal, than a human life. That is one point."
   - *What the action does with it:* The story never gives Pietro any dignity in death: "an apple, some string, a shilling map of London, and a photograph" in his pockets, a Catholic emblem round his neck, a name supplied by an inspector who "makes a specialty of... the Italian Quarter." His sister was the maid suspected of the original theft. The pearl passes from a Prince's bedroom through a maid, a brother, and a sculptor into plaster, and the only one who dies for it is the poorest man in the chain. Holmes dismisses the question of how Beppo got it: "It is of no consequence to us which is the correct solution." The text leaves the dead man's story unexplained and unmourned.

4. **The press is an instrument, and truth is not its function.** Holmes feeds the newspaper a lie to make the criminal feel safe.
   - *Where it is spoken:* "Tell him for me that I have quite made up my mind, and that it is certain that a dangerous homicidal lunatic, with Napoleonic delusions, was in his house last night. It will be useful for his article." Later, reading the result: "The Press, Watson, is a most valuable institution, if you only know how to use it."
   - *What the action does with it:* Harker, the journalist, is drawn with gentle mockery: "all my life I have been collecting other people's news, and now that a real piece of news has come my own way I am so confused and bothered that I can't put two words together." He is the one man on the spot and the last to print, then prints Holmes's planted falsehood as settled fact ("there can be no difference of opinion upon this case"). The story treats the press as a thing to be worked rather than a record, and no one in it objects.

5. **Honesty in small things persists even among people who have nothing to gain by it.** Mr. Sandeford volunteers that he paid fifteen shillings for the bust Holmes offers ten pounds for.
   - *Where it is spoken:* "Well, I am an honest man, though not a very rich one. I only gave fifteen shillings for the bust, and I think you ought to know that before I take ten pounds from you." Holmes: "I am sure the scruple does you honour, Mr. Sandeford. But I have named that price, so I intend to stick to it."
   - *What the action does with it:* The story places this scrupulous little man immediately before the revelation of a pearl worth incalculably more than ten pounds, and has Holmes make him sign a transfer "of every possible right that you ever had in the bust." Holmes's "I am a methodical man, you see, and you never know what turn events might take afterwards" is a lawyer's caution against the man he has just praised for honesty. Sandeford's candour is rewarded with ten pounds and the loss of a fortune he never knew he owned. The text does not comment; the juxtaposition does.

6. **The reasoning machine wants applause, and the story catches it wanting.** Holmes bows like a dramatist.
   - *Where it is spoken:* Watson: "It was at such moments that for an instant he ceased to be a reasoning machine, and betrayed his human love for admiration and applause. The same singularly proud and reserved nature which turned away with disdain from popular notoriety was capable of being moved to its depths by spontaneous wonder and praise from a friend."
   - *What the action does with it:* Holmes stages the finale deliberately: white cloth, bust centred, hunting-crop, the shout of triumph, the pearl held up "like a plum in a pudding." He has known since the previous night what he would find, and has arranged the audience. Lestrade's tribute ("there's not a man... who wouldn't be glad to shake you by the hand") moves him more than Watson has ever seen; then "a moment later he was the cold and practical thinker once more." The story treats the need for recognition as a genuine human weakness in a man who pretends not to have it, and treats it with affection.

7. **Rivalry can turn to pride when one party stops needing to win.** Lestrade's "We're not jealous of you."
   - *Where it is spoken:* Lestrade: "I think I have done a better day's work than you. I have identified the dead man." Later: "We're not jealous of you at Scotland Yard. No, sir, we are very proud of you."
   - *What the action does with it:* Through the whole case Lestrade is wrong about what matters and is allowed by Holmes to pursue his Mafia theory without correction ("I suggest that you go on your line and I on mine"). His theory is not refuted at the end so much as absorbed: Beppo is Italian and did kill his confederate. The generosity of his closing speech is earned by Holmes's forbearance, and Holmes, in turn, offers only "a hint or two" in future. The relationship is drawn as one that has found its balance.

8. **Chance, acknowledged as chance, still governs the method.** Holmes names his odds.
   - *Where it is spoken:* "I have great hopes—in fact, the betting is exactly two to one—that if you will come with us to-night I shall be able to help you to lay him by the heels." And: "it all depends upon a factor which is completely outside our control."
   - *What the action does with it:* The story is unusual in making the detective calculate probabilities aloud and admit what he does not know ("I could not say that he had not found the pearl in Harker's bust. I had not even concluded for certain that it was the pearl"). The reasoning is sound and the result is a win, but the text makes clear that the win at Chiswick was a two-to-one bet, and that the pearl's being in the last bust was certain only by elimination. Confidence here is a matter of counting, not of genius.

## What it teaches
This is the lightest of the collection, and its lesson is accordingly about attention: the ridiculous thing in front of you may be the shape of something serious, and the cure for ridicule is to ask small concrete questions (why under a lamp? why these three busts?) rather than large explanatory ones (madness? anarchy? the Mafia?). Everyone around Holmes is quick with a category and slow with a fact, and the story makes the categories look foolish without making the people fools. Lestrade's instinct to identify the dead man is good police work; it simply leads to the wrong place because he has decided in advance that the murder is the case and the plaster is a nuisance.

What stays with a reader is at the edges. A poor Italian is killed on a doorstep over a jewel he may never have held, and the story cannot be bothered to work out his part ("It is of no consequence to us"). An honest man in Reading tells the truth about fifteen shillings and signs away a pearl. A journalist prints a lie because the detective found it useful. None of this is condemned; it is the furniture of a story whose attention is on the detective's triumph, and the triumph is real and beautifully staged. But the staging is the point: Holmes arranges the cloth and the crop and the witnesses because he wants to be applauded, and Watson sees it and loves him for it. The story is as much about the human need to be admired as it is about the pearl, and it is honest enough to show the machine blushing.

## Preoccupations
- the trivial as a disguise for the serious
- ready-made explanations versus concrete attention
- the detective's hunger for applause behind the mask of reason
- life valued cheaply against hidden wealth
- the press as a tool rather than a record
- rivalry between amateur and official turning into respect
