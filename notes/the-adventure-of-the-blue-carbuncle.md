# The Adventure of the Blue Carbuncle

## What happens
Two days after Christmas, Holmes is amusing himself by reading a stranger's life from a battered hat that the commissionaire Peterson picked up, along with a dropped goose, after a street scuffle. Then Peterson's wife finds in the goose's crop the Countess of Morcar's stolen blue carbuncle, for which a plumber, John Horner, already awaits trial on the word of a hotel attendant. Holmes advertises for the hat's owner, Henry Baker, a shabby scholar who proves to know nothing. Holmes and Watson trace the goose back through the Alpha Inn's goose club to a Covent Garden salesman, whom Holmes tricks with a bet into opening his ledger, and there meet the real thief, the hotel attendant James Ryder, frantically asking after the same birds. In Baker Street Ryder confesses: he and the maid framed Horner, he hid the stone in a goose at his sister's farm, and the wrong bird went to market. He begs for mercy. Holmes throws him out and lets him go, reasoning that without Ryder's testimony the case against Horner collapses.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **In a crowded city, chance alone will produce events as strange as any crime.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "Only one of those whimsical little incidents which will happen when you have four million human beings all jostling each other within the space of a few square miles. Amid the action and reaction of so dense a swarm of humanity, every possible combination of events may be expected to take place."
   - What the action does with it: The story confirms and then exceeds the claim. A stolen jewel travels from a hotel dressing-room to a Brixton goose-yard to Covent Garden to a Bloomsbury pub to a Tottenham Court Road gutter to Baker Street, carried by at least six people who do not know one another. "Chance has put in our way a most singular and whimsical problem." The chain is accidental at every link. What begins as "no crime" turns out to hinge on an innocent man's seven years.

2. **A person's history is written on what he owns, and reading it is an act of inference, not sight.**
   - Where it is spoken: Watson: "I can see nothing." Holmes: "On the contrary, Watson, you can see everything. You fail, however, to reason from what you see. You are too timid in drawing your inferences."
   - What the action does with it: Holmes reads decline, drink, lost affection and self-respect from a hat, and Henry Baker arrives matching the description: "a man of learning and letters who had had ill-usage at the hands of fortune," with cuffless wrists and a tremor in his hand. The story confirms the method but also shows its limit: the hat tells Holmes everything about Baker's fall and nothing about the stone, because Baker had nothing to do with it. The test that clears him is not deduction but an offer: he takes the fresh goose and laughs off the "disjecta membra" of the old one. Innocence shows itself in indifference.

3. **A beautiful object draws crime to it the way bait draws fish.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes, holding the stone: "Of course it is a nucleus and focus of crime. Every good stone is. They are the devil's pet baits. In the larger and older jewels every facet may stand for a bloody deed... Who would think that so pretty a toy would be a purveyor to the gallows and the prison?"
   - What the action does with it: The stone has already caused "two murders, a vitriol-throwing, a suicide, and several robberies" in twenty years. In this story it produces a frame-up, a wrongful arrest, and a man "without ever having touched the wealth for which I sold my character." The action confirms the claim, with the added irony that for most of the story the stone sits inside a goose, doing nothing, while men ruin themselves hunting it.

4. **The temptation of sudden, easy wealth defeats people who were not otherwise wicked.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes to Ryder: "the temptation of sudden wealth so easily acquired was too much for you, as it has been for better men before you; but you were not very scrupulous in the means you used." Ryder: "I never went wrong before! I never will again."
   - What the action does with it: The story takes Ryder's claim half-seriously. He is "a shrimp," without "blood enough to go in for felony with impunity," sweating at every policeman, unable even to keep track of a goose. But Holmes also says, "there is the making of a very pretty villain in you," and the frame-up of Horner was cold and deliberate. The story shows weakness and malice as the same quality seen from two sides: a man too frightened to be a good thief and not too frightened to send another man to prison.

5. **People will give for a bet what they will not give for money or a plea.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes, after losing a sovereign to Breckinridge: "When you see a man with whiskers of that cut and the 'Pink 'un' protruding out of his pocket, you can always draw him by a bet... if I had put £100 down in front of him, that man would not have given me such complete information as was drawn from him by the idea that he was doing me on a wager."
   - What the action does with it: The salesman has refused Ryder repeatedly and with fury; he refuses Holmes's direct question too. Pride in being right opens the ledger that neither bribe nor begging could. The story confirms that vanity is a more reliable lever than greed, and that the detective's skill lies as much in reading what a man wants to be as in reading what he is.

6. **Mercy to a frightened man may do more good than the law, and the one who grants it has no authority to.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "I am not retained by the police to supply their deficiencies. If Horner were in danger it would be another thing; but this fellow will not appear against him, and the case must collapse. I suppose that I am commuting a felony, but it is just possible that I am saving a soul. This fellow will not go wrong again; he is too terribly frightened. Send him to gaol now, and you make him a gaol-bird for life."
   - What the action does with it: Holmes's reasoning is practical, not sentimental, and the story backs him on the facts: Horner cannot be convicted without Ryder. But the story also shows what the mercy costs others. Horner has been arrested, fainted in court, and sits awaiting Assizes; the Countess's maid, Catherine Cusack, goes entirely unpunished and unmentioned. "It is the season of forgiveness" is said over a pipe, by a man who has decided the matter alone. The story endorses the outcome and leaves the authority for it where Holmes puts it: in himself.

7. **The guilty man's fear is for his parents' hearts, not for the man he has wronged.**
   - Where it is spoken: Ryder: "For God's sake, have mercy! Think of my father! Of my mother! It would break their hearts." Holmes: "It is very well to cringe and crawl now, but you thought little enough of this poor Horner in the dock for a crime of which he knew nothing."
   - What the action does with it: Holmes's rebuke is exact and the story lets it stand. But Holmes then releases Ryder for reasons that have nothing to do with Horner's suffering either; he never once mentions making amends to Horner, and Ryder's offer to "fly" and "leave the country" is accepted without any instruction to confess. The wronged man is cleared by default, not by justice. The story observes that even the merciful think about the person in front of them rather than the one in the cell.

## What it teaches
This is the gentlest story in the group, and the gentleness is earned by an unusual honesty about what Holmes is doing at the end. He names it: "I suppose that I am commuting a felony." He gives his reasons and they are good ones, and he also admits that the solution "is its own reward," which is to say that he solved this for pleasure and is disposing of its consequences by temperament. The story asks you to feel the rightness of letting a terrified small man run down the stairs on a cold night two days after Christmas, and it asks you not to forget that a plumber with a previous conviction has been in the dock for a week on that man's false word.

What is unresolved is the gap between the two acts of mercy the story contains. The first is Holmes's, for Ryder, and the story shows it. The second would be for Horner, and the story only promises that it will happen by default when the prosecution collapses. Nobody goes to tell Horner. Nobody mentions Catherine Cusack. The stone goes back to the Countess, the goose is eaten, and the two men sit down to dinner. The story's word for all this is "whimsical," and it is right, and the whimsy has a man in a cell at the edge of it.

## Lines worth keeping
- "Amid the action and reaction of so dense a swarm of humanity, every possible combination of events may be expected to take place."
- "On the contrary, Watson, you can see everything. You fail, however, to reason from what you see. You are too timid in drawing your inferences."
- "Every good stone is. They are the devil's pet baits. In the larger and older jewels every facet may stand for a bloody deed."
- "Who would think that so pretty a toy would be a purveyor to the gallows and the prison?"
- "Shillings have not been so plentiful with me as they once were."
- "It is my business to know what other people don't know."
- "It is always awkward doing business with an alias."
- "the temptation of sudden wealth so easily acquired was too much for you, as it has been for better men before you"
- "I am myself a branded thief, without ever having touched the wealth for which I sold my character."
- "you thought little enough of this poor Horner in the dock for a crime of which he knew nothing."
- "I suppose that I am commuting a felony, but it is just possible that I am saving a soul."
- "Send him to gaol now, and you make him a gaol-bird for life. Besides, it is the season of forgiveness."

## Preoccupations
- chance as the engine of events in a crowded city
- mercy exercised privately, outside the law
- the lure of sudden wealth
- reading a life from its objects
- the weak man as both victim and villain
- the forgotten innocent at the edge of the story
