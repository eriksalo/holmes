# The Five Orange Pips

## What happens
On a night of equinoctial gale, young John Openshaw comes to Baker Street with a family history. His uncle Elias, a former Confederate colonel who left America after the war, received an envelope from Pondicherry containing five dried orange pips and the letters "K. K. K."; he burned a box of papers, made a will, drank, raved, and seven weeks later was found drowned in two feet of water. Two years later John's father received the same pips from Dundee with the instruction "Put the papers on the sundial," ignored it, and died in a chalk-pit within days. Now John has received the pips from London. The police have smiled at him. Holmes identifies the Ku Klux Klan, deduces that the senders travel by sailing ship, and tells John to put the one surviving page in the brass box on the sundial at once. John leaves and is found drowned near Waterloo Bridge that night. Holmes traces the barque Lone Star, posts five pips to its captain, and wires Savannah. The ship is lost with all hands in the Atlantic; the letter is never received.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **The forces that threaten people are older and larger than the civilisation that pretends to contain them.**
   - Where it is spoken: Watson's opening: "even here in the heart of great, hand-made London we were forced to raise our minds for the instant from the routine of life and to recognise the presence of those great elemental forces which shriek at mankind through the bars of his civilisation, like untamed beasts in a cage."
   - What the action does with it: The story confirms the frame twice over. The Klan, which Openshaw's father dismisses with "We are in a civilised land here, and we can't have tomfoolery of this kind," kills him anyway. And then the storm, the actual elemental force, destroys the Klansmen in turn. Neither law nor reason gets to finish the story. The civilised instruments (police, encyclopaedia, Lloyd's register, a letter to Savannah) all arrive too late or never arrive.

2. **A person who believes he is safe because he is reasonable is in more danger than one who is afraid.**
   - Where it is spoken: Joseph Openshaw: "Some preposterous practical joke... What have I to do with sundials and papers? I shall take no notice of such nonsense." And on the police: "And be laughed at for my pains. Nothing of the sort."
   - What the action does with it: The uncle, who was terrified, lasted seven weeks and might have survived had he not drunk himself out of the house. The father, who scoffed, lasted days. The son, who was frightened but passive ("I have felt helpless. I have felt like one of those poor rabbits when the snake is writhing towards it"), lasted two days after the letter. None of the three attitudes saved anyone, but the story is hardest on the man who refused to believe. Reason here is a way of not looking.

3. **Guilt does not fade with distance or years; it waits.**
   - Where it is spoken: Elias Openshaw on seeing the pips: "'My God, my God, my sins have overtaken me!'" And his will: "If you can enjoy it in peace, well and good! If you find you cannot, take my advice, my boy, and leave it to your deadliest enemy."
   - What the action does with it: The story confirms the uncle's cry literally. But it also makes the inheritance of guilt a monstrous thing: the father and son did nothing in America and die for papers they never read and which no longer exist. The will's phrase "a two-edged thing" is exactly right. The uncle's "sins" are only hinted at (he "took a strong part in opposing the carpet-bag politicians"; the diary records that "Paramore" was "visited" after others "cleared"), and the story leaves the reader to understand that the man who first sent pips is now receiving them.

4. **Knowledge and reasoning can be complete and still fail to save anyone.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "The ideal reasoner would, when he had once been shown a single fact in all its bearings, deduce from it not only all the chain of events which led up to it but also all the results which would follow from it." And: "Problems may be solved in the study which have baffled all those who have sought a solution by the aid of their senses."
   - What the action does with it: Holmes does exactly this. From three postmarks he deduces a sailing ship, a gang, and a timetable, and he is right in every particular. He identifies the Lone Star and its three American officers in a single day. And John Openshaw is already dead before Holmes finishes breakfast. Watson's opening admits the case has "points in connection with it which never have been, and probably never will be, entirely cleared up." The story confirms the power of reason and shows its uselessness against speed and darkness.

5. **Failure, for the proud, is felt as an injury to the self before it is felt as grief for the other.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes, on reading of Openshaw's death: "That hurts my pride, Watson. It is a petty feeling, no doubt, but it hurts my pride. It becomes a personal matter with me now... That he should come to me for help, and that I should send him away to his death—!"
   - What the action does with it: Holmes names his own feeling accurately and calls it petty, and then the sentence breaks off into something more like grief. Watson records him "more depressed and shaken than I had ever seen him," and later "pale and worn," having forgotten to eat. The story lets the two feelings coexist and does not sort them. The revenge Holmes plans, sending the pips back, is a gesture of pride; the wire to Savannah is justice. Both are swallowed by the sea.

6. **Vengeance, even the detective's, borrows the methods of the enemy.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "Young Openshaw shall not long remain unavenged. Why, Watson, let us put their own devilish trade-mark upon them. It is well thought of!" He writes "S. H. for J. O." inside the flap and says, "It may give him a sleepless night."
   - What the action does with it: Earlier Holmes told Openshaw, "Do not think of revenge, or anything of the sort, at present. I think that we may gain that by means of the law." Now he adopts the Klan's own ritual of terror. The story does not comment, but the letter never arrives, so the gesture is cancelled by events: the one thing Holmes does that imitates the killers is the one thing that comes to nothing.

7. **Human plans, good and evil alike, have a flaw in them that nothing can plan for.**
   - Where it is spoken: Watson: "There is ever a flaw, however, in the best laid of human plans, and the murderers of John Openshaw were never to receive the orange pips which would show them that another, as cunning and as resolute as themselves, was upon their track."
   - What the action does with it: The Klan's plan worked three times; Holmes's plan would have worked; the storm made both irrelevant. Watson's final sentence, "that is all which we shall ever know of the fate of the _Lone Star_," refuses even the satisfaction of knowing. The story gives the reader a dead client, dead murderers, and no human agent responsible for the second fact. Whether this is justice or merely weather is left for the reader to decide.

## What it teaches
This is the story in which the detective does everything right and loses. Holmes's reasoning is impeccable and his urgency is real; he sends Openshaw home with the correct advice, and Openshaw is killed between Baker Street and Waterloo. What the story asks you to sit with is the gap between understanding and power. To know who the killers are, where they are going, and how they travel is not to be able to stop them. Holmes himself feels this as a wound to pride, and the story is unusually honest about that: the feeling is petty and it is also the engine of what little he can do.

The harder thing is the ending. The Lone Star goes down with all hands and Watson calls this a "flaw" in Holmes's plan rather than a judgment on the Klansmen. There is no triumph in it, and no one to hold accountable, and the one surviving page of the register, with its record of men "cleared" and "visited," is presumably at the bottom of the Atlantic too. The story began with a storm shrieking at civilisation through its bars, and it ends with the storm having the last word. If there is a lesson, it is that the structures of reason and law are real and worth having and will not always be enough, and that a person who comes to you for help may die before you can give it, through no fault that can be named.

## Lines worth keeping
- "those great elemental forces which shriek at mankind through the bars of his civilisation, like untamed beasts in a cage."
- "I have been beaten four times—three times by men, and once by a woman."
- "None of those which come to me are. I am the last court of appeal."
- "'My God, my God, my sins have overtaken me!'"
- "If you find you cannot, take my advice, my boy, and leave it to your deadliest enemy. I am sorry to give you such a two-edged thing."
- "'We are in a civilised land here, and we can't have tomfoolery of this kind.'"
- "I have felt like one of those poor rabbits when the snake is writhing towards it."
- "You must act, man, or you are lost. Nothing but energy can save you."
- "we have our web to weave, while theirs is already woven."
- "That hurts my pride, Watson. It is a petty feeling, no doubt, but it hurts my pride."
- "There is ever a flaw, however, in the best laid of human plans."
- "that is all which we shall ever know of the fate of the _Lone Star_."

## Preoccupations
- the limits of reason against speed, darkness and chance
- guilt inherited by the innocent
- the thinness of civilisation over older violence
- disbelief as a form of danger
- pride as the detective's wound and motive
- justice that arrives, if at all, by accident
