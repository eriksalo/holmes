# The Man with the Twisted Lip

## What happens
Watson is sent by a friend's wife to fetch her opium-addicted husband from a den in Upper Swandam Lane, and finds Holmes there in disguise, investigating the disappearance of Neville St. Clair, a prosperous Kent householder whose wife saw him at a second-floor window of the den, collarless and agitated, before he vanished. The room held his clothes, bloodstains on the sill, and a hideous beggar named Hugh Boone, who is arrested. St. Clair's coat, weighted with hundreds of coppers, is found in the mud at low tide. Holmes tells Mrs. St. Clair frankly that he believes her husband dead; she produces a letter from him posted that day. After a night on five pillows and an ounce of shag, Holmes drives to Bow Street with a bath-sponge, washes the sleeping beggar's face, and reveals Neville St. Clair, who confesses that he has been Hugh Boone for years, earning far more by begging than he could by honest work, and has hidden it from his wife and children. The police agree to hush the matter up if Boone never appears again.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **A habit is easier to begin than to end, and its ruin falls on those who love the addict more than on himself.**
   - Where it is spoken: Watson on Isa Whitney: "He found, as so many more have done, that the practice is easier to attain than to get rid of, and for many years he continued to be a slave to the drug, an object of mingled horror and pity to his friends and relatives." Whitney in the den: "Good heavens! I thought it was Wednesday. It is Wednesday. What d'you want to frighten a chap for?"
   - What the action does with it: The Whitney frame confirms the claim and then is dropped; Watson sends him home in a cab and the story never returns to him. The real subject turns out to be a different kind of habit, St. Clair's begging, which also began as an experiment ("It was only by trying begging as an amateur") and became a life. Both men are hidden from their wives in the same building. The story pairs an addiction everyone recognises with one no one would name, and lets the structure do the arguing.

2. **People in grief go to the person who will receive them, not to the person who can solve the problem.**
   - Where it is spoken: Watson: "Folk who were in grief came to my wife like birds to a lighthouse." Kate Whitney: "I didn't know what to do, so I came straight to you."
   - What the action does with it: Watson's wife does nothing in the case but is the reason the case reaches Holmes at all. Mrs. St. Clair plays the same role at the other end: she insists on plain answers ("Do not trouble about my feelings. I am not hysterical, nor given to fainting") and her certainty of her husband's survival is the one thing Holmes cannot reason away. The story shows that the solver depends on the receivers, and that the women who open the door are doing a kind of work the detective cannot.

3. **Respectability is a performance that can be put on and taken off like paint.**
   - Where it is spoken: St. Clair: "I could every morning emerge as a squalid beggar and in the evenings transform myself into a well-dressed man about town." And Holmes's revelation: "Let me introduce you to Mr. Neville St. Clair, of Lee, in the county of Kent."
   - What the action does with it: The story literalises the claim: the face "peeled off under the sponge like the bark from a tree." But it also complicates it. The gentleman and the beggar are the same man; neither is the disguise. St. Clair has a real house, a real wife, real children, a real bank balance of £220, and real takings of £700 a year. Holmes, too, is in disguise in the den, and St. Clair was once an actor. The story does not say which face is true. It says the respectable villa in Lee is paid for by the twisted lip in Threadneedle Street.

4. **Pity pays better than labour, and the market for it is a market like any other.**
   - Where it is spoken: St. Clair: "you can imagine how hard it was to settle down to arduous work at £2 a week when I knew that I could earn as much in a day by smearing my face with a little paint, laying my cap on the ground, and sitting still. It was a long fight between my pride and the money, but the dollars won at last." Holmes, earlier: "a small rain of charity descends into the greasy leather cap."
   - What the action does with it: The story confirms the economics without approving them. St. Clair notes that his "facility of repartee" improved his takings; charity, in this account, rewards wit and ugliness rather than need. The 421 pennies and 270 half-pennies in the coat are a day's compassion from the City, counted. The story does not resolve whether St. Clair has defrauded his donors or simply given them what they paid for. It records that he says "the dollars won" and lets the phrase stand.

5. **The deepest shame is not before the law but before one's children.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "You would have done better to have trusted your wife." St. Clair: "It was not the wife; it was the children... God help me, I would not have them ashamed of their father." And: "I would have endured imprisonment, ay, even execution, rather than have left my miserable secret as a family blot to my children."
   - What the action does with it: The story takes this seriously. St. Clair preferred to be charged with his own murder, and let his wife spend a week believing him dead, rather than be identified as a beggar. Holmes's reproach is gentle and St. Clair's correction of it is allowed to stand. The action shows a man whose sense of honour is strong enough to accept a gaol cell and weak enough to let his wife suffer; the story does not call this hypocrisy. It calls it what it is: shame organised around status rather than conduct.

6. **A woman's intuition about someone she loves may outrun the analyst's reasoning, and the analyst should admit it.**
   - Where it is spoken: Mrs. St. Clair: "There is so keen a sympathy between us that I should know if evil came upon him... Do you think that I would respond to such a trifle and yet be ignorant of his death?" Holmes: "I have seen too much not to know that the impression of a woman may be more valuable than the conclusion of an analytical reasoner."
   - What the action does with it: She is right and he is wrong. He told her "Frankly, then, madam, I do not" believe her husband alive, and she produced the letter. Later Holmes calls himself "one of the most absolute fools in Europe." The story confirms her instinct, with the irony that her "keen sympathy" never once detected that the man she lived with begged for a living. Intuition saw that he was alive; it did not see who he was.

7. **The law can be persuaded to look away when the offence is only against appearances.**
   - Where it is spoken: St. Clair: "If I am Mr. Neville St. Clair, then it is obvious that no crime has been committed, and that, therefore, I am illegally detained." Holmes: "No crime, but a very great error has been committed." Bradstreet: "If the police are to hush this thing up, there must be no more of Hugh Boone."
   - What the action does with it: The inspector agrees to suppress the matter on the strength of an oath. The begging prosecutions St. Clair has already had were, he says, nothing: "what was a fine to me?" The story ends with a bargain, not a judgment. Hugh Boone is abolished by promise, and the household at Lee goes on. Whether Mrs. St. Clair is ever told is not said.

## What it teaches
What this story leaves you with is the question of which man is the real one. St. Clair's confession is calm and complete, and nothing in it is a lie; he was a schoolmaster's son, an actor, a reporter, a beggar, a husband, and a father, in that order, and he was all of them with the same face under different paint. The story's great visual moment, the sponge taking the beggar's face off, feels like a revelation, but the man revealed is sad and ashamed rather than freed, and his first act is to scream and hide his face in the pillow. Unmasking is not the same as rescue.

The harder thing is the economic honesty. St. Clair tells you plainly that pity paid seven times what honest work did, and that pride lost to money after "a long fight." He is not a monster and the story does not treat him as one; he is a man who found the market's price for his own degradation and took it. Nobody in the story is in a position to judge him: the police need no more Hugh Boones, the wife needs her husband, the children need not to know. The story ends on breakfast. You are left to decide whether anything has been put right, or whether a man has simply been allowed to keep one face and promised to burn the other.

## Lines worth keeping
- "the practice is easier to attain than to get rid of"
- "Folk who were in grief came to my wife like birds to a lighthouse."
- "I came to find a friend." "And I to find an enemy."
- "You have a grand gift of silence, Watson. It makes you quite invaluable as a companion."
- "I have seen too much not to know that the impression of a woman may be more valuable than the conclusion of an analytical reasoner."
- "it is better to learn wisdom late than never to learn it at all."
- "It was a long fight between my pride and the money, but the dollars won at last."
- "My dear wife knew that I had business in the City. She little knew what."
- "It was not the wife; it was the children... I would not have them ashamed of their father."
- "I would have endured imprisonment, ay, even execution, rather than have left my miserable secret as a family blot to my children."
- "Many times; but what was a fine to me?"
- "I reached this one by sitting upon five pillows and consuming an ounce of shag."

## Preoccupations
- respectability as performance and what pays for it
- shame before family versus guilt before the law
- the economics of pity
- addiction and the double life
- a woman's intuition against the reasoner's conclusion
- the law choosing to look away
