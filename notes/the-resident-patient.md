# The Resident Patient

## What happens
Watson opens by confessing the historian's difficulty: the cases where Holmes shines are often trivial, and the dramatic ones are often those where "the share which he has himself taken in determining their causes has been less pronounced than I, as his biographer, could wish." This is one of the latter. A nervous young physician, Percy Trevelyan, explains how a stranger named Blessington bought him a Brook Street practice in exchange for three quarters of his earnings and the right to live upstairs as a "resident patient." Weeks ago Blessington went into a panic over a newspaper "burglary"; then a supposed Russian nobleman with catalepsy and his Herculean son came twice to consult Trevelyan, and on each visit the son slipped upstairs into Blessington's rooms. Holmes goes to Brook Street, is met by a pistol in the dark, asks Blessington who the men are, is lied to, and walks out with the advice "to speak the truth." By seven the next morning Blessington is hanging from the lamp-hook. The inspector calls it suicide; Holmes reads four cigar-ends and a scratched lock and reconstructs a night-time tribunal of three men admitted by the page, followed by an execution. By afternoon he has names: Blessington was Sutton, the informer of the Worthingdon bank gang, on whose evidence one comrade was hanged and three were imprisoned; the three, released early, have hunted him down. Holmes declares that the "sword of justice is still there to avenge." The coda reports that the three were never seen again, are only "surmised" to have drowned off Portugal, and that the case against the page "broke down for want of evidence."

## What the work observes about being human

1. **A shameful secret can be more terrifying to a man than the death it is bringing on him.**
   - Where it is spoken: Trevelyan: "From his manner it struck me that he was in mortal dread of something or somebody, but when I questioned him upon the point he became so offensive that I was compelled to drop the subject." Holmes afterwards: "His secret was a shameful one, and he could not bring himself to divulge it."
   - What the action does with it: Blessington himself sends for Holmes ("It was his suggestion that I should come round to you"), stands on the stair with a pistol, weeps in an armchair, and still, with the one man who could save him in the room, says "But I have told you everything." He is hanged that night. The story confirms Holmes's reading exactly, but the confirmation is a corpse. The shame of having been an informer outlasts the instinct for survival; Blessington would rather be guarded by a lie than rescued by the truth.

2. **Fear is legible even when speech is not, and reading it is not the same as being able to help.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "I can read in a man's eye when it is his own skin that he is frightened for. It is inconceivable that this fellow could have made two such vindictive enemies as these appear to be without knowing of it." And: "It is just possible that to-morrow may find him in a more communicative mood."
   - What the action does with it: Holmes is right about the fear and right that Blessington knows his enemies, and he leaves anyway, with "a gesture of disgust," calling the evening "a fool's errand." Tomorrow does not find Blessington in a more communicative mood; it finds him dead. The story does not blame Holmes, but it places his pride in his own perception next to a man he correctly perceived to be in mortal danger and then abandoned to it. Insight without patience saves no one.

3. **Capital buys talent, and the arrangement can make both men rich and both men sick.**
   - Where it is spoken: Blessington: "It's the old story. More in your brains than in your pocket, eh?... it's just like any other speculation, and safer than most... I'll sink them in you." Trevelyan: "I may say with confidence that he never had occasion to regret his speculation... I have made him a rich man."
   - What the action does with it: The speculation pays. Yet Trevelyan is thirty-four with "a haggard expression and unhealthy hue [which] told of a life which has sapped his strength and robbed him of his youth," and he admits he has "got in the way of late of holding as little communication with him as possible" with the man who made his career. The money that founded the practice turns out to be a bank robber's share of seven thousand pounds and a murdered caretaker. The story shows a success story from the outside and a quiet revulsion from the inside, without ever having Trevelyan say why.

4. **Professional expertise is itself a hunger, and a hunger can be baited.**
   - Where it is spoken: Trevelyan on the "cataleptic" stranger: "My first feeling, as I have just said, was one of pity and horror. My second, I fear, was rather one of professional satisfaction." Holmes: "A fraudulent imitation, Watson, though I should hardly dare to hint as much to our specialist. It is a very easy complaint to imitate. I have done it myself."
   - What the action does with it: The letter is addressed to the one authority on catalepsy in London, and he admits it "interested me deeply, because the chief difficulty in the study of catalepsy is the rareness of the disease." The specialist is deceived precisely in his specialty; the "filial anxiety" that touched him was a performance by a man with "the limbs and chest of a Hercules." The story's point is not that Trevelyan is a fool but that wanting a rare case is a door anyone can walk through.

5. **Criminals keep their own court, and its memory is longer than the law's.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "it is evident to me that a consultation of some sort was held. Probably it was something in the nature of a judicial proceeding." And: "When they got out the other day, which was some years before their full term, they set themselves, as you perceive, to hunt down the traitor and to avenge the death of their comrade upon him."
   - What the action does with it: Three men sit and smoke for an hour before hanging the fourth, with a block and pulley brought along as a gallows. The form of the thing — hanging, as Cartwright was hanged — answers the betrayal in kind. Fifteen years in prison have not diluted the loyalty of thieves to a dead comrade; the only thing the state's punishment produced was time to plan. The story is not sentimental about them ("cold-blooded murder"), but it lets the ritual stand as a grim mirror of the courtroom that Sutton's evidence once filled.

6. **The law's shield can fail, and its sword can fail too.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "wretch as he was, he was still living under the shield of British law, and I have no doubt, Inspector, that you will see that, though that shield may fail to guard, the sword of justice is still there to avenge."
   - What the action does with it: The coda flatly refutes the confidence: "From that night nothing has been seen of the three murderers by the police," their drowning is a surmise, and the page goes free "for want of evidence." The shield failed the night before; the sword never falls. If there is a reckoning it is the sea's, not the court's, and even that is rumour. Holmes's stately sentence is the kind of thing a man says at the end of a case; the story's last paragraph is what actually happened.

7. **A changed name and a changed body do not escape a past that can arrive in a newspaper.**
   - Where it is spoken: Trevelyan on Blessington: "He was very fat, but had apparently at some time been much fatter, so that the skin hung about his face in loose pouches." And: "No doubt the day on which he was perturbed was the day when he had seen of their release in the newspapers." Holmes: "His talk about a burglary was the merest blind."
   - What the action does with it: Sutton has become Blessington: a new name, a reclusive life as an invalid, a body so altered that his skin hangs loose, wealth hidden in a box because "I would never trust a banker." All of it is undone by a paragraph in a paper and a pair of men who do not even know his daily routine. The story treats disguise as something a man can do to himself for years and still lose in a morning.

8. **Watson's own warning about this story turns out to be accurate.**
   - Where it is spoken: Watson: "It may be that in the business of which I am now about to write the part which my friend played is not sufficiently accentuated; and yet the whole train of circumstances is so remarkable that I cannot bring myself to omit it entirely."
   - What the action does with it: Holmes deduces the cigar-smokers' seating plan and the thief's shoe-length with his usual flourish, but he prevents nothing, catches no one, and his one intervention — walking out on a lying client — precedes the murder. The narrator's preface is not false modesty; it is a correct description of a story in which the detective is mostly a brilliant reader of what has already happened. The story knows the limits of its hero.

## What it teaches
The hard thing in this story is how little anyone's cleverness is worth against a man's refusal to say what he is ashamed of. Holmes reads Blessington's fear in his eye, reads the footprints on the stair, and reads the whole night's tribunal from four cigar ends — and all of this reading happens on either side of a death it could not prevent, because the one thing that needed reading was a secret the man would not give up. The story places shame above fear in the hierarchy of what moves people: Blessington will arm himself, weep, send for help, and still lie, because telling the truth would mean saying out loud that he sold a comrade to the gallows. What the story asks a reader to sit with is that this is not strange. People do hold to a lie while the house burns, and the detective's "My advice to you, sir, is to speak the truth" is both the right advice and, offered with disgust and a turned back, useless.

The other thing the story leaves unresolved is where justice is. Holmes closes with a sentence about the sword of British law, and the text then reports that the law caught nobody and proved nothing. The three murderers held a court of their own with a cigar-holder and a pulley and carried out a sentence that the real court, on Sutton's evidence, had carried out on their friend. The story does not approve of them, but it does not pretend the state did better. If the reader wants to believe the three drowned off Oporto, the story offers only "it is surmised." That is the story's honesty: it is a tale of a wretched man who was killed by other wretched men, solved completely and punished by nobody, told by a narrator who warned us in the first paragraph that his friend's part would be small.

## Preoccupations
- the past catching up with a man who has changed his name
- shame stronger than fear, and secrets kept at the cost of life
- justice outside the law, and the law's failure to guard or avenge
- money and talent bound together in a bargain that sickens both parties
- expertise as a blind spot
- the detective's limits: reading perfectly what he could not prevent
