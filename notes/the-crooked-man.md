# The Crooked Man

## What happens
Holmes arrives at Watson's door near midnight, lectures him on the "meretricious" effect of his sketches, and lays out the death of Colonel James Barclay of the Royal Mallows at Aldershot. Barclay, risen from the ranks, was devoted to his beautiful wife Nancy; the regiment thought them a model couple, though he was prone to fits of black depression and could not bear to be alone after dark. On Monday evening Nancy returned from a charity meeting in a strange temper, locked herself in the morning-room with her husband, was overheard screaming "You coward!... Give me back my life," and was found insensible beside his corpse, his skull cut on the fender, a carved club on the floor, his face set in horror, and the door-key gone. Holmes finds the footprints of a man who ran across the lawn and of a long, short-legged animal that climbed the curtain after a canary, and learns from Nancy's companion Miss Morrison that on the walk home they met a bent, crippled man who cried "My God, it's Nancy!" Holmes tracks him to lodgings: Henry Wood, once the handsomest corporal in the regiment and Nancy's true love, whom Sergeant Barclay, his rival, sent through the rebel lines during the Mutiny and betrayed to the enemy by a native servant. Tortured into deformity, Wood lived thirty years in Asia rather than let Nancy see him crawl; returning home, he followed the Barclays, saw the quarrel through the window, burst in with his mongoose, and Barclay dropped dead of terror at the sight. The inquest finds apoplexy; Holmes decides there is no object in raking up the dead man's crime; and Watson finally asks who "David" was.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **A marriage that looks like a model from outside can be built on a buried crime, and the husband's devotion can be the shape of his guilt.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes, reporting Major Murphy: "they were regarded in the regiment as the very model of a middle-aged couple." "Barclay's devotion to his wife was greater than his wife's to Barclay. He was acutely uneasy if he were absent from her for a day." And: "the smile had often been struck from his mouth, as if by some invisible hand."
   - What the action does with it: Every oddity the officers noted — the depressions, the fear of being alone after dark, the clinging to his wife — turns out to be the visible edge of a betrayal thirty years old. The story does not say Barclay did not love Nancy; it shows that his love and his terror were the same thing, and that the regiment read the symptoms as temperament. Nancy's cry, "Give me back my life," names what the marriage was from her side once she knew.

2. **Conscience can kill, and the story lets the moral and the medical explanation stand side by side.**
   - Where it is spoken: Wood: "It was a just providence that killed him... If his own guilty conscience had not struck him down it is likely enough that I might have had his blood upon my soul." And: "The bare sight of me was like a bullet through his guilty heart." Major Murphy: "death was due to apoplexy. You see it was quite a simple case after all." Holmes: "Oh, remarkably superficial."
   - What the action does with it: Barclay's face is set in "the most dreadful expression of fear and horror which a human countenance is capable of assuming." The inquest says apoplexy; Wood says providence; Holmes says the inquest is superficial but does not supply the other half. The story gives the reader both accounts of the same death and declines to adjudicate, which is its own statement: the physical cause and the moral cause are not competitors.

3. **Pride can keep a man dead to everyone he loves for thirty years, and homesickness is what finally overcomes it.**
   - Where it is spoken: Wood: "I had rather that Nancy and my old pals should think of Harry Wood as having died with a straight back, than see him living and crawling with a stick like a chimpanzee. They never doubted that I was dead, and I meant that they never should." And: "But when one gets old one has a longing for home. For years I've been dreaming of the bright green fields and the hedges of England."
   - What the action does with it: "Even my wish for revenge would not make me do that." Wood's exchange with Nancy under the lamp — "I thought you had been dead this thirty years, Henry." "So I have." — states the cost. He kept his silence out of vanity about his body and let the woman who loved him live with the man who destroyed him; the story does not call this noble or foolish. What breaks the vow is not justice or love but the green fields. The story seems to say that the longings which move us at the end are smaller and older than the ones we think govern us.

4. **Betrayal and courage can both be done for love, and the woman is the prize rather than the agent.**
   - Where it is spoken: Wood: "There were two men that loved her, and one that she loved." And: "There were a thousand lives to save, but it was of only one that I was thinking when I dropped over the wall that night." Of Nancy's father: "set upon her marrying Barclay. I was a harum-scarum, reckless lad, and he had had an education."
   - What the action does with it: Wood volunteers for a suicidal errand thinking of Nancy; Barclay hands him to the enemy thinking of Nancy. The father arranges her marriage on prospects. The story's parallel to David and Uriah, delivered as the last line, makes the structure plain: a man sends his rival to die to take his wife. But Nancy herself never speaks a line directly in the story. She is overheard through a door, reported by a friend, and then silenced by brain fever. The woman the plot revolves around has no voice in it.

5. **The investigator's reflex is to suspect an affair, and the story again shows that reflex to be wrong.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "My first conjecture was, that possibly there had been some passages between this young lady and the old soldier, which the former had now confessed to the wife."
   - What the action does with it: Holmes dismisses the idea on the evidence, but it is where his mind went first, as in other cases. The truth is both more terrible and more honourable than adultery: a thirty-year-old treachery and a lover who chose exile. The story's pattern — the shameful explanation offered and discarded — is a running correction to the reasoner's imagination of other people's lives.

6. **Justice, as Holmes practises it, can mean burying the truth to protect the dead and the living.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes to Wood: "It's every man's business to see justice done." And later: "there is no object in raking up this scandal against a dead man, foully as he has acted. You have at least the satisfaction of knowing that for thirty years of his life his conscience bitterly reproached him for this wicked deed."
   - What the action does with it: Holmes solves the case and then suppresses it. Barclay keeps his reputation and his regiment's honour; Wood keeps his secret; Nancy's acquittal comes by the inquest rather than by the truth. Whether Nancy is ever told what Holmes knows, or whether she and Wood meet again, the story does not say. "Justice done" here means no one is charged and the betrayal stays private, with thirty years of a guilty man's depression offered as the sentence already served.

7. **A promise given in friendship can rightly be broken when keeping it would destroy the friend.**
   - Where it is spoken: Miss Morrison: "I promised my friend that I would say nothing of the matter, and a promise is a promise... but if I can really help her when so serious a charge is laid against her, and when her own mouth, poor darling, is closed by illness, then I think I am absolved from my promise."
   - What the action does with it: The "little, ethereal slip of a girl" turns out to be "by no means wanting in shrewdness and common sense," and her reasoning is the clearest ethical statement in the story. Her breach of confidence is what saves Nancy from the dock. The story treats a kept secret and a kept promise as different things, and lets a minor character show the difference.

8. **The narrator's art is accused of cheating, and the story then does the thing it was accused of.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "the effect of some of these little sketches of yours, which is entirely meretricious, depending as it does upon your retaining in your own hands some factors in the problem which are never imparted to the reader." And at the end: "That one word, my dear Watson, should have told me the whole story had I been the ideal reasoner which you are so fond of depicting."
   - What the action does with it: Watson withholds the meaning of "David" until the final lines, exactly the trick Holmes complained of, and Holmes himself admits he missed it. The frame is a small confession about storytelling: the detective's brilliance and the narrator's suspense both depend on what is kept back, and the "ideal reasoner" is a portrait rather than a man. Watson's own admitted motive — "that half-sporting, half-intellectual pleasure" — sits oddly beside a widow in brain fever, and the story lets it sit.

## What it teaches
The story's deepest observation is that a life can be lived for thirty years in the aftermath of a single act, and that the people around will read the aftermath as personality. Barclay's gloom, his superstition, his clinging devotion, were a man carrying a dead comrade; the regiment thought he was moody. Wood's silence was a man carrying his own ruined body; Nancy thought he was dead. Both men's secrets held until chance — a charity meeting, a quiet street, one lamp — put them in the same circle of light. The thoughtful reader is left with the question of what the people they know are carrying, and with the uneasy knowledge that the things we call character are often the visible part of a history.

What is unresolved is Nancy. She is the person wronged by both men — the one who betrayed for her and the one who hid from her — and she ends the story unconscious, acquitted by a coroner's word, with Holmes deciding on everyone's behalf that the truth need not come out. Wood's pride and Barclay's guilt both chose for her; now the detective does. Whether burying the scandal is mercy or one more decision made over a woman's head, the story does not say, and the last line turns the whole thing into a Bible reference and a joke about rusty knowledge. That lightness is the story's way of walking away from a room it has shown us but will not stay in.

## Preoccupations
- the buried past returning
- guilt lived out as character
- pride that keeps a man hidden from those he loves
- betrayal done for love
- the woman at the centre who never speaks
- justice as suppression of the truth
- the narrator's method questioned by the hero
