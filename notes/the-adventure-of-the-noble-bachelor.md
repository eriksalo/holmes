# The Adventure of the Noble Bachelor

## What happens
Lord Robert St. Simon, forty-one, second son of a duke whose pictures have been sold, has married Hatty Doran, daughter of a Californian gold millionaire. At the wedding breakfast she pleads indisposition, goes upstairs, puts on an ulster and bonnet, and vanishes. A former dancer, Flora Millar, who had made a scene at the door, is arrested after Hatty is seen walking with her in Hyde Park. Lestrade drags the Serpentine and finds the wedding dress, with a note in the pocket signed "F. H. M." Holmes, who formed his conclusion before St. Simon arrived, reads the hotel bill on the back of the note, finds Francis Hay Moulton at a Northumberland Avenue hotel, and brings him and Hatty to Baker Street. She explains: she married Frank secretly in a mining camp, believed him killed by Apaches, agreed to marry St. Simon out of duty, and saw Frank alive in the front pew as she reached the altar. She dropped her bouquet to receive his note and followed him. St. Simon refuses supper and leaves; Holmes dines with the Americans.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **Marriage between old rank and new money is a transaction, and everyone knows it but no one says so.**
   - Where it is spoken: The society paper: "the Californian heiress is not the only gainer by an alliance which will enable her to make the easy and common transition from a Republican lady to a British peeress." St. Simon: "A fair dowry. Not more than is usual in my family." Holmes: "And this, of course, remains to you, since the marriage is a _fait accompli_?" St. Simon: "I really have made no inquiries on the subject."
   - What the action does with it: St. Simon's denial is immediately undercut by his own words about his wife's "immense social stride" and "so much that many have aspired to without success." Holmes's final verdict names the loss plainly: "deprived in an instant of wife and of fortune." The story confirms the transaction and shows that the man who made it cannot admit, even to himself, that fortune was part of it.

2. **Love does not obey the will, but conduct can.**
   - Where it is spoken: Hatty: "I felt all the time that no man on this earth would ever take the place in my heart that had been given to my poor Frank. Still, if I had married Lord St. Simon, of course I'd have done my duty by him. We can't command our love, but we can our actions."
   - What the action does with it: The story confirms her principle in the breach. She intended duty and would have performed it; the sight of a living husband dissolves the second contract instantly: "I never doubted for a moment that my first duty was now to him." Her account shows duty following the prior claim, not the feeling, which is why St. Simon's wound is both real and beside the point. She would have been a faithful wife to a man she did not love, and the story treats that as an ordinary thing.

3. **A prior claim is a prior claim, whether to a goldfield or a person.**
   - Where it is spoken: St. Simon: "Lady St. Simon said something about 'jumping a claim.' She was accustomed to use slang of the kind. I have no idea what she meant." Holmes: "claim-jumping—which in miners' parlance means taking possession of that which another person has a prior claim to."
   - What the action does with it: The slang St. Simon dismisses is the whole solution, and the metaphor is exact. Hatty's father broke the first engagement when "the richer Pa grew the poorer was Frank"; Frank left to make his pile "and never come back to claim me until he had as much as Pa." The story runs marriage, mining and inheritance on the same vocabulary, and the man who thinks himself above the vocabulary is the one who does not understand what has happened to him.

4. **The aristocrat's secret and the dancer's grievance are the same scandal seen from two incomes.**
   - Where it is spoken: St. Simon on Flora: "We have been on a friendly footing for some years—I may say on a _very_ friendly footing... I have not treated her ungenerously, and she had no just cause of complaint against me, but you know what women are, Mr. Holmes." Hatty, in passing: "Some woman came talking something or other about Lord St. Simon to me—seemed to me from the little I heard as if he had a little secret of his own before marriage also."
   - What the action does with it: Flora is arrested, suspected of murder, and dropped from the story without apology; her only crime was making a scene. St. Simon's past is referred to as a "very friendly footing" and costs him nothing. The story does not comment. It places Hatty's one glancing sentence where the reader will notice that the man complaining of a "public slight" had hired plainclothes police to keep his own history off the church steps.

5. **Pride wounded cannot accept that no one meant to wound it.**
   - Where it is spoken: St. Simon: "It was a slight, sir, a public slight." Holmes: "It is the purest accident. I cannot allow that there is any humiliation... I fail to see that anyone is to blame." St. Simon: "I will make no allowance. I am very angry indeed, and I have been shamefully used."
   - What the action does with it: Hatty's apology is complete and generous ("I guess you have every cause to be"), and he will not look at her. He shakes her hand "coldly," refuses supper, and leaves. The story gives him the last stiff word and then gives Holmes a gentler one: "perhaps you would not be very gracious either." The action shows a man whose code requires him to feel insulted by an accident, and the story asks for mercy toward him without asking the reader to admire him.

6. **Openness is braver than flight and costs more.**
   - Where it is spoken: Frank Moulton: "we've had just a little too much secrecy over this business already. For my part, I should like all Europe and America to hear the rights of it." Hatty: "Frank was all for openness, but I was so ashamed of it all that I felt as if I should like to vanish away."
   - What the action does with it: Holmes takes Frank's side and makes the couple face St. Simon. The scene is painful and achieves nothing visible: St. Simon forgives no one. Yet the story treats this as the right outcome, and Hatty's account shows that her flight ("It was awful to me to think of all those lords and ladies sitting round that breakfast-table") was shame, not malice. The action confirms that doing the open thing can be correct and still fail to repair anything.

7. **The official mind reasons from objects to the nearest story; the detective reasons from people to the likeliest one.**
   - Where it is spoken: Lestrade: "it seemed to me that if the clothes were there the body would not be far off." Holmes: "By the same brilliant reasoning, every man's body is to be found in the neighbourhood of his wardrobe." And: "Lady St. Simon is a myth. There is not, and there never has been, any such person."
   - What the action does with it: Lestrade's note points to Flora; Holmes turns it over and reads a hotel bill. Lestrade drags a lake; Holmes reasons that a bride who was willing at the altar and gone in ten minutes must have seen someone, and that someone must be American. The story confirms Holmes but also shows he needed Lestrade's bag: "friend Lestrade held information in his hands the value of which he did not himself know." The rivalry is real and the dependence is mutual.

## What it teaches
Nobody in this story does anything wicked, and that is what makes it uncomfortable. Hatty was faithful to a dead man and then to a living one. Frank kept his word across a continent and a captivity. St. Simon married for money and position and would have been, by his lights, a decent husband. Flora loved a man who called her "a dear little thing" and was arrested for it. Everyone behaves according to a code, and the codes do not fit together, and the person with the oldest and grandest code ends up with the least.

The story's quiet instruction is in Holmes's two speeches at the end: first, that we should "judge Lord St. Simon very mercifully," and second, the hope that the children of Britain and America will one day be "citizens of the same world-wide country." The first is kindness toward a man who could not be kind; the second is a hope that the vocabulary of claims and prizes and "charming invaders" might one day not apply to people. The story offers both and knows they are not yet true. The supper for five is eaten by four.

## Lines worth keeping
- "This looks like one of those unwelcome social summonses which call upon a man either to be bored or to lie."
- "the status of my client is a matter of less moment to me than the interest of his case."
- "One by one the management of the noble houses of Great Britain is passing into the hands of our fair cousins from across the Atlantic."
- "They often vanish before the ceremony, and occasionally during the honeymoon; but I cannot call to mind anything quite so prompt as this."
- "I was amused by her society, and she could see that I was amused."
- "I have not treated her ungenerously, and she had no just cause of complaint against me, but you know what women are, Mr. Holmes."
- "jealousy is a strange transformer of characters."
- "Lady St. Simon is a myth. There is not, and there never has been, any such person."
- "We can't command our love, but we can our actions."
- "The richer Pa grew the poorer was Frank."
- "I may be forced to acquiesce in these recent developments, but I can hardly be expected to make merry over them."
- "perhaps you would not be very gracious either, if, after all the trouble of wooing and wedding, you found yourself deprived in an instant of wife and of fortune."

## Preoccupations
- marriage as transaction between rank and money
- duty against love, and the prior claim
- the double standard between a lord's past and a woman's
- wounded pride that cannot accept an accident
- openness versus flight
- the official mind against the detective's
