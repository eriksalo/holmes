# The Adventure of the Lion's Mane

## What happens
Holmes, retired to the Sussex coast with his bees and housekeeper, narrates this one himself because Watson "had passed almost beyond my ken." Walking with his neighbour Stackhurst, who runs a coaching school, he sees the young science master Fitzroy McPherson stagger up the cliff path and die, his back covered in whip-like weals, gasping "the lion's mane." The mathematics coach Ian Murdoch, a taciturn man with a ferocious temper who once hurled McPherson's dog through a window, is the obvious suspect: he kept the students back that morning, he had loved the same woman, Maud Bellamy, and he is seen leaving her house. Maud reveals she and McPherson were secretly engaged. McPherson's dog dies in agony at the same spot. Holmes, stuck for a week "at the limit of my powers," remembers a phrase in a naturalist's book, and the inspector arrives to urge Murdoch's arrest just as Murdoch himself staggers in with the same wounds. Holmes leads them to the bathing-pool and finds the killer: a giant jellyfish, Cyanea capillata, washed in by the gale. He crushes it with a boulder. Murdoch, cleared, explains he had been the lovers' go-between.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **People will build a murderer out of an unlikeable man because a crime needs a culprit.**
   - Where it is spoken: Inspector Bardie: "There is really no one else when you come to think of it. That's the advantage of this solitude. We narrow it down to a very small compass. If he did not do it, then who did?" Murdoch, after: "I do not blame you, Inspector, nor you, Mr. Holmes, for your suspicions were natural."
   - What the action does with it: Holmes, Stackhurst, and the inspector all converge on Murdoch for the same reasons: temper, oddity, jealousy, the dog incident, his wish to leave. Every fact is true and every inference is wrong. Holmes himself admits "Suspicion, vague and nebulous, was now beginning to take outline in my mind." The story shows how a character reading substitutes for evidence, and it saves Murdoch only by nearly killing him.

2. **The aloof and ill-tempered can be the most loyal; coldness of manner is not coldness of heart.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes on Murdoch: "so taciturn and aloof that none can be said to have been his friend." Murdoch: "I have lost to-day the only person who made The Gables habitable." And: "from the day when she chose my friend McPherson my one desire was to help her to happiness. I was well content to stand aside and act as their go-between."
   - What the action does with it: The man who threw a dog through a window has spent a year carrying love-letters for his rival and rushes to tell the woman of the death "lest someone should forestall me in a more sudden and heartless manner." The story confirms his loyalty absolutely and never softens his manner; Stackhurst's "We shall understand each other better in the future" is the only reconciliation, and it comes from the man who had just sacked him.

3. **A woman's silence to protect a man is read as guilt, by men who assume she must be hiding something shameful.**
   - Where it is spoken: Maud: "I would rather not answer that question. It has really nothing to do with the matter which you are investigating." Murdoch: "She would not tell you, sir, of our relations lest you should disapprove and I might suffer."
   - What the action does with it: Her one withheld fact (that Murdoch carried the note) was withheld for his protection, and it was the fact that would have cleared him at once. The story shows discretion backfiring on the person it was meant to shield. It also gives Maud the clearest judgement in the case: "No single person could ever have inflicted such an outrage upon him." She is right, and nobody follows her reasoning.

4. **Family "protection" of a woman is often control, and she knows the difference.**
   - Where it is spoken: Bellamy: "I object to my girl picking up with men outside her own station." Maud: "So I would, father, if you had ever shown sympathy... It was your prejudice against him which prevented us from telling you." And: "This is my business, William. Kindly leave me to manage it in my own way."
   - What the action does with it: The father and brother who call McPherson's attentions "insulting" are themselves briefly suspected by the inspector. The story gives Maud the room's authority: she overrides her brother, answers Holmes in her father's presence against his wish, and is called "a most complete and remarkable woman" by a narrator who says women "have seldom been an attraction to me." The men who claim to guard her are, in the story's eyes, the obstacle.

5. **The mind that holds everything can lose anything; knowledge is useless until memory surrenders it.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "My mind is like a crowded box-room with packets of all sorts stowed away therein--so many that I may well have but a vague perception of what was there." And: "You have known what it was to be in a nightmare in which you feel that there is some all-important thing for which you search and which you know is there, though it remains for ever just beyond your reach."
   - What the action does with it: The solution was in Holmes's garret all along, in a book he had read. It took a dead man's last words, a dead dog, and a week of failure to shake it loose. The story confirms Holmes's description of his own mind and also shows its limit: "In all my chronicles the reader will find no case which brought me so completely to the limit of my powers." A reader is told, by Holmes himself, how near he came to failing.

6. **One wrong assumption early poisons every inference after it.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "It was the towel which misled me. The poor fellow had never thought to dry himself, and so I in turn was led to believe that he had never been in the water. Why, then, should the attack of any water creature suggest itself to me? That was where I went astray."
   - What the action does with it: Confirmed and admitted. A folded dry towel is read as "never entered the water," and from that a human assailant becomes necessary, and from that a suspect. Holmes refuses the inspector's praise ("To accept such praise was to lower one's own standards") and closes on a joke against himself: "Cyanea Capillata very nearly avenged Scotland Yard." The narrator-detective is more candid about his errors than Watson ever lets him be.

7. **Nature is indifferent and sufficient; a storm can do what no human malice could.**
   - Where it is spoken: Inspector: "It don't belong to Sussex." Holmes: "Just as well for Sussex. It may have been the south-west gale that brought it up." And: "all Nature was newly washed and fresh" on the morning of the death.
   - What the action does with it: The story opens in a pastoral: bees, the Downs, a "soothing life of Nature for which I had so often yearned." The thing that kills McPherson and his dog is part of that same nature, brought by the same weather that made the morning beautiful. Holmes treats the jellyfish as a "murderer" and "ends" it with a boulder, language of justice applied to something that cannot be guilty. The retired detective's peace was always shared with what came in on the tide.

## What it teaches
The Lion's Mane is a story in which there is no villain, and everyone behaves as though there must be one. The grief-stricken headmaster sacks his best teacher; the inspector wants to arrest him; Holmes himself searches his rooms. All of it is reasonable and all of it is wrong, and the story lets you feel how reasonable it was. The one person who argues from the body rather than from character, Maud Bellamy, is overruled by her father and ignored by the investigators. The lesson is uncomfortable for anyone who has ever judged by manner: the unpleasant man was the faithful friend, the thing that looked like a flogging was a sting, and the "solitude" that made the case seem simple was exactly what made it misleading.

The other thing the story leaves you is Holmes alone. He is retired, he says women have never attracted him, his friend is gone, and he nearly lets an innocent man be arrested because a towel was dry. He tells you so. The retirement he yearned for has brought a case to his door and a death to his beach, and the peace he describes in the first paragraph is not the peace the story shows. Whether he knows that is not clear; he ends with a joke. But the reader who has watched him stand "alone by that place of death" with the thing "for ever just beyond your reach" has seen something Watson's stories never quite showed: a great mind at the edge of its own limits, with no one to tell it so.

## Lines worth keeping
- "Ah! had he but been with me, how much he might have made of so wonderful a happening."
- "My house is lonely. I, my old housekeeper, and my bees have the estate all to ourselves."
- "He seemed to live in some high, abstract region of surds and conic sections with little to connect him with ordinary life."
- "I have lost to-day the only person who made The Gables habitable."
- "Women have seldom been an attraction to me, for my brain has always governed my heart."
- "So I would, father, if you had ever shown sympathy."
- "No single person could ever have inflicted such an outrage upon him."
- "In all my chronicles the reader will find no case which brought me so completely to the limit of my powers."
- "My mind is like a crowded box-room with packets of all sorts stowed away therein."
- "I feel that on the very eve of my arrest I have only cleared myself by sharing the fate of my poor friend."
- "To accept such praise was to lower one's own standards."
- "It was the towel which misled me."

## Preoccupations
- suspicion built from character rather than evidence
- the unlikeable man who is the loyal one
- a woman's silence protecting a man and endangering him
- the limits of a great mind, admitted by its owner
- nature as killer without malice
- solitude in retirement
