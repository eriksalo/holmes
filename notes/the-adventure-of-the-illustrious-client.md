# The Adventure of the Illustrious Client

## What happens
An intermediary, Sir James Damery, brings Holmes a case on behalf of a client who refuses to be named: Violet de Merville, a general's daughter, is about to marry Baron Adelbert Gruner, an Austrian who almost certainly murdered his first wife and has ruined many women. She has heard every accusation and dismisses all of them. Holmes visits the Baron, who warns him off; brings the Baron's discarded mistress Kitty Winter to confront Violet, who freezes them both out; is beaten nearly to death in Regent Street by the Baron's hirelings; and, feigning worse injuries than he has, uses Watson posing as a china collector to distract Gruner while he burgles the Baron's locked "lust diary." Kitty Winter, brought along for the burglary, throws vitriol in Gruner's face. The diary ends the engagement; Kitty gets a light sentence; Holmes is never prosecuted, because the client is illustrious enough that "the rigid British law becomes human and elastic."

## What the work observes about being human

1. **A person in love can hear every fact against the beloved and be moved by none of them; argument does not reach where feeling lives.**
   - Where it is spoken: Damery: "The cunning devil has told her every unsavoury public scandal of his past life, but always in such a way as to make himself out to be an innocent martyr. She absolutely accepts his version and will listen to no other." Violet: "the opinion of all the world is no more to me than the twitter of those birds outside the window."
   - What the action does with it: The story confirms it brutally. Holmes pleads "with all the warmth of words that I could find in my nature" and "could not bring one tinge of colour to those ivory cheeks." Kitty's raw testimony as a living victim fails too. Only the Baron's own handwriting, the diary, breaks the spell, and even that works offstage: Watson never sees it happen. The story grants that no outside voice could do it; the lover had to be confronted with the beloved's own record.

2. **Charm is a weapon, and the most dangerous cruelty wears good manners.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "Some people's affability is more deadly than the violence of coarser souls." And: "a real aristocrat of crime, with a superficial suggestion of afternoon tea and all the cruelty of the grave behind it."
   - What the action does with it: Confirmed. Gruner's "velvet way" has convinced Violet, once convinced Kitty ("I loved him myself at that time. Whatever he did went with me"), and in his interview with Holmes he is amused rather than threatened. The one honest sign is physical: Watson sees "a murderer's mouth... a cruel, hard gash," which Gruner has tried to hide under a moustache. The body tells what the manner conceals.

3. **Love and hate come from the same root, and the discarded lover knows the beloved better than the current one.**
   - Where it is spoken: Kitty: "It's not out of love for you I'm speaking. I don't care a tinker's curse whether you live or die. It's out of hate for him and to spite him." Holmes on seeing the two women: "If ever you saw flame and ice face to face, it was those two women."
   - What the action does with it: Kitty is the story's only reliable witness to Gruner's inner life: the diary, the murders hinted "in his velvet way," his habits. She is also the one who finally punishes him, outside Holmes's plan ("it seems she had some of her own"). The story depends on her hatred to produce both the truth and the justice, but it also shows that hatred disfiguring her as much as him: she is "so worn with sin and sorrow that one read the terrible years."

4. **Victims become perpetrators, and the law cannot cleanly sort them.**
   - Where it is spoken: Kitty: "what I am Adelbert Gruner made me. If I could pull him down!... if I could only pull him into the pit where he has pushed so many!"
   - What the action does with it: She does pull him down, with vitriol, and the story refuses to let anyone be fully clean. Watson, tending the screaming Baron, "could have wept over the ruin had I not remembered very clearly the vile life which had led up to so hideous a change." The court gives her the lowest possible sentence; Holmes faces a burglary charge that quietly evaporates. The ending names this openly: justice here is done by crimes, and forgiven unevenly.

5. **Men collect; women are among the things collected.**
   - Where it is spoken: Kitty: "this man collects women, and takes a pride in his collection, as some men collect moths or butterflies. He had it all in that book." Damery: "He collects books and pictures... a recognized authority upon Chinese pottery."
   - What the action does with it: The story turns the collector's mania against him. Gruner can resist every moral appeal but cannot resist a Ming saucer: "He has the collection mania in its most acute form." The same appetite that ruins women gets him burgled. And the saucer itself comes from "the collection of his client," so the trap is one collector's prize used against another's.

6. **Hidden power bends the law; justice is "elastic" for those with the right friends.**
   - Where it is spoken: Watson, closing: "when an object is good and a client is sufficiently illustrious, even the rigid British law becomes human and elastic. My friend has not yet stood in the dock."
   - What the action does with it: Holmes at first refuses the case because of mystery "at both ends," then takes it anyway; he keeps the client's incognito even from Watson at the end ("Let that now and for ever be enough for us"). The story is frank that the outcome, a thwarted marriage and a stolen diary with no consequence for the thief, depends on rank. Watson's tone is complacent; the facts he reports are not.

7. **Even the closest friend is kept at a distance by the person he loves most.**
   - Where it is spoken: Watson: "There was a curious secretive streak in the man... I was nearer him than anyone else, and yet I was always conscious of the gap between."
   - What the action does with it: Holmes lies to Watson about his recovery, sends him on an errand (the pottery) with no explanation, and springs Kitty on him. Watson obeys "by long experience." The friendship works, but only because one party accepts being used. The gap Watson names at the start of the drying-room scene ("less reticent and more human than anywhere else") is the gap the whole story keeps.

8. **People cannot be saved against their will; the rescue has to go around them.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "if her poor old broken father cannot move her, how shall I, a stranger, prevail?" and later, "It is his moral side, not his physical, which we have to destroy."
   - What the action does with it: Violet is never persuaded; she is confronted with a document and the engagement ends in a newspaper paragraph. Her father, the general, "has lost the nerve which never failed him on the battlefield." The story lets the strong-willed daughter be "saved," but she never consents to it and never speaks again after her icy interview. What she feels at the end is left blank.

## What it teaches
This is a story about the limits of telling the truth. Everyone tells Violet de Merville the truth about the man she loves: her father, a famous detective, and the man's own ex-mistress standing in front of her. None of it matters. The text does not treat this as stupidity. Holmes sees in her "the ethereal other-world beauty of some fanatic," and the Baron's talk of "post-hypnotic suggestion" is half a boast and half an accurate description of how a clever person pre-empts criticism by predicting it. The hard thing the story leaves you with is that the only thing that worked was a theft, aided by an act of mutilation nobody planned, and that the woman at the centre of it is never heard from again. Whether she was saved or merely overruled is a question Watson does not ask.

The other difficulty is Kitty Winter. The story needs her hatred: it supplies the clue, the testimony, and the punishment. It also shows what that hatred has cost her and does not pretend her act was good. Holmes says "the wages of sin" and means Gruner, but the sentence hangs over Kitty too, and over Holmes himself, who ends the story an unprosecuted burglar because of who his client was. The tale is comfortable with this in a way a careful reader may not be.

## Lines worth keeping
- "To revenge crime is important, but to prevent it is more so."
- "A complex mind... All great criminals have that."
- "Woman's heart and mind are insoluble puzzles to the male. Murder might be condoned or explained, and yet some smaller offence might rankle."
- "Some people's affability is more deadly than the violence of coarser souls."
- "Hell, London, gets me every time."
- "what I am Adelbert Gruner made me."
- "this man collects women, and takes a pride in his collection, as some men collect moths or butterflies."
- "You may have noticed how extremes call to each other, the spiritual to the animal, the cave-man to the angel."
- "I am not often eloquent. I use my head, not my heart."
- "I was nearer him than anyone else, and yet I was always conscious of the gap between."
- "If a man has a hobby he follows it up, whatever his other pursuits may be."
- "when an object is good and a client is sufficiently illustrious, even the rigid British law becomes human and elastic."

## Preoccupations
- love that will not hear evidence
- charm as a disguise for cruelty
- justice outside the law, and the law bending for rank
- the victim who becomes an avenger
- collecting and possession (objects, women)
- secrecy between friends
