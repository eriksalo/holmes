# The Adventure of the Devil's Foot

## What happens
Ordered to rest or break down, Holmes is in a Cornish cottage with Watson when the vicar and his lodger Mortimer Tregennis bring news that Tregennis's sister Brenda sat dead at the card table overnight and his two brothers beside her have gone mad, all three faces fixed in terror, nothing stolen, no one in the house. Tregennis calls it devilish; Holmes insists on exhausting natural explanations, takes a footprint by upsetting a watering-pot, and gets nowhere. The explorer Leon Sterndale, who had got as far as Plymouth on his way to Africa, turns back on a telegram and asks whom Holmes suspects. The next morning Tregennis himself is found dead in his rooms with the same face, a lamp burning in daylight. Holmes scrapes powder from the lamp's shield, tests it on himself and Watson—both are nearly destroyed by a flood of terror and Watson drags them out onto the grass—and then confronts Sterndale with a reconstruction: gravel at the window, a short interview, a cigar on the lawn while a man died. Sterndale produces a photograph of Brenda, whom he loved for years and could not marry, explains that Tregennis stole the "devil's-foot root" from his cabinet to seize the family money, and that he came "as judge and executioner." Holmes tells him to go and finish his work in Africa.

## What the work observes about being human
1. **People reach for the devil to explain what is really greed, and the one who cries "devilish" loudest may be the one who did it.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Mortimer Tregennis: "It's devilish, Mr. Holmes, devilish! It is not of this world... What human contrivance could do that?" Holmes: "if the matter is beyond humanity it is certainly beyond me. Yet we must exhaust all natural explanations before we fall back upon such a theory as this."
   - *What the action does with it:* The supernatural reading comes from the murderer, who also plants the "something moving among the bushes" to look like an outside visitation. The cause is a root, a fire, and a brother who wanted to be "sole guardian of their joint property." The vicar's "We are devil-ridden!" is sincere and useless. The story shows the appeal to the inexplicable working as a screen for the most ordinary motive there is.

2. **A reconciliation can be entirely hollow and look complete; the merriest table may seat a murderer.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Tregennis: "I won't deny that there was some feeling about the division of the money and it stood between us for a time, but it was all forgiven and forgotten, and we were the best of friends together." Holmes later: "How bitter that quarrel may have been, or how hollow the reconciliation we cannot tell."
   - *What the action does with it:* He left them "as merry as could be" after a hand of whist and threw the poison in the fire on his way out. The housekeeper had "never known them more cheerful and prosperous." The story's own opening describes Mounts Bay "inviting the storm-tossed craft to tack into it for rest and protection. Then come the sudden swirl round of the wind"—shelter that is a trap—and the family evening repeats the pattern exactly.

3. **Fear is a faculty of the mind, and what it shows is not outside us; the only thing that broke it was concern for another person.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Watson under the poison: "my mind told me that in this cloud, unseen as yet, but about to spring out upon my appalled senses, lurked all that was vaguely horrible, all that was monstrous and inconceivably wicked in the universe." Sterndale on the root: "it stimulates those brain centres which control the emotion of fear."
   - *What the action does with it:* Nothing is in the room. Brenda dies "of fright" at a vision her own brain supplied; two strong men are emptied of reason by it. What saves Watson is not courage but the sight of "Holmes's face, white, rigid, and drawn with horror... It was that vision which gave me an instant of sanity and of strength." He moves to rescue his friend and so rescues himself. The story places the terror in the machinery of the brain and the exit in attention to someone else.

4. **Love forbidden by law can make a whole hidden life; what looks like misanthropy may be devotion.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Sterndale: "For years I have loved her. For years she has loved me. There is the secret of that Cornish seclusion which people have marvelled at... I could not marry her, for I have a wife who has left me for years and yet whom, by the deplorable laws of England, I could not divorce. For years Brenda waited. For years I waited. And this is what we have waited for."
   - *What the action does with it:* Watson introduces him as a man of "absolutely lonely life... paying little apparent heed to the affairs of his neighbours." Every word of that is wrong; the hermit's bungalow was the nearest he could live to the woman he loved. The waiting ends not in marriage but in her death at a card table. The story gives the law no defender: Sterndale's "deplorable" is the only judgment on it offered.

5. **When the law cannot reach a crime, a man may make himself judge and executioner, and the story's chief voice of reason lets him.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Sterndale: "Should I appeal to the law? Where were my proofs?... I have come at last to be a law to myself... I told him that I had come both as judge and executioner." Holmes: "Go and do the other half. I, at least, am not prepared to prevent you... Our investigation has been independent, and our action shall be so also."
   - *What the action does with it:* The vengeance is exact—"he endured nothing which my innocent darling had not felt before him"—and Holmes and Watson both decline to denounce it. But the story leaves something unspoken: it was Sterndale who showed Tregennis the powder, explained its effects, and boasted "how powerless European science would be to detect it." The avenger armed the murderer by vanity, and no one, including Holmes, says so. The private justice is approved; its roots are not examined.

6. **A person can judge what he would do for love without having felt it, and that judgment decides another man's fate.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Sterndale: "Perhaps, if you loved a woman, you would have done as much yourself." Holmes: "I have never loved, Watson, but if I did and if the woman I loved had met such an end, I might act even as our lawless lion-hunter has done. Who knows?"
   - *What the action does with it:* Holmes's verdict rests on an act of imagination about a feeling he disclaims. Yet the story shows him capable of it in another form: his apology after the experiment—"doubly so for a friend"—and Watson's "I have never seen so much of Holmes's heart before." The man who says he has never loved releases a killer out of fellow-feeling he says he does not possess.

7. **A mind built for work is injured by rest and by work alike; it cannot be kept safe.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Watson: "The state of his health was not a matter in which he himself took the faintest interest." Holmes: "To let the brain work without sufficient material is like racing an engine. It racks itself to pieces."
   - *What the action does with it:* The rest cure lasts until the first knock on the door, "to my sorrow and to his unfeigned delight." The same man who knows idle thought racks the brain then burns an untested poison in a closed room with his friend opposite, and must be hauled out by him. "A candid observer would certainly declare that we were so already [mad]," he jokes. The story shows the restlessness as neither cured nor curable, and Watson as the one who pays for it.

8. **Grief can be performed convincingly by the one who caused it.**
   - *Where it is spoken:* Tregennis: "It is a bad thing to speak of, but I will answer you the truth"; "I'll never get the sight of that room out of my mind so long as I live."
   - *What the action does with it:* Watson records his "pale, drawn face," his "thin hands clasped convulsively," his eyes that "seemed to reflect something of the horror of the scene"—and the reader is meant to take it as feeling. It was. The horror he reflected was the horror he had made, and he had looked at it in the morning on purpose. Holmes afterwards reads guilt off his "foxy face," but only after the lamp and the gravel have done the work; the performance held as long as it needed to.

## What it teaches
This story is about the inside of fear and the inside of love, and it finds that both are capable of killing. The poison does not bring anything into the room; it opens a door already in the mind, and what comes through is "all that was monstrous and inconceivably wicked in the universe," which is to say the brain's own stock. Watson's account of it is the most interior writing in the story, and what pulls him out is the sight of a friend in danger. That is the only antidote the text offers, and it is offered quietly: you survive your own terror by turning to someone else's.

The harder matter is the ending. Two men love Brenda Tregennis in the story—Sterndale openly, Holmes by proxy, through the imagination of a love he has never had—and between them they decide that her brother's killer should go free to Africa. The reader is invited to agree, and probably does. But the text has also told us, without comment, that Sterndale handed the murderer the weapon and the assurance it could not be traced; that his retribution was a copy of the crime in every particular; and that the wronged man's heart "was flint." Holmes's "Who knows?" is honest. It admits that the standard being applied is not a principle but a sympathy, and that sympathy is a thing none of us can be sure we would apply the same way twice.

## Preoccupations
- fear as something made inside the mind
- justice outside the law, approved by the detective
- love that the law forbids and the hidden lives it makes
- the gap between how people look (hermit, grieving brother) and what they are
- greed inside the family
- the restless mind that cannot rest or be kept safe
- friendship as the thing that pulls a person back
