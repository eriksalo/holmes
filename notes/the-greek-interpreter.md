# The Greek Interpreter

## What happens
Watson, who has come to think of Holmes as "a brain without a heart," is startled when a conversation about heredity leads Holmes to mention a brother, Mycroft, who has keener powers of observation but "no ambition and no energy," and who lives between Whitehall and the Diogenes Club, a club for men who do not wish to speak to one another. In the club's window the brothers read a passing widower's whole life off his boots and parcels; then Mycroft produces a problem he has not had the energy to pursue. Mr. Melas, a Greek interpreter, was taken by a young man named Latimer in a carriage with papered windows to an unknown house, where a giggling, twitching older man made him interrogate a starving prisoner whose face was criss-crossed with sticking-plaster. The prisoner, writing on a slate, refused to sign papers; Melas smuggled questions of his own into the interrogation and learned his name, Kratides, and his origin, Athens, before a woman entered, cried "Oh, my God, it is Paul!" and was pushed from the room. Melas was paid five sovereigns, threatened with death if he spoke, and dumped on Wandsworth Common. Mycroft advertised; Holmes warns Melas he is now in danger; an answer to the advertisement names the house as The Myrtles, Beckenham, but by the time Holmes, Watson, Mycroft and Inspector Gregson have complied with "the legal formalities" and reached it, Melas has been kidnapped again. They break in to find the house abandoned and two men bound in a room full of charcoal fumes: Melas, whom Watson revives, and Kratides, who is dead. The villains and the woman have fled; the explanation is that Latimer had gained "an ascendancy" over the Greek heiress Sophy Kratides, her friends had "washed their hands of the matter," and her brother came to England and was starved to make him sign away their property. Months later a cutting from Buda-Pesth reports that two Englishmen travelling with a woman have been found stabbed; the Hungarian police think they quarrelled, but Holmes believes the girl avenged herself and her brother.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **A man can seem to be a brain without a heart, and the seeming is the observer's failure as much as the man's.**
   - Where it is spoken: Watson: "This reticence upon his part had increased the somewhat inhuman effect which he produced upon me, until sometimes I found myself regarding him as an isolated phenomenon, a brain without a heart, as deficient in human sympathy as he was pre-eminent in intelligence."
   - What the action does with it: Within a page the "isolated phenomenon" has a brother, a grandmother who was a painter's sister, a family of country squires. When Mycroft proposes driving to Brixton for "the sister's story," Sherlock answers "the brother's life is more valuable than the sister's story... We know that a man is being done to death, and every hour may be vital," and it is Sherlock who runs into the charcoal with his hand to his throat and hurls the brazier through the window. Watson's judgment, stated as the opening premise, is refuted by the story's events; the story also gives the reader, in Mycroft, a picture of what a mind without the will to act really looks like, and it is not Sherlock.

2. **The same gift in two people makes two different lives; what differs is not intelligence but the energy to use it.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "If the art of the detective began and ended in reasoning from an armchair, my brother would be the greatest criminal agent that ever lived. But he has no ambition and no energy. He will not even go out of his way to verify his own solutions, and would rather be considered wrong than take the trouble to prove himself right." Mycroft: "I really had not the energy to follow it up save in a very incomplete fashion, but it gave me a basis for some pleasing speculation." And: "Sherlock has all the energy of the family."
   - What the action does with it: Mycroft's "pleasing speculation" is a man being starved to death a few miles away. He placed advertisements and went back to the club. The story confirms Holmes's description, then complicates it: "somehow this case attracts me," says Mycroft, arriving in Baker Street by hansom, and he follows the others up the stairs at Beckenham "as quickly as his great bulk would permit." Even the man with no energy can be moved; the question the story leaves is why it took a second kidnapping to move him.

3. **Modesty is not a virtue to a logician; seeing oneself exactly is.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "I cannot agree with those who rank modesty among the virtues. To the logician all things should be seen exactly as they are, and to underestimate one's self is as much a departure from truth as to exaggerate one's own powers."
   - What the action does with it: Holmes applies this to rating his brother above himself, and the window scene bears him out: Mycroft caps every deduction ("Children, my dear boy, children"). But the story then shows that accurate self-knowledge and accurate observation are not the whole of worth. The brother who sees most does least. Truth about one's powers is only a virtue if the powers are then used; the story quietly separates seeing from doing.

4. **People who want no company still want to watch other people.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "There are many men in London, you know, who, some from shyness, some from misanthropy, have no wish for the company of their fellows. Yet they are not averse to comfortable chairs and the latest periodicals." Mycroft: "To any one who wishes to study mankind this is the spot. Look at the magnificent types!"
   - What the action does with it: From behind glass, in a room where speaking is punishable by expulsion, the brothers read a widower's grief off his mourning, his rattle and his picture-book — "The wife probably died in childbed" — as a demonstration. Then a man comes into the Stranger's Room, the one room where talk is allowed, "in a wailing voice," and the study of mankind becomes a kidnapping, a starved prisoner, and a sister's scream. The story moves from a club of men who will not speak to a house where a man's mouth is taped shut; detachment is where it starts and suffocation is where it ends.

5. **A man of no physical courage can be the only one in the room with any ingenuity, and still be nearly killed for it.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "He is a man of no physical courage, as they are well aware from their experience the other night. This villain was able to terrorise him the instant that he got into his presence." Melas: "But soon a happy thought came to me. I took to adding on little sentences of my own to each question... I played a more dangerous game." And: "I know that there is foul play going on, and I want to help that unhappy man if I can."
   - What the action does with it: Holmes's judgment is correct — Melas cannot speak of Kemp "save with trembling hands and a blanched cheek," and he submits twice. But everything the rescuers know comes from his slate-and-question trick, performed "under their very noses." The story distinguishes bodily cowardice from moral courage and gives the second to the man who lacks the first. His reward is a blow from a stick and a room full of charcoal. Courage of this kind is not protected by anyone; Holmes's warning ("I should certainly be on my guard") is all the protection it gets.

6. **Refusal is the one power left to the powerless, and it can cost everything.**
   - Where it is spoken: Melas describes Kratides as "a man whose spirit was greater than his strength." On the slate: "'Never!'", "'I care nothing for myself.'", "'It shall not go to villains.'", "'Then I shall never see her.'"
   - What the action does with it: Kratides is starved, taped, interrogated twice, and dies without signing. His refusal saves the property; it does not save him, and it does not free his sister, who leaves with the men who killed him. Watson's "for him at least our aid had come too late" is the story's only epitaph. The text honours the refusal without pretending it won anything a living man could use.

7. **Control over who may speak to whom is a form of imprisonment, and recognition breaks it in a single word.**
   - Where it is spoken: Watson's explanation: "finding that through his ignorance of the language he was helpless in their hands, [they] had kept him a prisoner... the plaster over the face had been for the purpose of making recognition difficult in case she should ever catch a glimpse of him. Her feminine perception, however, had instantly seen through the disguise."
   - What the action does with it: The whole crime depends on language as a wall: a Greek who cannot speak English, a hired interpreter told to "say no more than you are told to say," a mouth sealed with plaster. Sophy's "Oh, my God, it is Paul!" ends the scheme in an instant. Watson files this under "feminine perception"; what the text shows is simpler — a sister knows her brother through a disguise that was designed by men who did not think she would. The story's villains are undone by underestimating ordinary recognition.

8. **Respectable people wash their hands, institutions are slow, and the price is paid by someone else.**
   - Where it is spoken: Melas: "I do not believe that the police credit me—on my word, I do not." Watson: "Her friends, shocked at the event, had contented themselves with informing her brother at Athens, and had then washed their hands of the matter." And: "it was more than an hour before we could get Inspector Gregson and comply with the legal formalities which would enable us to enter the house."
   - What the action does with it: The friends who disapproved of the elopement did nothing but send a letter; the brother who answered it died. The police did not believe the interpreter; the interpreter was nearly killed. Holmes says "every hour may be vital" and then loses more than an hour at Scotland Yard for a warrant, and arrives to a corpse that "had ceased to moan as we laid him down." The story does not argue against legal formalities; it simply shows what they cost on this particular night.

9. **The law never touches the guilty; a private vengeance may, and the story will only guess at it.**
   - Where it is spoken: Watson: "the Hungarian police were of opinion that they had quarreled and had inflicted mortal injuries upon each other. Holmes, however, is, I fancy, of a different way of thinking, and holds to this day that, if one could find the Grecian girl, one might learn how the wrongs of herself and her brother came to be avenged."
   - What the action does with it: Latimer and Kemp leave England untouched. Their end comes in a newspaper cutting, with an official explanation and Holmes's unofficial one. The woman over whom Latimer had "acquired an ascendancy," who was "herself a prisoner," is the person Holmes credits with the knife, and she is never heard from. The story titles itself "still involved in some mystery" and means it: justice, if it happened, happened off the page, by a hand the story cannot show.

## What it teaches
The story's quiet argument is that intelligence and feeling are not opposites, but that neither of them is action. Watson begins by calling Holmes a brain without a heart; the story answers by introducing Mycroft, who has the bigger brain and sits in a chair, and by showing Sherlock choosing a stranger's life over a good story and running into poisoned air. What separates the brothers is not sympathy — Mycroft is kind enough to Melas — but the willingness to be put to trouble. A reader is left asking how much of what we call coldness in people is really inertia, and how much of what we call warmth is simply the habit of getting up.

The harder lesson is about who pays. Kratides refuses and dies; Melas plays a dangerous game and is beaten and gassed; Sophy's friends send a letter and go on with their lives; Scotland Yard takes an hour. The courageous in this story are the weak, and the strong are either absent or late. The ending refuses to tidy this. Two villains are dead in Budapest, perhaps by the hand of the woman they used, perhaps not, and Holmes's opinion is offered as a guess the narrator does not endorse. The story asks you to accept that the wrong was real, that the rescue half-failed, and that the reckoning, if there was one, belongs to a woman who is given one line in the whole account.

## Lines worth keeping
- "a brain without a heart, as deficient in human sympathy as he was pre-eminent in intelligence."
- "Art in the blood is liable to take the strangest forms."
- "to underestimate one's self is as much a departure from truth as to exaggerate one's own powers."
- "He will not even go out of his way to verify his own solutions, and would rather be considered wrong than take the trouble to prove himself right."
- "It is for the convenience of these that the Diogenes Club was started, and it now contains the most unsociable and unclubable men in town."
- "To any one who wishes to study mankind this is the spot."
- "I really had not the energy to follow it up save in a very incomplete fashion, but it gave me a basis for some pleasing speculation."
- "the protruding, brilliant eyes of a man whose spirit was greater than his strength"
- "'I care nothing for myself.'"
- "the brother's life is more valuable than the sister's story."
- "Her friends, shocked at the event, had contented themselves with informing her brother at Athens, and had then washed their hands of the matter."
- "for him at least our aid had come too late."
- "that dark valley in which all paths meet"

## Preoccupations
- intelligence without the will to act
- the gap between watching people and helping them
- courage of the mind in a body that is afraid
- language and silence as instruments of power
- respectable people washing their hands, and institutions arriving late
- vengeance outside the law, left as a guess
- the woman at the centre of the plot who is never given a voice
