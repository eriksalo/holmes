# The Adventure of the Three Garridebs

## What happens
A chubby American "Counsellor at Law" named John Garrideb tells Holmes a tale: a Kansas millionaire of the same rare name has left fifteen million dollars to be split among three adult male Garridebs, and only a third is wanted. Holmes sees at once that the man is lying (English clothes, no agony-column advertisements, an invented mutual acquaintance) and goes to meet the second Garrideb, Nathan, an elderly recluse who never leaves the museum-like room where he has lived for five years, dreaming of becoming "the Hans Sloane of my age." The American produces a planted advertisement for a third Garrideb in Birmingham and sends Nathan off by train. Holmes identifies the American as "Killer" Evans, who once shot a counterfeiter named Prescott, Nathan's predecessor in the room. Holmes and Watson wait behind a cupboard; Evans opens a trapdoor to Prescott's hidden press and two thousand forged notes, then shoots Watson in the thigh when discovered. Holmes clubs him down and, for an instant, lets his fear for Watson show. Nathan Garrideb, his dream collapsed, ends in a nursing home; Evans goes back to prison.

## What the work observes about being human

1. **A great hope offered to a lonely person will be believed because it is wanted, not because it is likely.**
   - Where it is spoken: Nathan: "It is certainly hard for me to refuse you anything, considering the glory of hope that you have brought into my life." And: "Just think what I could do with five million dollars... I shall be the Hans Sloane of my age."
   - What the action does with it: Nathan is "a crazy boob of a bug-hunter" to Evans and "our poor old friend" to Watson, and the story makes him both. He accepts a preposterous will from a stranger because it matches his one desire, and he is not greedy in any ordinary way: he wants the money to fill "gaps in my collection." When the lie dissolves, "his castle in the air fell down, it buried him beneath the ruins." The story's opening line calls this a comedy or a tragedy; for Nathan it is simply the tragedy, and the comedy is at his expense.

2. **A life that never leaves one room is still a life that can be invaded.**
   - Where it is spoken: Nathan: "My doctor lectures me about never going out, but why should I go out when I have so much to hold me here?" Holmes: "He wanted to get our amiable friend out of his room... as the collector never went out, it took some planning to do it."
   - What the action does with it: The whole elaborate fraud exists only because a sedentary man would not budge. His retreat from the world is both his protection (Evans considers "it would have been easy enough" to kill him, and does not) and the reason he is targeted. The story is dry about this: the man's "little interests in life" sit, unknowing, on top of a counterfeiter's cellar. Safety was an illusion from the day he moved in.

3. **A liar's story is undone by small, unforced details, not by its central claim.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "Here is a man with an English coat frayed at the elbow and trousers bagged at the knee with a year's wear, and yet by this document and by his own account he is a provincial American lately landed in London." And of the advertisement: "it was bad English but good American."
   - What the action does with it: Evans's fiction about the will is never directly disproven; it is irrelevant. What catches him is a coat, a misspelt "plow," the word "buckboards," and an eagerness to confirm a Dr. Lysander Starr who never existed. The story confirms Holmes's method, and also confirms Holmes's observation that Evans is "complex and ingenious": the scheme itself was good; its surface was not.

4. **The hard man thinks of himself as soft, and of his crime as a service.**
   - Where it is spoken: Evans: "I'm a soft-hearted guy that can't begin shooting unless the other man has a gun also." And: "Five years--when I should have had a medal the size of a soup plate... if I hadn't put him out he would have flooded London with them."
   - What the action does with it: Evans has shot three men in the States and one in London, and has just fired two shots at Watson, yet his self-portrait is of a reasonable fellow unjustly treated. The story half-agrees with him: Scotland Yard "would willingly have subscribed to that soup-plate medal," because the counterfeiter "stands in a class by himself as a public danger." Then "an unappreciative Bench" sends him down anyway. The killer's self-justification is not simply false; it is just not the whole account.

5. **A cold man's love shows only when he thinks he has lost what he loves.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "You're not hurt, Watson? For God's sake, say that you are not hurt!" Watson: "It was worth a wound--it was worth many wounds--to know the depth of loyalty and love which lay behind that cold mask... For the one and only time I caught a glimpse of a great heart as well as of a great brain."
   - What the action does with it: This is Watson's reading, and the story gives him grounds: "The clear, hard eyes were dimmed for a moment, and the firm lips were shaking." But the mask closes at once ("His face set like flint"), and the next words are a threat: "If you had killed Watson, you would not have got out of this room alive." The love is real and it is expressed as violence toward a third party. Watson treats this as the culmination of "years of humble but single-minded service," which tells you as much about Watson as about Holmes.

6. **The pursuit of justice uses the innocent as bait and does not always put them back.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "I might have told him that he was clearly going on a wild-goose chase, but, on second thoughts, it seemed better to clear the stage by letting him go."
   - What the action does with it: Holmes knowingly lets Nathan make a hundred-mile journey on a lie so the trap can be sprung, and never tells him the truth before the collapse. The result is a man in a nursing home. Holmes recovers a counterfeiter's press and the police sleep better; the client who hired him is wrecked, and the story reports this in a single sentence without comment. The detective solved the case and did not protect the one man in it who had done nothing.

7. **Names and chance can do what cunning cannot.**
   - Where it is spoken: Holmes: "there is a certain devilish ingenuity about it, even if the queer name of the tenant did give him an opening which he could hardly have expected." Evans on the dead millionaire: "he took a kind of pride in the queerness of his name."
   - What the action does with it: The whole plot hangs on a coincidence of spelling. Evans builds a fiction around the name he finds; Nathan's identity, "his real name, anyhow," is the one true thing in the fraud and the thing that makes it possible. The story treats this lightly, but its implication is not light: a person can be ruined because of a label he never chose.

## What it teaches
The first sentence tells you what to watch for: "It may have been a comedy, or it may have been a tragedy." The story then shows you that the same events are one or the other depending on where you stand. For Holmes and Watson it is a successful trap, a scratch, and a moment of revealed feeling that Watson will treasure for life. For Scotland Yard it is a recovered press. For Evans it is a return to the shades. For Nathan Garrideb, who did nothing but believe a stranger and want to complete his collection, it is the end of his mind. Watson gives him one sentence. The lesson is uncomfortable: the people who are the occasion of a story are not always the people the story is about, and the one with the most to lose can be the one nobody is working for.

The other thing to carry away is the shape of Holmes's affection. It appears once, in extremity, and is immediately converted into a threat to kill. Watson calls it "a great heart." A reader can grant him that and still notice that the heart speaks only in the language of harm, and only when the mask slips. Whether that is the price of being what Holmes is, or a limitation the stories never challenge, the text does not say.

## Lines worth keeping
- "It may have been a comedy, or it may have been a tragedy. It cost one man his reason, it cost me a blood-letting, and it cost yet another man the penalties of the law."
- "in all our explorations of human complexities we have ever come upon anything more singular."
- "why should I go out when I have so much to hold me here?"
- "I shall be the Hans Sloane of my age."
- "Touch him where you would he was false."
- "the glory of hope that you have brought into my life."
- "it was bad English but good American."
- "we can but possess our souls in patience and see what the hour may bring."
- "You're not hurt, Watson? For God's sake, say that you are not hurt!"
- "For the one and only time I caught a glimpse of a great heart as well as of a great brain."
- "I'm a soft-hearted guy that can't begin shooting unless the other man has a gun also."
- "When his castle in the air fell down, it buried him beneath the ruins."

## Preoccupations
- hope exploited in the lonely
- the recluse and the world that reaches in anyway
- lies betrayed by small details
- the criminal's self-justification
- love shown only through fear of loss
- the innocent used as bait and left to pay
